## ARTICLE 301-redirects

### 301 Redirects Explained: How They Impact SEO

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
A 301 redirect permanently moves a URL, and — since 2016 — passes full PageRank (no more ~15% "leak"). That makes redirects both a hygiene task (fix chains, loops, broken redirects, sitemap pollution) and a growth lever: merge similar underperforming pages ("cocktail technique") or acquired sites ("merger method") to consolidate authority into one stronger page. Irrelevant redirects are treated as soft 404s and pass nothing.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Implementation:** server config rules for single pages, domain-to-domain, www/non-www, HTTP→HTTPS, or combined cases (order of rules matters — wrong order creates chains); CMS redirect managers make it point-and-click. Redirects must target the FINAL destination.

**Audit — 9 redirect issues to fix:**
1. **HTTP→HTTPS redirect in place sitewide** (including subdomains; never HTTPS→HTTP).
2. **No 3XX URLs in sitemaps** — redirected pages don't exist; purge them from XML sitemaps (wastes crawl budget).
3. **Redirect chains** — collapse to a single hop to the final URL; better: update internal links to point directly at the destination.
4. **Redirect loops** — infinite redirects; fix the target or restore 200 status.
5. **Broken redirects** — 301 pointing to a 404/5XX; reinstate the page or fix the target.
6. **404s with backlinks** — redirect to a RELEVANT page (irrelevant = soft 404, no value); if no relevant target but strong links, consider republishing the content.
7. **302/meta-refresh used for permanent moves** — replace with 301 (or remove if genuinely temporary).
8. **301 pages still earning organic traffic** — engine hasn't processed the redirect; request indexing and clean the sitemap.
9. **Bad external 301s** — your outbound links redirected to unrelated content (expired domains, merged sites); remove those links.

**Growth play 1 — Cocktail technique (merge pages):**
1. Find cannibalizing pages (multiple URLs ranking for the same keywords) with backlinks.
2. Verify the opportunity: combined referring domains vs the incumbent's links (e.g., two pages with 467 RDs vs a #1 page with 192).
3. Merge the best of both into one page (check the backlinks' anchor text to preserve what people linked for; match SERP intent/format).
4. Republish at the better URL (or a new one) and 301 the rest.

**Growth play 2 — Merger method (buy & merge sites):**
Warning: only for genuine business acquisitions. Buying expired or lapsed domains just to redirect their old signals to your site violates spam policy.
1. Re-home + redirect pages with traffic, relevance, and quality (update stale content after the move).
2. Delete + redirect low-potential or duplicate-topic pages to your relevant existing page.
3. Redirect leftovers to the homepage as last resort — better than a guaranteed soft 404, but never redirect pages with toxic backlink profiles (404 those instead).

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Verify HTTP→HTTPS 301s sitewide; check for reverse redirects
- [ ] Crawl for chains, loops, broken redirects; fix to single-hop final destinations
- [ ] Replace internal links to redirected URLs with direct final-URL links
- [ ] Purge 3XX URLs from sitemaps; add final URLs
- [ ] 301 any 404 with dofollow backlinks to a relevant page
- [ ] Convert long-standing 302/meta refreshes to 301s
- [ ] Periodically audit external redirects behind your outbound links
- [ ] Mine cannibalizing pages with links for merge opportunities
- [ ] On acquisitions: page-level redirect map (re-home → redirect-to-related → homepage last resort)

## Data points & benchmarks
- Pre-2016 assumption: ~15% PageRank loss per redirect hop; a 3-hop chain ≈ 38% loss. Google stated in 2016 that 301s no longer dilute PageRank.
- Merging two aging posts into one (one large SEO blog) produced a clear traffic step-change post-redirect.
- Merger method (one reported case, not a benchmark): ~116% organic traffic increase in 12 months after buying and redirecting a relevant site page-by-page.
- Example opportunity: two same-topic pages with 467 combined referring domains could out-consolidate a #1 result with 192.
- Googlebot follows a limited number of hops per chain (guidance: keep it under 5); the goal is a single hop.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Redirecting everything to the homepage and calling it a merger — page-level mapping is required.
- Redirecting unrelated pages (soft 404 = zero equity transfer).
- Redirecting pages with spammy backlink profiles — let them 404 or disavow first.
- Leaving redirected URLs in the sitemap or internal-linked (chains for crawlers and users).
- Wrong rule order in server config creating accidental chains.

## Key principles
- Since 2016, 301s pass 100% of link equity — consolidation is free.
- Relevance is the gate: engines ignore redirects between dissimilar pages.
- Redirects are a traffic strategy, not just plumbing: merges and acquisitions are authority consolidation plays.

## ARTICLE 301-vs-302-redirects

### 301 vs. 302 Redirects for SEO: Which Should You Use?

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
301 = permanent move: the new URL gets indexed and link signals consolidate FORWARD. 302 = temporary move: the old URL stays indexed and signals consolidate BACKWARD. Use the type that matches reality — but if you slip, search engines often figure out your intent eventually (a long-standing 302 gets treated as a 301, sometimes immediately for established destinations).

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Use 301 when permanently:** changing a URL, migrating domains, moving HTTP→HTTPS, fixing www/non-www duplicates, merging pages, restructuring URLs.

**Use 302 when temporarily:** geo/language steering, A/B testing a page, gathering feedback on a new page without risking the old one's rankings, seasonal/promo redirects to a sales page.

**How search engines respond:**
- 301 → index the new URL; backlinks to the old URL effectively count for the new one. Exception: irrelevant redirects (e.g., blog post → homepage) are treated as soft 404s and pass nothing.
- 302 → keep the old URL indexed, signals consolidate backward. Exception: if the engine believes you meant permanent, it flips to 301 behavior — timing varies from days to months, or immediately when the destination is established.

**Auditing accidental 302s:**
1. Check individual URLs: URL Inspection tool — "not on Google" = treated as permanent; still indexed = temporary.
2. At scale: find 302 redirects that still earn organic traffic (a telltale sign they're treated as temporary); swap those to 301s. Verify canonicals match after recrawl.

**Implementation:** server config (one line per URL; regex rules for whole-site moves) or CMS redirect managers. For whole-domain moves, registrar/DNS-level forwarding beats keeping old hosting alive just to serve .htaccess (confirm it sends 301s, not 302s).

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Map every redirect to permanent or temporary intent before choosing a status code
- [ ] Permanent moves: 301 (domain, HTTPS, www, merges, restructures)
- [ ] Temporary moves: 302 (tests, promos, geo-steering)
- [ ] Audit for accidental 302s on permanent moves — prioritize ones with organic traffic
- [ ] Verify relevance of redirect targets (irrelevant = soft 404 = no signal transfer)
- [ ] Confirm consolidation in a backlink tool (old referring domains credited to new URL)
- [ ] For domain moves, use registrar/DNS forwarding instead of paying for old hosting

## Data points & benchmarks
- 302→301 reinterpretation timing: usually weeks to months; can be immediate for redirects to established sites (one reported 302 site-to-site redirect flipped the indexed domain right away).
- Redirects are a canonicalization signal, not a directive — rare inversions happen both ways.
- Organic traffic landing on a 302-redirected URL = engine still treats it as temporary.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Using 302 for domain migrations or HTTPS moves (almost always permanent).
- Redirecting dissimilar pages and expecting link equity to transfer (soft 404 treatment).
- Fixing "accidental" 302s the engine has already reinterpreted — changing the code then changes nothing.
- Keeping old hosting alive solely to serve redirect rules — use DNS forwarding.

## Key principles
- Match the status code to the move's permanence — that's the whole rule.
- Signals follow the indexed URL: forward for 301, backward for 302.
- Relevance gates equity transfer; the engine must believe the destination is the same content.

## ARTICLE accessibility-seo

### Accessibility for SEOs: How to Comply With ADA & WCAG

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Accessibility isn't a direct ranking factor, but it overlaps heavily with technical SEO (navigation, sitemaps, headings, alt text, anchor text) and is a legal requirement — ADA lawsuits have risen sharply in recent years and many countries mandate web accessibility. Target WCAG Level AA: it's the level courts and settlements reference, and most fixes double as SEO improvements.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**The legal landscape:**
- ADA (US civil rights law) applies to B2C and government/funded sites; inaccessible features = discrimination. Big brands (pizza chains, sportswear, media, universities) have been sued.
- WCAG conformance levels: A (minimum: keyboard nav, no keyboard traps, text alternatives, captions, color not sole information carrier), AA (the legal benchmark: 4.5:1 contrast, meaningful alt text, consistent navigation, labeled form fields, logical headings), AAA (highest; not fully achievable — 7:1 contrast, sign language, no timed activities).

**Where accessibility meets SEO:**
1. **Navigation** — simple, consistent navigation (AA requirement) aids disabled users AND crawler understanding; add "skip to content" links; keep menus in the same position site-wide; use breadcrumb trails (labeled as navigation landmarks; also shown in SERPs).
2. **Sitemaps** — an HTML sitemap gives users an overview and an alternative to complex navigation; XML sitemaps serve crawlers.
3. **Titles & headings** — screen readers announce title tags on load; use real heading tags in logical H1→H2→H3 order (not styled text).
4. **Internal links** — descriptive anchor text tells users what to expect before clicking and tells search engines what the target is about.
5. **Alt text** — screen readers rely on it; search engines use it for image ranking and image search traffic.

**Testing:** browser dev-tools accessibility audits (score /100 with issue lists), dedicated accessibility checkers by country, and page-annotation evaluation tools that highlight failures (e.g., exactly which images lack alt text) on-page. CMS accessibility extensions can fix common theme issues without code.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Run an automated accessibility audit (browser audit + dedicated checker); forward critical issues to devs
- [ ] Verify keyboard navigation works with no traps; add skip-to-content links
- [ ] Ensure color contrast ≥4.5:1 (3:1 for large text)
- [ ] Add meaningful alt text to all informative images
- [ ] Caption videos
- [ ] Make navigation consistent across pages (same menu positions)
- [ ] Label all form fields clearly
- [ ] Fix heading hierarchy: one H1, cascading H2/H3
- [ ] Use descriptive anchor text (never "click here")
- [ ] Offer an HTML sitemap as a navigation fallback
- [ ] Re-audit after each template redesign

## Data points & benchmarks
- 61M+ adults in the US have a disability (older estimate) — a large share of any audience.
- ~800 federal web-accessibility complaints in 2017 → 2,200+ in 2018 after WCAG 2.1 released (older figures); counts have kept rising.
- Roughly 40 nations required web accessibility in older counts; the European Accessibility Act has applied to many digital products and services since June 2025.
- The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities recognizes access to information and communications technology, including the web.
- One high-profile brand suit cited missing alt text, no keyboard access, and inaccessible dropdowns.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Treating accessibility as optional because it's "not a ranking factor" — hard-to-use sites shed users, referrals, and other signals over time, hurting visibility indirectly.
- Styling text as headings without semantic tags.
- Using color as the only indicator of state or action.
- Assuming an overlay widget alone equals compliance — audits and structural fixes still matter.
- Ignoring Level AA basics (contrast, labels, consistent nav) that lawsuits most commonly cite.

## Key principles
- Accessibility work is SEO work: the same elements serve screen readers and crawlers.
- Target WCAG AA as the compliance baseline; AAA as aspiration for key content.
- Legal risk compounds: each guideline revision (2.1, 2.2, and the 3.0 draft) triggers new lawsuit waves.

## ARTICLE canonical-tags

### Canonical Tags Explained: Why They Matter For SEO

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
rel=canonical tells Google which URL is the master version when duplicate/similar content exists at multiple URLs — preventing wrong versions getting indexed, consolidating link signals, and saving crawl budget. Use absolute URLs, one canonical per page, and audit for the common canonical issues in the checklist below.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**When to use canonicals:** trailing-slash variants, desktop/mobile duplicates, parameterized URLs (e.g., filter facets), syndicated/republished content.

**4 ways to declare canonicals:**
1. rel=canonical link tag in `<head>`
2. Redirects (for deprecated duplicates)
3. rel=canonical in HTTP header (for PDFs and non-HTML)
4. Sitemap (only list canonical URLs — weak signal)
If none used, Google chooses via hreflang, internal links, URL length.

**Golden rules:**
1. Absolute URLs only (`https://example.com/page/`, not `/page/`)
2. Correct (HTTPS) domain — never declare HTTP canonicals
3. Exactly ONE canonical per page — multiple = all ignored

**Implementation:** WordPress → any popular SEO plugin (advanced section); hosted site builders → built-in SEO settings; hosted ecommerce platforms → usually handled out of the box (template edit for custom cases); other site builders → page-level header injection.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Self-referencing canonical on the canonical version itself
- [ ] Canonicals use absolute HTTPS URLs
- [ ] Don't block canonicalized URLs in robots.txt (Google can't see the tag)
- [ ] Never mix noindex + canonical on the same page
- [ ] Canonicalized URLs return 200 (not 4xx/5xx/redirect)
- [ ] Don't canonicalize paginated pages to page 1 — each page self-canonical
- [ ] Hreflang annotations must point to canonical URLs
- [ ] Only canonical URLs in XML sitemap
- [ ] No canonical chains (A→B→C); point directly A→C
- [ ] og:url matches canonical
- [ ] Canonical tag in `<head>` only (body tags ignored; check rendered DOM — unclosed tags/iframes can break head parsing)
- [ ] Run a site audit crawler's canonical checks; verify Google's chosen canonical in Search Console URL Inspection

## Data points & benchmarks
- Google ignores canonicals when more than one conflicting canonical is declared on a page.
- Sitemap inclusion = weak canonicalization signal; canonical tag = strong signal; internal links and redirects are also signals.
- HTTPS has been a ranking factor since 2014.
- Canonicals pointing to 4xx/5xx/redirects get ignored → wrong version indexed.
- rel=prev/next no longer used by Google; Bing has said it may still use them as a hint (older statement).

## Mistakes to avoid
- Blocking canonicalized URLs in robots.txt — kills equity transfer.
- noindex + canonical on same page — contradictory; noindex may win and waste equity.
- Canonicalizing pagination to page 1.
- Multiple conflicting canonical tags from CMS + theme + plugins — all may be ignored; use overwrite options.
- Swapping HTML canonical with JavaScript — mixed signals.
- Canonical in `<body>` (ignored) — often caused by broken head parsing.

## Key principles
- Canonicals consolidate ranking signals (links) to one URL — "the master version."
- Google treats canonicals as hints, not directives — it can ignore them (check Search Console for mismatches).
- Syndicated content without canonicals risks the syndicator outranking you.
- Duplicate content wastes crawl budget even after Google learns — it must crawl first.

## ARTICLE canonicalization

### Search Engines Use ~40 Canonicalization Signals

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Canonicalization clusters duplicate URLs and picks one indexed version where all signals consolidate. It's decided by ~40 weighted signals — canonical tags, sitemap URLs, internal/external links, redirects, hreflang, PageRank, HTTPS preference, shorter-URL preference, first-seen source, and more — evaluated by machine learning. Your job is signal consistency: every signal should point at the same preferred URL.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**How it works:**
1. Duplicate detection runs multiple times (initial crawl, pattern detection, after rendering); only 200-status pages cluster.
2. All signals are weighed; the winner is indexed and receives consolidated signals (links, etc.). The choice can change over time.
3. Duplicate causes: http/https, www/non-www, trailing slash, case, index/default pages, mobile/AMP/print variants, same-language international pages, dev/staging mirrors, URL parameters, full-content feeds/category/paginated pages, scraped/syndicated copies, boilerplate-only translations, error pages.

**Key signal behaviors:**
- Canonical tag: strong hint, ignored when outweighed; must be in `<head>` (a prematurely-closed head — unclosed tags, iframes, injected JS — pushes it into the body where it's ignored); blank/unfilled-variable canonicals get ignored.
- HTTPS beats HTTP — unless invalid certificate, mixed-content links to HTTP, HTTPS→HTTP redirect, canonical pointing to HTTP, or insecure redirect loops.
- Shorter (clean, parameter-free) URL beats longer variant.
- Permanent redirects (301, 308, 0-delay meta/HTTP refresh, JS location, crypto redirects) push signals FORWARD; temporary ones (302, 303, 307 server-side, >0 delays) pull signals BACK — until enough accumulated signals flip the scale. Keep redirects in place at least a year (indefinitely while the old URL still earns links or traffic) so signals fully consolidate.
- Hreflang pages are more likely to be chosen canonical (x-default matters for selection); fully translated pages are NOT duplicates.
- Higher-PageRank duplicate tends to win.
- Sitemaps: list only canonical URLs — except post-migration, where temporarily listing old URLs speeds redirect processing.

**Checking:** the URL Inspection tool shows declared vs Google-selected canonical, and the Page indexing report flags "Duplicate, Google chose different canonical than user". Don't rely on site: searches for this.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Pick one preferred variant for scheme, host, slash, case; 301 everything else
- [ ] Self-canonicals sitewide; exactly one canonical tag per page (watch CMS + theme + plugin collisions)
- [ ] Keep canonical tags out of the body; audit for premature head-ending tags/iframes/scripts
- [ ] Align internal links, sitemap entries, and canonicals on the same URL
- [ ] Never pair noindex with canonical, never block canonicalized URLs in robots.txt (kills equity transfer), never 4XX them
- [ ] Paginated pages: self-canonicals, not canonical-to-page-1
- [ ] Use hreflang + same-language canonicals together
- [ ] Post-migration: temporary "old URLs" sitemap to accelerate redirect processing, then remove
- [ ] Verify chosen canonicals via the URL Inspection tool after changes

## Data points & benchmarks
- ~40 canonical selection signals (per a Google analyst).
- ~60% of the web is duplicate content (older Google estimate).
- Redirect signals can take up to a year to fully consolidate; keep redirects at least that long.
- No duplicate-content penalty exists — a version will rank; it just may not be yours (e.g., syndication hijacking).

## Mistakes to avoid
- Mixed signals (canonical says A, links say B, sitemap says C) — you surrender the choice to the engine.
- Using the URL removal tool for canonicalization — it can deindex ALL versions.
- Multiple canonical tags (CMS/theme/plugin each adding one) — all get ignored.
- Swapping an HTML canonical with JavaScript after initial response — mixed signals.
- Assuming hreflang fixes international duplication — swapping is best-effort and breaks; localize content properly.

## Key principles
- Canonicalization is a weighted election among ~40 signals — campaign for one candidate consistently.
- The canonical tag is a strong hint, not a directive; consensus beats any single signal.
- Signals evolve (e.g., HTTPS and shorter-URL preferences can conflict) — re-verify periodically.

## ARTICLE core-web-vitals

### What Are Core Web Vitals (CWVs) & How To Improve Them

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Core Web Vitals = LCP (visual load), CLS (visual stability), INP (responsiveness; replaced FID March 12, 2024), measured from real Chrome users (CrUX field data) at the 75th percentile. Targets: LCP ≤2.5s, INP ≤200ms, CLS ≤0.1. They're a ranking factor but a tiny one ("tiebreaker") — prioritize UX and conversions, not rankings.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**The 3 metrics & thresholds (Good / Needs improvement / Poor):**
- LCP: ≤2.5s / 2.5–4s / >4s — time to render the largest viewport element (usually hero image or H1; can be img, SVG image, video poster, CSS background, text block)
- INP: ≤200ms / 200–500ms / >500ms — latency across ALL click/tap/keyboard interactions
- CLS: ≤0.1 / 0.1–0.25 / >0.25 — layout shift, measured in the worst 5-second window

**Diagnosis workflow:**
1. Check Search Console Core Web Vitals report (page groups by template — fix template once, fixes all pages in group)
2. PageSpeed Insights for per-page field + lab data (Lighthouse); check LCP element under Diagnostics
3. Chrome DevTools Performance panel (screenshots → find LCP node); WebPageTest filmstrip with "Highlight Layout Shifts" for CLS
4. Lab data (repeatable conditions) for testing fixes; field data (CrUX, 28-day rolling average) is what Google uses

**Improvement playbooks:**
- LCP: prioritize resource loading, shrink files, CDN/closer servers, host resources same server, caching
- CLS: set dimensions on images/videos/iframes/ads, optimize font loading, avoid layout-triggering animations, enable bfcache
- INP: reduce JavaScript, defer JS, break up long tasks, web workers, SSR/prerendering

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Search Console → Core Web Vitals report → identify failing URL groups
- [ ] Identify LCP element per template (PSI Diagnostics / DevTools)
- [ ] Set width/height (or aspect-ratio) on all images, embeds, iframes, ad slots
- [ ] Preload hero image + fonts; use font-display swaps carefully
- [ ] Audit JS: remove unused, defer non-critical, split long tasks
- [ ] Verify with lab data (Lighthouse) since field data lags 28 days
- [ ] Track desktop AND mobile separately (mobile signals → mobile rankings)
- [ ] Don't over-invest: most pages pass responsiveness (INP) checks; focus on failing templates

## Data points & benchmarks
- LCP good ≤2.5s; INP good ≤200ms; CLS good ≤0.1.
- Field data assessed at 75th percentile — 70% good + 5% poor still = "needs improvement."
- In one older study, only 11.4% of 42M+ pages had CWV metrics associated with them (insufficient data → group/site-level signals used).
- Mobile page experience used for ranking since May 2021; desktop since Feb 2022.
- FID → INP switch: March 12, 2024.
- CrUX = 28-day rolling average; changes take weeks to show in field data.
- Google reps call CWV "tiny ranking factors" / tiebreakers (Gary Illyes, Pubcon 2023).
- AMP no longer required for Top Stories after CWV rollout.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Expecting rankings gains from CWV fixes — impact is minimal unless extremely slow.
- Chasing the Lighthouse score instead of the actual CWV field metrics.
- Testing only lab data and ignoring CrUX field data (or vice versa).
- Forgetting CLS causes: late-loading fonts, JS-injected content, dimensionless embeds.
- Ignoring that data lags 28 days — verify fixes with lab tests.
- Trying to fix CWV without developers — it's complex; work with them.

## Key principles
- CWV is a tiebreaker among 200+ ranking signals; the business case is UX/conversions.
- Fix templates, not individual pages (Search Console groups URLs).
- Field data (CrUX, 75th percentile) is the source of truth for ranking.
- Metrics and thresholds will keep evolving (FID→INP proves it); other metrics may be added.
- Platforms (CMSs, CDNs) are automating more optimizations over time.

## ARTICLE crawl-budget

### When Should You Worry About Crawl Budget?

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Crawl budget = crawl demand (how much Google wants to crawl) × crawl rate limit (how much your server can take). Most sites shouldn't worry about it. Worry when: new sites with many pages, millions of pages, frequently updated sites, or many "Discovered - currently not indexed" pages in Search Console. More crawling ≠ better rankings; uncrawled pages simply can't rank.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**When to worry:**
- New sites with lots of pages (Google doesn't yet trust/crawl much)
- Large sites (millions of URLs) or frequently updated sites
- Pages not crawled/updated as often as needed
- Many Search Console "Discovered - currently not indexed" pages (never crawled — possible crawl budget issue)

**What counts against crawl budget:** ALL URLs and requests — AMP/m-dot, hreflang variants, CSS, JS, XHR requests (not cached → heavy for JS-framework sites), images. Multiple Googlebots (desktop, mobile, image...) share one budget.

**How Google adjusts:**
- *Crawl demand* rises with page popularity (links/PageRank), change frequency, and how often Google serves the page. Staleness: unchanged pages get crawled on expanding intervals (1 day → 3 → 10 → 30 → 100...).
- *Crawl rate limit* rises if site health is good; drops with 5xx errors, 429s, or slow connect times.

**To crawl faster/smarter:**
1. Speed up server / fix 5xx and 429s (logs)
2. Add important pages to sitemaps
3. Eliminate duplicate/technical URL variants (HTTP/HTTPS, www/non-www, trailing slashes, case, index.* defaults, parameters)
4. Build more links — external AND internal (an internal-link opportunities report from any SEO tool helps)
5. Fix redirected internal links; remove 3XX URLs from sitemaps
6. Host resources on a different hostname/CDN
7. Use GET instead of POST (GET is cacheable)
8. Google Indexing API (job postings/live video only); IndexNow / Bing URL submission (any site) — all are hints, not commands
9. 304 Not Modified support (complex, rarely worth it; caching is better)

**To crawl slower:** the Search Console crawl-rate limiter was removed (January 2024); in an emergency, serve 503/429 briefly (risky — pages drop from the index after a few days).

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Search Console Crawl Stats: check total crawl requests trend, flagged crawl statuses, host status
- [ ] Check Search Console Page Indexing for "Discovered - currently not indexed" volume
- [ ] Consolidate duplicate variants (redirect HTTP→HTTPS, www choice, trailing slash, lowercase)
- [ ] Block faceted/parameter URLs in robots.txt or switch ? to # where safe
- [ ] Sitemap: only canonical, important, 200-status URLs
- [ ] Fix internal 3xx links (site crawler → internal URLs with 3xx status)
- [ ] Add internal links to under-crawled priority pages
- [ ] Check server logs for 5xx/429s and slow connect times
- [ ] Don't bother: crawl-delay (Googlebot ignores), removing 3rd-party scripts, noindex for budget, trivial page edits

## Data points & benchmarks
- ~60% of the internet is duplicate content (older Google estimate) — mostly technical duplicates wasting crawl budget.
- Duplicate variants list: HTTP/HTTPS × www/non-www × trailing slash × case × index.* × parameters.
- Staleness backoff: crawl intervals expand ~1d → 3d → 10d → 30d → 100d for unchanged pages.
- Google slows crawling on 5xx / 429 status codes and slow connect times.
- Serving 503/429 for more than a few days risks deindexing.
- Nofollow is a hint — Google may still crawl nofollow links.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Obsessing over crawl budget on small/healthy sites.
- Making trivial edits (dates, punctuation) to trigger recrawls — Google detects insignificant changes.
- Using noindex to save crawl budget — Google must still crawl to see it.
- crawl-delay in robots.txt — Googlebot ignores it (some other crawlers respect it).
- Removing third-party scripts for crawl budget — they don't count against it.
- Long-running 503/429 to slow crawling — deindexing risk.

## Key principles
- "More crawling doesn't mean you'll rank better, but pages that aren't crawled and indexed can't rank at all."
- Crawl demand is popularity-driven: links (internal + external) are the lever you control.
- Everything (JS, CSS, XHR, variants) consumes budget — dedupe and simplify.
- Google self-adjusts to server health; keep 5xx/429 low and responses fast.

## ARTICLE cumulative-layout-shift-cls

### What Is Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) & How To Improve It

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
CLS measures visual stability: how much visible content moves during load (scored over the worst 5-second window; shifts within 500 ms of user input are excluded). Good = ≤0.1 at the 75th percentile. Most shifts come from dimensionless images/embeds/ads, late-loading fonts, JS-injected content, and layout-triggering animations — all cheap to fix with space reservation and transform animations.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**The formula:** layout shift score = (% of viewport affected) × (distance moved by unstable elements).

**The 4 fixes:**
1. **Reserve space for images, videos, iframes, ads** — set width/height (or CSS aspect-ratio) on every media element; use width/height with srcset for responsive images; reserve maximum space for dynamic ad slots so the placeholder never grows.
2. **Optimize fonts** — font swaps cause invisible/unstyled text flashes (FOIT/FOUT). Best: system fonts. Otherwise: preload + `font-display: optional` — the font either loads in a brief window or the default font renders this visit (custom font appears on subsequent loads from cache).
3. **Animate without layout changes** — never animate `top`, `left`, `box-shadow`, `box-sizing`, etc.; use `transform` animations instead.
4. **Qualify for back/forward cache (bfcache)** — instant reloads with zero shifts. Requirements: never use the `unload` event, minimize `Cache-Control: no-store`, refresh stale data after bfcache restore, avoid `window.opener` ops, close open connections on navigation, and test cacheability.

**Measurement:** field data (real users, 28-day window) is the ranking input; lab tests diagnose causes ("Avoid large layout shifts" diagnostics identify culprits). Search Console groups failing URLs by template.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Add width/height or aspect-ratio to every image, video, iframe, and embed
- [ ] Reserve max-size slots for ads and dynamically injected embeds
- [ ] Choose font strategy: system font, or preload + font-display: optional
- [ ] Convert layout-triggering animations to transform-based ones
- [ ] Audit JS that injects content above existing content; reserve its space
- [ ] Verify bfcache eligibility (no unload handlers, careful no-store, close connections)
- [ ] Fix templates via Search Console URL groups, not page-by-page

## Data points & benchmarks
- CLS thresholds: good ≤0.1 / needs improvement 0.1-0.25 / poor >0.25.
- 72.8% of sites achieved good CLS in April 2023 (older figure) — the most improved Core Web Vital at the time.
- CLS is similar across desktop and mobile, but worse on slower connections.
- Page-level CLS is worse than origin-level — teams fix high-traffic pages and leave the long tail failing.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Shipping images/embeds without dimensions (the #1 shift cause).
- Using font-display: swap on late-loading fonts — a likely visible jump.
- Animating layout properties instead of transforms.
- Assuming origin-level "good" means every page passes — audit at page/template level.
- Breaking bfcache with unload listeners or blanket no-store headers.

## Key principles
- CLS is about user trust: misclicks from shifting layouts (especially onto ads) destroy UX.
- Space reservation solves most shifts — dimensions are the cheapest fix in all of CWV.
- Exclude user-initiated shifts from worry; only uninstructed movement counts.

### How to Fix "Discovered - currently not indexed"

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
"Discovered - currently not indexed" = Google knows the URL but hasn't crawled it yet. Fix in 5 steps: request indexing, fix crawl-budget waste, improve content quality, add internal links, build backlinks. Google deprioritizes crawling pages it expects to be low-value.

   - Orphan pages (sitemap-only discovery = low importance)

4. **Check internal linking** — pages with few/no internal links look unimportant. Add contextual links (an internal link opportunities report from any SEO tool helps); consider an HTML sitemap page to shorten crawl depth; use real `<a>` tags, not JS onClick() navigation.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Search Console → URL Inspection → Request indexing (≤10-15/day)

- [ ] Fix duplicate variants (www/HTTP/staging)
- [ ] Audit thin/translated/spun/AI content: merge, improve, or noindex
- [ ] Sort crawled pages by inlink count → find weakly-linked pages
- [ ] Find orphan pages (crawl vs sitemap vs logs) → add internal links
- [ ] Internal link opportunities by keyword → add contextual links
- [ ] Check important pages' backlink counts in a backlink tool → build links where weak

## Data points & benchmarks
- Search Console indexing requests: ~10–15 URLs per day (undocumented limit).
- 90% of sites don't need to worry about crawl budget (Google's Gary Illyes).
- Crawl capacity is tracked per hostname; assets on a separate hostname/CDN don't compete with your pages.
- Orphan pages are best found by comparing crawl data with sitemaps, analytics, and server logs — crawlers following links miss them by definition.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Mass-submitting indexing requests without fixing the underlying cause.
- Redirecting deleted pages that have no links or traffic (404 is fine; redirects waste budget).
- Nofollowing internal links to pages you want indexed.
- Relying on JS onClick() navigation instead of crawlable `<a href>` links.
- Expecting sitemap inclusion alone to get pages crawled.

## Key principles
- "Discovered" = known but uncrawled: a prioritization problem, not an error.
- Google predicts quality from similar pages already crawled — site-wide quality affects new pages.
- Internal links = importance signal; backlinks = value signal; both drive crawl priority.
- If content isn't meant for organic search, noindex or remove it — noindex keeps it out of the index but doesn't save crawl budget by itself.

## ARTICLE duplicate-content

### Duplicate Content: Why It Happens and How to Fix It

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
There is NO duplicate content penalty for accidental duplication (only for manipulative/spammy intent), but duplicates still hurt: wrong URL ranks, backlink equity splits, crawl budget burns, scraped copies outrank you. Fix by redirecting or canonicalizing the 12 common technical causes; don't stress over small amounts.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Why duplicates hurt (4 ways):** undesirable URLs in SERPs, backlink dilution, wasted crawl budget, scraped/syndicated copies outranking originals.

**How Google handles it:** clusters duplicate URLs → picks a "best" representative → consolidates signals (canonicalization). It usually works — but not always (one example site: both /library/ and /resources/ versions ranked, with 106 and 144 referring domains split between them).

**12 common causes + fixes:**
1. Faceted/filtered navigation → robots.txt rules, canonicals (complex — see the faceted navigation article)
2. Tracking parameters (UTM) → canonicalize to clean URL
3. Session IDs → canonicalize
4. HTTP/HTTPS + www/non-www variants → 301 redirect to one version
5. Case-sensitive URLs (URL paths are case-sensitive, so /Page and /page can be separate URLs) → consistent internal links, redirect/canonicalize
6. Trailing slash vs not → redirect one version; Google: "if only one version can be returned, that's great"
7. Print-friendly URLs → canonicalize to original
8. Mobile URLs (m.) → canonicalize to desktop + rel="alternate"
9. AMP URLs → canonicalize to non-AMP + rel="amphtml"
10. Tag/category pages → drop tags or noindex them

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR

The canonical tag is only a hint (dozens of canonicalization signals exist); when duplicates, canonical chains/loops, or rendering issues contradict it, the engine picks its own version. Usually harmless for rankings, but you may lose the version you wanted shown.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Causes:**
1. **Duplicate or similar content** — URL variants (case, trailing slash, IDs, parameters), ecommerce product variants with thin unique content, same-language international pages (US/UK/AU) consolidated to one URL (hreflang still swaps the right version in per market), untranslated pages awaiting localization, or syndicated/stolen content where the copy wins.
2. **Canonical tag mistakes** — chains (A→B→C), loops (A→B→A), or typos (stray characters) that get ignored.
3. **Rendering issues** — JavaScript app-shell sites return near-identical empty HTML for every URL; pages get clustered as duplicates before rendering (worst case: wrong page or wrong site ranks).

**Fixes by cause:**
- Duplicates you want indexed separately → make the content genuinely unique (translated pages are NOT duplicates; finish translations).
- Duplicates you want consolidated → self-referencing canonical on the preferred version + canonicals (or 301/308 redirects) from the variants; fix any chain/loop to point every version at the one preferred URL.
- Rendering → pre-rendering or SSR so bots receive unique content in the initial HTML.
- Stolen/syndicated content winning → usually self-resolves (days); for owned content, file a copyright (DMCA) removal request with the engine's takedown tool.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Review the Page Indexing report for this status; inspect example URLs
- [ ] Crawl for duplicate clusters; check which have canonical tags set vs broken
- [ ] Standardize URL variants (case, slash, parameters) and redirect or canonicalize them
- [ ] For product variants: canonical to the main version (or enrich each with unique content)
- [ ] Verify international same-language pages have correct hreflang pairs — "duplicate" status is expected there; traffic reports attribute to the canonical
- [ ] Trace canonical chains/loops; make every version point directly to the preferred URL
- [ ] Check rendered vs raw HTML on JS sites; implement SSR/pre-rendering if shells are identical
- [ ] If scraped content outranks your original, wait a few days, then file a DMCA takedown

## Data points & benchmarks
- ~60% of the web is duplicate content (older estimate from a Google representative).

- "Non-canonical page specified as canonical" (covers chains/loops) appeared on 1.36% of sites in an older industry study of 1M domains.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Panicking: this status usually doesn't impact rankings — the content still ranks via the chosen canonical.
- Assuming hreflang is broken because international pages show as duplicates — swapping alternates is by design.
- Publishing untranslated pages to foreign-language URLs and leaving them (fix by finishing translation).

## Key principles
- Canonicalization is signal consensus: align canonicals, internal links, redirects, sitemaps, and hreflang on one preferred URL.

- [ ] Revisit "maybe" pages on a schedule and convert to redirect/delete decisions

- One-size-fits-all rules (deleting everything, or noindexing everything).
- Removing internal links from pages you're keeping live — it hurts rankings.
- Letting OOS products dominate category listings — UX damage.
- Forgetting historic deleted products with backlinks — permanent value leak.

- Match the page's fate to inventory reality: return = live, gone = redirect/delete, unknown = deprioritize with a timer.

### Faceted Navigation: Definition, Examples & SEO Best Practices

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Faceted navigation (filters) creates near-infinite URL combinations causing duplicate content, index bloat, crawl budget waste, and PageRank dilution. Fix with canonicals → robots.txt blocks → nofollow/remove links → noindex (escalating ladder), or prevent issues entirely with AJAX filters + no crawlable facet links + editorially-created sub-categories for valuable long-tail terms.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**The 4 problems:** duplicate content (facet pages are near-copies), index bloat (valueless URLs indexed), crawl budget waste (crawler traps), PageRank dilution (link equity divided across all filter links).

**Detection:**
1. `site:domain.com` count vs known page count (index bloat signal)
2. Search Console Page indexing report: "Indexed, not submitted in sitemap" + "Crawled - currently not indexed" (spot facet URLs)
3. A site crawler's indexability report: indexable vs non-indexable distribution (e.g., 39 non-indexable per indexable URL = crawl trap); scan URL tables for facet patterns

**Fix ladder (escalate if previous step fails):**
1. **Canonical tags** on facet URLs → point to category page (consolidates link signals; Google may ignore if facet pages differ significantly or are heavily internally linked)
2. **robots.txt disallow** (e.g., `Disallow: *size=*` or `*/size/*`) — fixes crawling; fails with no URL patterns or when selectively allowing; blocked URLs can stay indexed if backlinked
3. **Nofollow / remove internal links** to facet URLs (nofollow is a hint, not directive; PageRank still splits across nofollowed links)
4. **noindex tag / X-Robots-Tag** — surefire deindexing, but requires REMOVING robots.txt blocks so Google can see the tag; signals not consolidated
5. **404 for empty filter combinations** (but 200 + helpful message for temporary stock issues)

**Prevention (ideal setup):**
1. Build filters with AJAX; no `<a href>` links on facets → nothing to crawl, no PageRank dilution
2. Keep URLs shareable via parameters (?) or hashes (#) — hashes are best (Google ignores them); canonicalize parameter versions to clean URL
3. Provide editorial crawl paths (sub-navigation links) to opt-in indexable sub-categories

**Traffic opportunity:** use facets for long-tail capture — in a keyword research tool, group keyword ideas by parent topic → find filter-matching keywords with real volume (e.g., "high rise skinny jeans" ~1,800/mo, illustrative) → create indexable sub-category pages inheriting the filtered listings → optimize URLs/titles/content → add to sitemap. Only do this where demand and unique content exist; don't mass-produce near-duplicate facet pages.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Run site: search; compare to expected page count
- [ ] Search Console Page indexing: review "Indexed, not submitted in sitemap" and "Crawled - currently not indexed" for facet URLs
- [ ] Site crawler indexability distribution: check indexable/non-indexable ratio
- [ ] Keep filter parameter order consistent (variable order multiplies URLs)
- [ ] Choose fix per goal: consolidate signals → canonical; save crawl → robots.txt; guaranteed deindex → noindex (unblock robots first)
- [ ] 404 empty filter combos; 200 for temporary out-of-stock
- [ ] New builds: AJAX filters without href links + shareable URLs + opt-in sub-categories
- [ ] Long-tail play: keyword ideas grouped by parent topic → sub-category pages with clean URLs + unique content + sitemap inclusion

## Data points & benchmarks
- Example crawl trap: 39 non-indexable URLs per indexable URL found in a partial crawl.
- Crawl budget matters at 1M+ unique pages, or 10K+ with content that changes daily (Google guidance).
- In one older study, 99.84% of keywords got <1,000 searches/mo and accounted for 39.33% of total search demand — the long-tail case for facet pages.
- "Freestanding washing machines" UK: ~90 searches/mo — ultra-specific filter combos get ~zero search demand.
- Canonicals are suggestions, not directives — Google ignores them when pages aren't true duplicates or facet pages are heavily linked internally.
- Nofollow (since 2019) = hint; internal nofollow tells Google to deprioritize crawling.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Letting every filter combination be crawlable+indexable by default.
- Inconsistent parameter order creating duplicate combinations.
- Applying noindex while keeping robots.txt blocks — Google never sees the noindex.
- Expecting canonicals to always work on substantially-different facet pages.
- Mass nofollowing facet links when you also want some facet pages indexed.
- 404ing temporarily empty categories (stock issues) — hurts UX.

## Key principles
- Faceted nav is a UX win with SEO costs — manage crawlability deliberately.
- Escalate fixes: canonical → robots.txt → nofollow/link removal → noindex.
- Best practice: filters via AJAX (no crawlable links) + editorial opt-in for valuable facets.
- Facets can be a long-tail traffic machine if paired with keyword research and proper sub-category pages.

## ARTICLE first-input-delay-fid

### Improving First Input Delay (FID): Faster Interactions

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
FID measured the delay between a user's first interaction (click, tap, key input) and the page's response. It was replaced by Interaction to Next Paint (INP) as a Core Web Vital on March 12, 2024 — but the fixes are identical: reduce JavaScript, load it later, break up long main-thread tasks. Most sites already pass responsiveness checks; focus only on failing templates.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**What causes delay:** JavaScript competes for the browser's single main thread (a one-burner stove). While a long task runs, the page can't respond to input; longer task = longer delay. Worse on slower devices.

**The 5 fixes:**
1. **Reduce JavaScript** — especially early-load JS piling onto the main thread; delete before optimizing.
2. **Load JS later** — use `async` (download parallel with parsing; blocks only during execution — good for analytics and scripts needed early) or `defer` (no parse blocking; executes after HTML parsing — for everything not needed immediately).
3. **Break up long tasks** — code splitting into smaller chunks so the thread frees up between tasks and input can be handled.
4. **Use web workers** — move JS off the main thread to a worker (trade-offs: no DOM access, caching considerations; needs a competent developer).
5. **Prerender or SSR** — shift rendering work from the browser to the server on JS-framework sites.

**Measurement:**
- Only real-user (field) data counts for the vital itself; good = 75% of interactions under 100 ms.
- Lab tests can't measure FID (they never click) — use Total Blocking Time (TBT) as the proxy; improving TBT improves FID/INP.
- Search Console groups failing URLs by template; fix the template once to fix the whole group.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Check Search Console CWV report before optimizing — most sites pass; don't fix what isn't failing
- [ ] Audit early-load JavaScript; remove what's unused
- [ ] Tag scripts: async for early/dependent (analytics), defer for the rest
- [ ] Split long tasks / large bundles via code splitting
- [ ] Move heavy computation to a web worker where feasible
- [ ] Consider SSR/prerendering for JS-heavy templates
- [ ] Track TBT in lab tests as the FID/INP proxy
- [ ] Re-check field data after the 28-day CrUX window

## Data points & benchmarks
- Historical FID thresholds (retired metric): good ≤100 ms / needs improvement 100-300 ms / poor >300 ms (75th percentile of real-user interactions).
- 95.3% of sites were already in the good FID bucket as of April 2023 (older figure).
- One Core Web Vitals study found almost no one needed to worry about FID on desktop, and very few on mobile, even on slow connections.
- FID → INP swap: March 12, 2024 (INP covers ALL interactions, not just the first).

## Mistakes to avoid
- Investing in FID fixes without checking field data first — the problem is rare.
- Optimizing only the first interaction and ignoring later ones (that's why INP replaced FID).
- Using async on scripts with unmet dependencies, or defer on scripts needed immediately.
- Hand-rolling web workers without developer support.

## Key principles
- One main thread: every millisecond of long JS is a millisecond of unresponsiveness.
- TBT (lab) and FID/INP (field) improve together — reduce main-thread blocking.
- The FID→INP transition raised the bar: all interactions now count, so long-task hygiene matters more.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**The crawl → index pipeline:**
1. URL collection — from discovered links, sitemaps, RSS feeds, and submitted URLs.
2. Prioritization + fetch — pages are scheduled, downloaded, stored.
3. Processing — extract links, plus resources needed to render (JS, CSS, API calls); those get crawled and cached too.
4. Rendering — pages rendered like a browser would, using cached resources.
5. Re-processing — detect changes and new links; the MOBILE version of the rendered page is what gets stored in the index.
6. New links return to the crawl queue (loop).

**Controlling crawling:**
- robots.txt (what may be crawled)
- nofollow attributes/tags (hint only)
- the Search Console crawl-rate limiter was removed in January 2024 (Googlebot adapts to server health automatically).

**Controlling indexing:**
- Delete the content, or put it behind authentication (crawlers don't log in)
- noindex meta tag / X-Robots-Tag
- Search Console removals tool (temporarily hides; still crawled)
- robots.txt controls crawling, not indexing — a blocked page can still be indexed from links (blocking Googlebot-Image does keep images out of image search).

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Log-file crawler IPs verified against Google's published IP ranges (spoofed bots are common)
- [ ] Search Console Crawl Stats reviewed: which crawler types hit which files, when
- [ ] HTML kept well under 2 MB and other files under 15 MB (check current Google docs); robots.txt under 500 KiB
- [ ] HTTP/2 enabled; compression (gzip/brotli) working
- [ ] Caching validators (ETag / Last-Modified) honored so unchanged pages return 304s
- [ ] No geo-blocking that would exclude the crawler's US-originated requests (verify with care — some locale-adaptive crawling exists)
- [ ] Mobile rendering treated as the source of truth for what's indexed

## Data points & benchmarks
- Googlebot accounted for ~24% of all "good bot" HTTP requests on the web in one CDN's radar data (older snapshot) — the single most active crawler; one SEO-industry crawler followed at ~14%, Bing's at under 5%.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Trusting user-agent strings alone — verify by IP or DNS before granting access or diagnosing "Googlebot" traffic.
- Assuming robots.txt controls indexing — it controls crawling; use noindex (on a crawlable page) to keep a page out of the index.
- Ignoring that the indexed content is the mobile-rendered DOM — desktop-only differences go unnoticed.

## Key principles
- Googlebot is polite by design: it adapts crawl rate to server health — slow responses teach it to crawl less.
- Rendering + caching is central: resources are cached, XHR requests are live, mobile rendered content is what ranks.

## ARTICLE hreflang-tags

### Hreflang: The Easy Guide for Beginners

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Hreflang tells Google which language/region version of a page to show which users. It's bidirectional (every page in a cluster must reference all others + itself), best implemented in XML sitemaps for large sites, and 67% of implementations in one older study had errors. Hreflang is a signal, not a directive.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Syntax:** `<link rel="alternate" hreflang="lang-country" href="URL" />` — ISO 639-1 language (+ optional ISO 3166-1 country, e.g., `en-gb`).

**3 golden rules:**
1. **Bidirectional** — if page A references B, B must reference A (forms an "hreflang cluster"; like a Facebook relationship status).
2. **Self-referencing tags** — each page lists itself plus all variants (optional per Mueller, but best practice).
3. **x-default tag** — fallback page when no language matches (recommended, not mandatory).

**Matching logic:** Google looks for language+country match → language-only match → x-default.

**3 implementation methods:**
1. HTML `<head>` tags — easy but must edit every variant when adding languages.
2. HTTP headers — for PDFs/non-HTML.
3. XML sitemap (xhtml:link markup) — often the most efficient: one file, no page edits, no page-weight overhead. Best for large sites; semi-automate with a spreadsheet template.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Every page in a cluster lists ALL variants + itself (+ x-default)
- [ ] Valid ISO codes only (en-gb not en-UK; dash not underscore)
- [ ] One URL per language per cluster (no duplicates like en-us AND en-uk pointing to identical content without localization)
- [ ] Hreflang points to canonical URLs only (never non-canonical)
- [ ] Hreflang targets return 200 (not broken/redirected)
- [ ] Don't block hreflang pages in robots.txt; don't noindex them
- [ ] Keep HTML lang attribute consistent with hreflang
- [ ] Schedule recurring site crawls with an hreflang check (return-tag errors, non-canonical or broken targets); Search Console's International Targeting report was retired
- [ ] Verify country traffic per language version in analytics or an SEO tool (e.g., /de/ should get DE/AT/CH traffic)
- [ ] Don't auto-redirect by IP/browser language — use a banner suggestion instead (redirects can block crawler access and risk cloaking)

## Data points & benchmarks
- 67% of hreflang implementations across 374,756 domains had issues (older industry study).
- Hreflang is one of ~40 Google canonicalization signals; cluster pages can share ranking signals (strongest page's rank position applies, SERP swaps to the user's best-match variant).
- Google + Yandex use hreflang; Bing has said it treats it as a weak signal (relies on content-language attribute, links, visitors); Baidu ignores it.
- No limit to number of hreflang tags per page.
- Google can often tolerate minor errors (underscore vs dash, en-UK vs en-GB, missing self-reference, relative URLs) — but don't rely on it; fix them.
- Canada language split: 56.9% English, 21.3% French (older census figures; example of multi-language same-country targeting).

## Mistakes to avoid
- One-way hreflang references (missing return tags) — cluster breaks.
- Same URL referenced for multiple languages.
- Hreflang + contradictory canonical tags.
- Auto-redirecting users by IP — cloaking risk and breaks indexing; use a small banner instead (too-large banner = interstitial penalty risk).
- Hreflang tags forced into `<body>` by broken `<head>` parsing (iframes, unclosed tags, JS injection) — ignored there.
- Assuming hreflang guarantees the right page shows — it's a signal, not a directive; localize same-language variants (pricing, spelling) to help.

## Key principles
- "One of the most complex aspects of SEO" — John Mueller.
- Hreflang = correct language version for users + duplicate-content mitigation + shared ranking signals within clusters.
- One practitioner prefers a single page per language with dynamic country personalization instead of en-us/en-uk duplicates.
- Automate implementation (sitemap + spreadsheet), audit continuously — errors are inevitable at scale.

## ARTICLE image-seo

### Image SEO: 12 Actionable Tips

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Image optimization is filenames, alt text, the right format, correct dimensions, compression, plus delivery mechanics (sitemap, srcset, lazy loading, caching, CDN). Even with machine-learning image recognition, search engines misread images — explicit signals still matter, and images are usually the largest contributor to page weight, so this work pays twice: in image search and in page speed.

## Framework / Step-by-step
1. **Descriptive filenames** — `my-new-black-kitten.jpg`, not `IMG00023.JPG`. No keyword stuffing.
2. **Alt text + captions** — formula: "This is a photograph of ___" → use the ending as alt text; add product/serial numbers for product images; place images near relevant text with captions.
3. **Right file type** — WebP or AVIF usually compress best (serve them with JPEG/PNG fallbacks via `<picture>` where needed); JPEG for photographs, PNG for line art/transparency; prefer video or animated WebP/AVIF over GIF.
4. **Resize to actual display width** — browsers download the full file even when displaying smaller; find your content column's max width in CSS and cap uploads there.
5. **Compress aggressively** — dial quality down; visual results stay good while savings are large. Batch-compress (keep JPEG and PNG in separate batches; keep filenames intact).
6. **Image sitemap** — adds images search engines might miss; may reference other domains (CDN-hosted images are fine — verify the CDN domain in Search Console).
7. **Vector graphics (SVG)** for logos/icons/text — minify + gzip; razor sharp at every resolution, tiny files.
8. **Responsive images (`srcset`)** — serve multiple widths; the browser picks the efficient one per device/DPR. Most CMSes generate sizes + srcset automatically.
9. **Structured data** — product, recipe, and video markup adds descriptive badges in image search results.
10. **Lazy loading** — recommended by page-speed tooling; implement so search engines still see the content, and never lazy-load the hero/LCP image.
11. **Browser caching** — cache images ~1 year via expires headers.
12. **CDN** — serve from edge servers near users; use a CNAME alias (cdn.yourdomain.com) so links and future migrations stay on your domain; image CDNs automate resizing/format negotiation.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Filenames descriptive across new uploads
- [ ] Alt text present on meaningful images (decorative can be empty)
- [ ] Modern format (WebP/AVIF with fallbacks) matched to content type
- [ ] No image uploaded wider than its display max width
- [ ] Compression run on legacy image libraries
- [ ] Image sitemap submitted (or images included in main sitemap)
- [ ] srcset/responsive variants served
- [ ] Lazy loading verified crawlable/rendered
- [ ] Cache headers set for images
- [ ] CDN on your own subdomain (CNAME)

## Data points & benchmarks
- Same photo, same visible quality: 31 KB vs 73 KB (58% smaller) — compression is free bandwidth.
- In a 15-image compression test: best tool averaged 69% reduction on JPEGs; dropping PNG quality settings to 40% achieved ~76% reduction.
- An 8.54 KB SVG minified + gzipped to 3.56 KB (42% saving).
- In one example, a brand found 2,300+ backlinks from 158 sites pointing at image/CDN file URLs instead of pages — every one a link-recovery opportunity.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Keyword-stuffed filenames or alt attributes — spam signal, poor UX.
- Uploading multi-thousand-pixel originals for a 720px content column.
- CDN URLs on a third-party domain — future migrations and earned links point at the wrong host.
- Letting embedders link to the .jpg file instead of the page — recover that equity with quick, polite outreach (success rates are often good).

## Key principles
- Every image signal (name, alt, caption, page context, schema) helps disambiguate what machine vision gets wrong.
- Image work IS speed work: images dominate page weight, and speed affects UX and Core Web Vitals (a minor ranking factor).
- Don't count on EXIF data as a ranking signal; strip GPS/personal metadata before publishing (it also shrinks files).

## ARTICLE indexed-though-blocked-by-robots-txt

### "Indexed, though blocked by robots.txt" Can Be More Than A Robots.txt Block

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
This Search Console status means URLs you blocked from crawling are still indexed — because crawling and indexing are separate processes, and a robots.txt-blocked page can still be indexed from links. The block itself may live in robots.txt, or hide deeper: intermittent blocks, user-agent blocks, or IP blocks. First decide whether you even want the URL indexed; that decides the fix.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Decision 1 — You DON'T want it indexed:**
- Add a noindex meta robots tag AND allow crawling (a crawler must see the tag to obey it).
- If the URL canonicalizes elsewhere, don't noindex — just set proper canonical signals and allow crawling so signals consolidate.

**Decision 2 — You DO want it indexed (find and remove the block), in this order:**
1. **robots.txt crawl block** — use the robots.txt report (Search Console → Settings) to find the offending Disallow (watch for `Disallow: /` sitewide blocks, common on new sites and post-migration; check CMS "search engine visibility" settings that write this line). Fix by deleting the rule (CMS setting, plugin file editor, FTP/hosting file manager).
2. **Intermittent blocks** — check robots.txt version history (the robots.txt report's fetch history, deploy history, web archives). Classic cause: a shared cache between staging and production alternately serving a blocking robots.txt — split the cache or exclude .txt files from it.
3. **User-agent blocks** — site detects and blocks specific bots (via .htaccess, server config, firewall, CDN, or hosting). Test by spoofing the user agent (browser devtools, an extension, or `curl -A "user-agent" -Lv URL`); if blocked only as the bot, hunt the rule or ask the host/CDN where it lives.
4. **IP blocks** — bot IPs denied at server/firewall level (e.g., `deny from x.x.x.x`). Hardest to self-diagnose; escalate to hosting/CDN.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] For each flagged URL, decide: want indexed or not
- [ ] Not wanted: noindex meta + crawl allowed; canonicalized duplicates: canonicals + crawl allowed
- [ ] Wanted: check the robots.txt report (Search Console → Settings) and URL Inspection; remove the Disallow
- [ ] Check CMS visibility settings ("discourage search engines" style toggles) after launches/migrations
- [ ] Review robots.txt history for intermittent blocks; split staging/production caches
- [ ] Curl with the bot's user agent to detect UA-specific blocks
- [ ] Escalate unexplained blocks to hosting/CDN (firewall/IP rules)
- [ ] Re-validate the fix in Search Console after recrawl

## Data points & benchmarks
- Crawling ≠ indexing: blocked pages stay indexable via links — blocking crawl never guarantees deindexing.
- Whole-site `User-agent: * / Disallow: /` blocks most often appear on brand-new sites or right after migrations.
- Recurring "fixed then broken" status = intermittent block until proven otherwise.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Expecting robots.txt to deindex pages — it can't; it only hides the noindex tag.
- Pairing noindex with a robots.txt block (crawler never sees the noindex).
- Noindexing URLs that canonicalize elsewhere — conflicting instructions.
- Checking only the live robots.txt and missing historical/intermittent versions.

## Key principles
- If a page shouldn't rank: noindex + crawl allowed. If it should: remove every layer of block (file, UA, IP).
- Blocks can exist in at least four places: robots.txt, cache layers, UA rules, IP rules.
- Test as the bot, not as a human browser.

## ARTICLE javascript-seo

### JavaScript SEO: Issues & Best Practices

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
JavaScript SEO makes JS-heavy sites easy to crawl, render, and index. Most sites' JS causes no problems — issues arise when JS builds entire pages or injects/changes key elements. The safest architecture is server-side rendering, static generation, or prerendering; full client-side rendering is the riskiest and worst-supported by other search engines.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**How Google processes JS pages (two-stage indexing):**
1. Crawl — downloads HTML plus linked resources (file-size caps apply — 2 MB for HTML per a 2026 doc update, 15 MB default for other files; mobile user-agent first).
2. Processing — extracts links/resources to queue, caches every file aggressively, eliminates duplicates, applies the most restrictive directive seen.
3. Render queue — pages with noindex in the raw HTML are not rendered; news pages index from raw HTML first, rendered later.
4. Renderer — an evergreen headless Chromium. No fixed timeout; no clicking or scrolling, but the viewport is stretched (mobile 411→12,140px tall) so "below the fold" content is still seen; pixels are never painted.

**Core best practices:**
1. Unique titles/meta descriptions per page (JS frameworks are templatized — check for duplicates; SEO modules like head-tag managers exist per framework).
2. Canonicals: JS-inserted canonicals are respected only if none exists in raw HTML; never end up with two.
3. Remember: the most restrictive directive wins across raw vs rendered HTML (noindex in raw HTML never even reaches the renderer).
4. Don't block JS/CSS files in robots.txt — including on domains serving your API.
5. Verify content is DOM-loaded by default (search a quoted phrase in Google; use a browser-based SEO toolbar to compare raw vs rendered HTML; check anything behind accordions/dropdowns — Google doesn't click).
6. Links must be real `<a href>` elements — not onclick handlers, `javascript:` URLs, spans, or `<option>` tags.
7. Avoid `#` fragments for routing or parameters — use history-mode routers and clean URLs.
8. Handle error pages: JS can't return a real 404 — redirect to a real 404 URL, or noindex + error message (soft 404).
9. Infinite scroll: keep a paginated fallback; consider blocking the scroll-triggering script if pages merge in the index.
10. Use file versioning/fingerprinting (file.12345.js) so updated resources bust search-engine caches.
11. Structured data via JS-injected JSON-LD works — validate it.
12. Support HTTP connections — crawlers don't do WebSockets/WebRTC (provide fallbacks).

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Render-critical content present in DOM without clicks
- [ ] Raw vs rendered HTML compared for titles, canonicals, robots tags
- [ ] JS/CSS crawlable in robots.txt (site + API domains)
- [ ] All internal links are `<a href>` anchors
- [ ] No hash-based routing/parameters
- [ ] Real 404 handling (or noindex fallback)
- [ ] Paginated versions exist alongside infinite scroll
- [ ] Sitemap generated via the framework/router ecosystem
- [ ] Alt attributes set on meaningful images
- [ ] URL Inspection tool used as source of truth for what the search engine sees

## Data points & benchmarks
- In an older study of title rewriting, Google rewrote titles 33.4% of the time and meta descriptions 62.78% of the time.
- In one older study, 67% of domains using hreflang had implementation errors.
- In one older study, pages reached the renderer at a median of ~5 seconds after crawling; 90th percentile was minutes — rendering delay is rarely a real problem.
- Rendering viewport (observed in one test): mobile loaded at 411x731, resized to 411x12140; desktop 1024x768 → 1024x9307.
- Googlebot's documented HTML size cutoff is 2 MB (15 MB default for other file types); keep HTML lean, especially with inlined JSON/state.
- XHR/fetch requests are NOT cached — they're fetched live during rendering and consume significant crawl budget.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Relying on full client-side rendering — other search engines (and social media crawlers reading OG tags) have limited or no JS support.
- Dynamic rendering (serving pre-rendered content per bot) — a discouraged, complexity-adding workaround.
- Checking pages with JavaScript disabled or via search-engine cache — both misrepresent what the crawler sees.
- Overwriting a raw-HTML noindex/canonical with JS and expecting the JS version to win — most restrictive signal prevails.
- The "five-second render timeout" myth — it comes from testing tools that fetch resources live and cut off early, not from the production renderer.

## Key principles
- Search engines load each page stateless, like a fresh visit — partial DOM updates during user navigation usually don't matter.
- Caching is aggressive: pages, JS, CSS are all cached and re-served at render time, which can occasionally index "impossible states" mixing old and new file versions — hence versioning.
- Don't fear JavaScript; partner with developers. The SEO's job is knowing how crawlers process it and how to troubleshoot, not writing it.

## ARTICLE keyword-cannibalization

### Keyword Cannibalization: What It Really Is & How to Fix It

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Cannibalization is NOT "Google getting confused by similar pages" — pages rank for hundreds of keywords, and overlapping keywords alone prove nothing. A real issue exists only when multiple pages target the same keyword AND the same intent, and merging them would net more traffic. The fix is consolidation (merge + 301), never keyword-level hacks like noindex, canonicals, or de-optimization.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**The test:** same keyword + same/very similar intent + evidence that consolidation beats the status quo. Different intents ranking for one keyword is normal and healthy (each page keeps its own long-tail traffic).

**How to find real issues:**
1. Content audit — overlapping-topic pages surface naturally on small/mid sites.
2. Historic rankings — filter your keyword in a rank tracker; multiple URLs swapping in and out (none ranking high) is the signature.
3. `site:yourdomain.com "topic"` searches — noisy (hundreds of vague matches), use only to enumerate candidates.
4. Un-filter Google results — append `&filter=0` to the SERP URL to disable host clustering and see more of your ranking URLs (this parameter may not always work); the top one is the consolidation target.
5. "Multiple URLs ranking" filter in a rank tracker — quick but confirm with ranking history.

**How to fix (consolidation):**
1. Write the new, merged guide (combine the best of each page).
2. Publish it at the strongest existing URL (the one Google already ranks highest).
3. Delete and 301-redirect the other pages to it.
4. Update all internal links that pointed at the old URLs.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Candidate pairs checked for intent match, not just keyword match
- [ ] Ranking history reviewed before deciding (is performance actually suffering?)
- [ ] Consolidation target = highest-ranking/most-linked URL
- [ ] Unique content from losers merged into the keeper
- [ ] 301 redirects from removed pages
- [ ] Internal links updated off the redirected URLs
- [ ] Traffic monitored pre/post merge to confirm the win

## Data points & benchmarks
- One site merged two competing guides on the same topic in 2018: afterward, the single consolidated page earned considerably more traffic than both old pages combined.
- A typical top-10 ranking page ranks for hundreds of keywords — removing it sacrifices its entire long-tail footprint, not one keyword.
- Top 10 pages averaged ~1,000 keywords in one older industry study of ranking footprints.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Deleting the "loser" page without merging its content and redirecting it — you lose all its other keyword rankings.
- Noindexing it — drops it from the index entirely; worst of both worlds.
- Canonicalizing it — canonicals are for duplicate/near-duplicate content, not similar-topic pages.
- De-optimizing for a single keyword — impossible to surgically remove one keyword without affecting the rest.
- "Fixing" cannibalization at keyword level when pages serve different intents.

## Key principles
- Google doesn't get confused; it ranks pages as it sees fit. The question is only whether consolidation improves total organic performance.
- Bigger sites accumulate overlapping content through teams and time — periodic content audits catch it early.
- Signals (backlinks, internal links) get diluted across clones; consolidation concentrates them.

## ARTICLE large-site-migration-playbook

### Large-Site Migration Playbook (10k–1M URLs, with Log Validation)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Big migrations fail from missing URLs, not bad redirects. Build a complete URL inventory from crawls, logs, sitemaps, backlinks, analytics and Search Console. Map it by template with rules, then by hand for high-value URLs. Test on staging, launch in a low-traffic window, then validate with crawls *and* server logs for 4–8 weeks. Expect a temporary dip. A dip that hasn't recovered after about 8–12 weeks means something is broken.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Phase 1: Inventory (weeks −8 to −6)**
1. Union of URL sources: a full crawl, the last 6–12 months of server logs (every URL Googlebot requested), XML sitemaps, Search Console page exports, analytics landing pages, and backlink-tool target URLs (pages with referring domains).
2. Deduplicate and normalize (protocol, trailing slash, case, parameters). Tag each URL with template type, traffic, conversions, referring domains and index status.
3. Choose your **priority tier**: the top ~5–10% of URLs by traffic, revenue or links get manual mapping and QA. Everything else is rule-based.

**Phase 2: Redirect map (weeks −6 to −3)**
4. Write rules per template (for example `/blog/{slug}` → `/resources/{slug}`), then generate the map programmatically.
5. Manually map the priority tier one-to-one to the closest-equivalent new URL. Never send everything to the homepage.
6. Retired content: redirect to the closest relevant page. If none exists, return 404/410 deliberately; don't create soft 404s.
7. Collapse chains: old→new must be a single hop, and old internal redirects must be updated to their final targets.

**Phase 3: Staging QA (weeks −3 to −1)**
8. Crawl staging (blocked from indexing with authentication, *not* robots.txt alone). Check status codes, canonicals, hreflang, meta robots, structured data, internal links (to final URLs), pagination and rendered content parity for JavaScript pages.
9. Run the full old-URL list through the staging redirect rules. Target ≥99% returning 301→200, zero loops, zero chains.
10. Benchmark performance (Core Web Vitals) on key templates.

**Phase 4: Launch (day 0)**
11. Deploy redirects together with the new site. Remove staging auth and noindex. Check robots.txt.
12. Submit new XML sitemaps. Temporarily keep an *old-URL* sitemap as well, so Google recrawls the redirects faster.
13. For domain changes: verify both properties in Search Console and use the Change of Address tool. Re-upload an existing disavow file only if you had one.
14. Update canonical tags, hreflang, internal links, paid and email links, and high-value external links you control.

**Phase 5: Validation (weeks 0–8)**
15. **Crawl the old-URL list daily for the first week, then weekly.** Every URL should return 301 to a 200 page.
16. **Watch the logs:**
   - Verify Googlebot by reverse DNS or published IP ranges.
   - Track Googlebot hits per day on old vs new URLs. Old-URL hits should decline as new-URL hits rise.
   - Track the status-code mix (watch spikes in 404, 5xx or 302).
   - Note directories Googlebot hasn't touched since launch.
17. Search Console: watch Pages (indexed/not indexed), crawl stats, and the performance of old vs new URLs. Note the launch date in your reporting and analytics annotations.
18. Rank and traffic tracking on the priority tier. Investigate any page that lost more than 30% after about 4 weeks.

**Short on time? A compressed 4-week plan** (higher risk, so cut scope rather than QA):
- Week −4: inventory (crawl + logs + Search Console + backlink targets) and rule-based redirect map; hand-map the top ~5% of URLs.
- Week −3: build redirects on staging; test the full old-URL list (≥99% single-hop 301 → 200).
- Week −2: content/template parity check, internal links to final URLs, new sitemaps (split into a sitemap index, max 50,000 URLs or 50 MB per file).
- Week −1: go/no-go review. **No-go if** redirect pass rate < 99%, priority URLs not 1:1 mapped, or staging still has noindex/robots blocks. Prepare a rollback (keep old routing deployable).
- Weeks 0–4: daily old-URL crawl in week 1, log monitoring, Page indexing report; investigate any priority page down > 30%.
- Same-domain folder moves (e.g., `/blog/` → `/resources/`) don't use the Change of Address tool; redirects, internal links, canonicals and sitemaps do the work.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] URL inventory from at least 5 sources, deduplicated and tagged
- [ ] Priority tier mapped by hand; the rest mapped by tested rules
- [ ] ≥99% of old URLs → single-hop 301 → 200 on staging
- [ ] Staging protected by authentication, not just robots.txt or noindex
- [ ] Internal links point to final URLs (no internal redirects)
- [ ] Old-URL sitemap submitted alongside new sitemaps at launch
- [ ] Change of Address tool used (domain moves)
- [ ] Log dashboard: Googlebot hits by status code and old/new URL, daily
- [ ] Keep redirects in place for at least 1 year (ideally permanently)
- [ ] Post-launch review at weeks 1, 2, 4 and 8

## Data points & benchmarks
- Google recommends keeping redirects for at least one year. Removing them early loses accumulated signals and breaks backlinks.
- Typical healthy migrations show a temporary fluctuation that recovers within weeks. Large structural or domain changes can take longer to settle.
- Pages with referring domains are the costliest to lose. Several practitioners value each recovered referring domain at hundreds of dollars in equivalent link-building cost.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Building the map from a crawl alone. Crawls miss orphaned URLs that logs and backlinks reveal.
- Redirecting everything to the homepage or a category page (treated as soft 404s)
- Launching with staging noindex or a robots.txt disallow still in place
- Changing URLs, templates, content and domain at once with no rollback plan. Stage changes where possible.
- Stopping monitoring after week 1

## Key principles
- Inventory is the migration. A URL you didn't list is a URL you'll lose.
- Logs show what Google actually did; crawls show what you built. You need both.
- Treat high-value URLs as individual projects and the long tail as a rules problem.

## ARTICLE largest-contentful-paint-lcp

### What Is Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) & How To Improve It

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
LCP measures how long the largest visible viewport element (usually the hero image or H1 text block) takes to render. Good = ≤2.5s at the 75th percentile of real-user loads. It's the Core Web Vital sites struggle with most — mobile and slow connections make the threshold nearly impossible without deliberate optimization. The playbook: identify the LCP element, then prioritize, shrink, move closer, and cache the resources it needs.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**What counts as the LCP element:** `<img>`, SVG image, video poster frame, CSS `url()` background image, or block-level text. Elements outside the viewport don't count.

**Improvement steps:**
1. **Find your LCP element** — page speed reports list it under Diagnostics; filter issues by LCP.
2. **Prioritize the critical rendering path** — load LCP-element resources and above-the-fold assets first; defer everything else.
   - Images early: delete if unneeded; compress; `fetchpriority="high"`, Early Hints, and/or preload (with imagesrcset for responsive images).
   - Images late: `loading="lazy"` — never on above-the-fold images.
   - CSS early: minify, remove unused, inline critical CSS into the HTML.
   - CSS late: preload non-critical stylesheets and apply via `onload`.
   - Fonts: preload (good) → `font-display: optional` + preload (better) → system font (best — nothing to load).
   - JS early: inline or preload only what above-the-fold content needs; prerender/SSR on frameworks.
   - JS late: `async` for early/dependent scripts (analytics), `defer` for the rest.
3. **Make files smaller** — tree-shake unused code; compress everything (server/CDN-level).
4. **Serve files closer** — use a CDN (server copies near users).
5. **Host resources on the same server** — avoids extra connection handshakes; otherwise use `preconnect`/`dns-prefetch` (dns-prefetch has broader support).
6. **Cache aggressively** — long cache lifetimes, purged on change; cache at the CDN too.
7. **Emerging extras** — speculative prerendering, signed exchanges, HTTP/3.

**Measurement:** field data (real users, 28-day rolling) is what counts; lab data diagnoses. Search Console buckets failing URLs into template groups — fix once per template.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Identify the LCP element per failing template
- [ ] Add `fetchpriority="high"` / preload to the hero image; compress and size it correctly
- [ ] Lazy-load below-the-fold images only
- [ ] Inline critical CSS; defer the rest via preload + onload
- [ ] Pick a font strategy (system > optional display > preload)
- [ ] Compress all assets; tree-shake unused JS/CSS
- [ ] Put the site + critical assets behind a CDN
- [ ] Add preconnect/dns-prefetch for required third-party origins
- [ ] Set long cache headers with change-triggered purges
- [ ] Verify with lab tests; field data lags 28 days

## Data points & benchmarks
- LCP thresholds: good ≤2.5s / needs improvement 2.5-4s / poor >4s (75th percentile).
- 57.1% of sites achieved good LCP site-wide in April 2023 (older figure) — the hardest vital to pass.
- Mobile LCP is markedly worse than desktop; the threshold is nearly unreachable on slow connections without optimization.
- Many sites pass just by adding Early Hints/preloads for the main image, stylesheets, and fonts.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Lazy-loading above-the-fold (especially the LCP) image.
- Fixing many reported issues but seeing no LCP gain — dependencies and conflicting priorities can mask wins; retest iteratively.
- Inlining ALL CSS/JS (only the critical subset).
- Ignoring connection overhead from scattered third-party asset hosts.

## Key principles
- LCP = perceived load speed; the biggest element is almost always the lever.
- Optimize the critical rendering path: earliest possible discovery + fastest delivery of one element.
- Fix templates, not individual URLs.

## ARTICLE log-file-analysis

### How to Do an SEO Log File Analysis

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Server access logs are the most trustworthy record of what Googlebot actually crawls. Analyze them (verified for fake bots) to find crawl-budget waste on non-200/non-indexable URLs, discover orphan pages, monitor priority URLs, and check bot/cache trends. Small sites can usually get by with Search Console Crawl Stats instead.

## Framework / Step-by-step
1. **Get access logs** (not error logs) from developers — be specific. Expect hurdles: data split across origin/load balancer/CDN servers, terabyte file sizes, PII (user IPs) compliance, short storage retention.
2. **Store logs** — a log-shipping integration from your CDN/server + a data warehouse = long-term storage you own; configure to strip PII.
3. **Verify Googlebot** — fake Googlebots are common. Options: reverse DNS lookup per Google's guide, firewall bad-bot rule sets, or match against Google's published Googlebot IP ranges (never a rough "66." prefix filter), or your CDN/WAF's managed fake-bot rule.
4. **Extract data** — SQL query in your data warehouse pulling date, URL, IP, user agent, status code, cache status, content type; filter `user_agent LIKE '%Googlebot%' OR user_agent LIKE '%bingbot%'`. Export CSV → spreadsheet.
5. **Enrich with data from a site audit crawler** — add organic traffic, status codes, crawl depth, indexability, internal links from a page-level crawl export.
6. **Run the analyses** (see checklist).

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Status codes overview: what share of bot hits are non-200? (crawl budget waste)
- [ ] URLs overview: filter non-200 URLs with many internal inlinks → fix via an internal link opportunities report
- [ ] Crawl budget wastage: frequently crawled 200 HTML pages that are non-indexable (canonicalized/noindexed) → investigate why (internal links, sitemap inclusion, external links) → remove the links or fix the signals (don't robots.txt-block URLs whose noindex or canonical Google needs to read)
- [ ] Priority URL monitoring: track daily hits for a short list of key URLs; verify redirects are picked up quickly
- [ ] Orphan URLs: 200 HTML URLs with hits but zero internal links → add internal links, and include them in a custom URL-list crawl to audit them
- [ ] Directories overview: is Googlebot over-crawling low-traffic directories vs high-value ones?
- [ ] CDN cache status: a high cache-hit ratio helps CWV/TTFB; investigate miss/dynamic causes
- [ ] Bots overview: which bots crawl most; track changes post-migration

## Data points & benchmarks
- Search Console Crawl Stats: shows samples, max 3 months history, not all URLs — enough for small sites.
- Log files benefit large/frequently-changing sites most (mirrors Google's crawl-budget guidance).
- Many Googlebot IPv4 addresses start with "66.", but that is NOT reliable — verify with reverse DNS or Google's published Googlebot IP ranges.
- Google crawls popular URLs more; infrequent crawling of static URLs is normal, not a problem.
- `<lastmod>` in XML sitemaps: Google's public statements have varied over the years; current docs say it is used only when consistently accurate.
- Many CDN/WAF plans offer fake-bot blocking.
- High-traffic site access logs can reach terabytes+.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Requesting "log files" without specifying access logs (developers will ask which).
- Analyzing unverified Googlebot data — fake bots inflate numbers.
- Assuming non-200 hits are always a problem — Google crawls old 301s for years; worry when internal links point to non-200s.
- Assuming infrequent crawl = problem — normal for rarely-changing URLs.
- Cache misses: parameterized URLs often bypass the CDN cache by default.
- Relying on `<lastmod>` alone given Google's inconsistent signals.

## Key principles
- Log files = ground truth of crawler behavior; everything else is inference.
- Crawl budget optimization = maximize hits on indexable, valuable, changed URLs.
- Cross-analyzing logs + crawl data + analytics beats any single source.
- Ownership of log data (your own warehouse) sidesteps PII and access friction long-term.

## ARTICLE meta-robots

### Robots Meta Tag & X-Robots-Tag: Indexation Control

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
The robots meta tag (HTML) and X-Robots-Tag (HTTP header) control how search engines index pages and snippets — robots.txt cannot do this. Use noindex for pages that shouldn't rank (thin, staging, admin, PPC landers), X-Robots-Tag for non-HTML files and bulk rules, and never combine noindex with a robots.txt block.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**The two mechanisms:**
1. Robots meta tag — `<meta name="robots" content="noindex, nofollow">` in `<head>`; `name` targets a specific crawler (`robots` = all).
2. X-Robots-Tag — HTTP response header with the same values; the only option for PDFs/images, and the scalable option (regex matching via server config).

**Content values (Google-supported):**
- `noindex` / `nofollow` / `all` (default; never needed) / `none` (avoid — not universally supported)
- `noarchive`, `notranslate`, `noimageindex`
- `unavailable_after: <RFC850 date>` — timed noindex for promos/contests
- Snippet controls: `nosnippet`, `max-snippet:[n]`, `max-image-preview:[none|standard|large]`, `max-video-preview:[n]`; plus `data-nosnippet` HTML attribute on div/span/section
- `indexifembedded` — pairs with noindex for iframe-embedded content

Conflicting directives resolve to the MOST restrictive one.

**When to use which:**
- Meta tag: HTML pages, per-page control, engines that ignore headers.
- X-Robots-Tag: PDFs/images, whole-subdirectory or parameterized bulk noindexing, server/CDN-level control.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] noindex on: thin pages, staging, admin/thank-you, internal search results, PPC landers, unreleased promos
- [ ] No page is BOTH noindexed and blocked in robots.txt
- [ ] Noindexed pages kept in sitemap (with refreshed lastmod) UNTIL deindexed, then removed
- [ ] Staging noindex/disallow removed before production pushes (add to dev checklist)
- [ ] "Secret" URLs noindexed, not listed in robots.txt (robots.txt is public — leaks URLs)
- [ ] X-Robots-Tag used for PDFs/images that shouldn't be indexed
- [ ] HTTP headers spot-checked after server config changes
- [ ] max-image-preview:large set on pages with 1200px+ images (feed/Discover eligibility)

## Data points & benchmarks
- A `noindex` line in robots.txt was never officially supported; Google informally honored it until September 1, 2019 and now ignores it.
- Snippet preview directives arrived in October 2019 (copyright-driven): `max-snippet:-1, max-image-preview:large, max-video-preview:-1` = no snippet restrictions.
- Large image previews need source images ≥1200px wide.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Blocking noindexed pages in robots.txt — search engines can't see the noindex, so URLs can stay indexed; you also lose the recrawl signal.
- Removing a page from the sitemap the same day you noindex it — sitemap + fresh lastmod accelerates recrawling.
- `none` directive — other search engines don't support it.
- Bulk-editing server config without backups — one syntax error can take down the site.
- Relying on meta robots for engines that only read headers (or vice versa) — know your target engines.

## Key principles
- robots.txt = can we crawl it; meta robots/X-Robots-Tag = can you index/show it. Two separate systems.
- Deindexing is a process: noindex → keep crawlable → keep in sitemap → confirm removal → clean up sitemap.
- Indexation directives are page-level; combine with sitemaps and robots.txt for sitewide strategy.

## ARTICLE mobile-first-indexing

### Mobile-First Indexing Goes Mobile-Only

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Mobile-first indexing means the mobile version of your content is what gets indexed and ranked — and since July 2024, content entirely inaccessible on mobile is no longer indexable at all. The rollout is complete (2016-2023), there's one index, and there's no opting out. Keep important content, links, and technical signals fully present on mobile.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Timeline:** mobile-friendliness as ranking factor (Apr 2015) → mobile-first indexing announced (Nov 2016) → rollout (Mar 2018) → >50% of sites (Dec 2018) → default for new sites (Jul 2019) → >70% (Mar 2020) → last stragglers moved (May 2023; a few desktop-only relics remain) → **mobile-only: content not accessible on mobile becomes unindexable (Jul 2024)**.

Verify your status: Search Console Settings → About (shows the indexing crawler); the URL Inspection tool shows "Crawled as: [bot] smartphone". Desktop crawling still happens occasionally (to relate desktop/mobile versions) — its presence is normal.

**Best practices:**
1. **Build mobile-friendly** — responsive design, mobile speed, mobile SERP optimization, no intrusive interstitials, device-split performance/rank tracking.
2. **Keep important content** — mobile doesn't need to be identical, but anything omitted "because it's long" can hurt. Content hidden behind UX patterns (tabs, accordions, expandables) is no longer discounted — ecommerce does this well (progressive product details, Q&A, reviews).
3. **Navigation & links** — external links consolidate fine with correct canonicals; ensure internal links (breadcrumbs, full menus) survive on mobile — missing links change how PageRank flows.
4. **Technical checks** — crawl once with a desktop user agent, again with mobile, and diff the results: on-page tags (titles, descriptions, canonicals, robots, hreflang, structured data, image alt), robots.txt differences (especially m-dot subdomains), CSS/JS/image crawlability, mobile Core Web Vitals, interstitials, viewport/text size/tap targets (check in Lighthouse or Chrome DevTools device mode; Search Console's Mobile Usability report and the Mobile-Friendly Test were retired in Dec 2023).

**Known failure modes (from engine guidance):** missing structured data or titles on mobile, noindex on mobile URLs, missing/blocked/low-quality images or alt text, mobile URL as error page or with anchor fragments, mobile page blocked by robots.txt, duplicate mobile targets, desktop redirecting to mobile homepage, video/host-load issues.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Confirm mobile-first indexing status and "Crawled as" agent in Search Console
- [ ] Crawl with mobile UA; diff vs desktop crawl for missing tags, links, and content
- [ ] Keep all important content and internal links (incl. breadcrumbs) on mobile
- [ ] Use tabs/accordions freely for UX — hidden content is fine when user-triggered
- [ ] Compare m-dot robots.txt vs main; allow CSS/JS/images everywhere
- [ ] Check mobile CWV (field data in Search Console/PageSpeed Insights) and run Lighthouse/DevTools mobile checks (viewport, tap targets, text size)
- [ ] Remove full-screen interstitials (except legal gates)
- [ ] Verify nothing on the site is desktop-only accessible

## Data points & benchmarks
- Rollout took ~7 years (2016 → May 2023 complete).
- Jul 2024 cliff: content invisible on mobile = not indexable.
- One index; no opt-out; desktop-UA crawls still occur for version relationship checks.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Trimming mobile content, internal links, or structured data "for space."
- Different robots.txt rules on m-dot subdomains.
- Blocking CSS/JS from crawling — rendering needs them.
- Assuming hidden/tabbed content is discounted — that rule died with mobile-first indexing.

## Key principles
- If it's not on mobile, it's not in the index — mobile parity is a hard requirement now.
- Hidden-behind-interaction content is fine; absent content is not.
- Crawl-and-diff (desktop vs mobile) is the core audit technique.

- [ ] Segment traffic and rankings by device; investigate device-specific drops

- Mobile ≈59% of global website traffic (Q4 2022).

- Counterpoint: one large SEO blog still gets 70%+ of organic traffic from desktop — B2B/SaaS audiences skew desktop; don't abandon desktop.

- Serving less content, fewer internal links, or different canonicals on mobile.
- Separate mobile URLs (m.) or dynamic serving — maintenance and misserve headaches.
- Assuming desktop rankings = mobile rankings.
- Full-screen pop-ups that block content on load.
- Ignoring mobile regressions (layout, CWV, tap targets) after dev releases.

## Key principles
- The crawler sees your mobile site first — mobile IS your site as far as indexing goes.
- Parity is the rule: same content, links, and signals on every device.

## ARTICLE negative-seo

### How to Detect (and Deflect) Negative SEO Attacks

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Negative SEO = competitors using black-hat tactics (spam links, fake link-removal requests, scraping, fake parameters, fake reviews, hacking, DDoS) to sabotage your rankings. It rarely works today — link spam is devalued rather than demoted, granularly — but vigilance is cheap: monitor new/lost backlinks, indexed-page counts, security issues, and brand SERPs, and harden the site so most attacks can't land.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Why attacks mostly fail:** since the link-spam algorithm update of 2016, Google devalues ("ignore this junk") rather than demotes sites, and does it granularly (page/section, not necessarily whole site). Its business model depends on spam not winning. Non-link attacks (hacks, reviews, DDoS) remain the real risks.

**The 7 attack types and defenses:**
1. **Spammy link building** (volume blasts or exact-match anchor manipulation) — detect via new-backlink alerts (abnormal referring-domain spikes), backlink graphs, anchor-text distribution (unnatural keyword-rich %), referring IP/subnet breakdown (hundreds of domains on one subnet = PBN). Fight: usually ignore (Google filters it); disavow only if you have a self-made link history, are in an ultra-competitive vertical, or traffic drops coincide with the attack.
2. **Fake link removal requests** — attackers email your linkers pretending to be you. Detect via lost-backlink alerts; when good links vanish without cause, ask the linking site why and request reinstatement.
3. **Content scraping** — copies can outrank originals on stronger domains. Detect by quoting a sentence in search (32-word limit), checking "Google-selected canonical" in the URL Inspection tool (an external canonical = possible scraping), or scale checkers. Fight: request attribution links, file DMCA takedowns as last resort; strong internal linking signals originality.
4. **False URL parameters** — attackers link to your URLs with fake ?params hoping to trigger duplicate indexing. Detect: sudden indexed-page spikes in the Page indexing report. Prevent: self-referencing canonicals on every page.
5. **Fake reviews** — monitor brand-review SERPs monthly (from multiple locations for local). Fight: report fakes to platforms (slow); never report genuine bad reviews — respond to them; drown fakes with a steady stream of genuine reviews.
6. **Hacking** — malware flags or "this site may be hacked" labels destroy rankings and CTR. Detect: Search Console Security Issues report. Prevent: strong passwords, updated CMS/plugins, security monitoring/cleanup services.
7. **DDoS** — flood your server (multi-terabit-per-second attacks have been recorded) or subtly slow it (hurting UX and CWV). Extended 503s can cause deindexation. Fight: CDN/DDoS protection, load balancing, origin shielding — not something you handle on origin hosting.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Set up new + lost backlink alerts with a sensible email cadence
- [ ] Learn your normal daily referring-domain baseline; investigate spikes
- [ ] Check the anchor-text distribution for unnatural exact-match concentration
- [ ] Deploy self-referencing canonicals site-wide (parameter attack inoculation)
- [ ] Watch indexed-page counts for unexplained spikes
- [ ] Monitor Search Console Security Issues; keep CMS/plugins patched
- [ ] Search your brand + "reviews" monthly from key locations
- [ ] Put the site behind a CDN with DDoS mitigation
- [ ] Only disavow when drops coincide with spam you didn't build

## Data points & benchmarks
- In one example, a widely used free tool attracted links from 1M+ spam pages yet still earned ~133K organic visits/month — spam devaluation works.
- In one reported example, spam links sold at ~40,000 links per dollar (60M for $1,475) — attacks are cheap, which is why they're common.
- Penguin 4.0 (2016): link spam devalued in real time, granularity finer than site-level.
- A Google representative once said they had reviewed hundreds of "negative SEO" cases and none were the actual cause of the drop (usually algorithm updates or site issues).
- Search engines process a maximum of 32 words per quoted search when hunting scrapers.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Blaming negative SEO for traffic drops before ruling out algorithm updates and your own site issues.
- Disavowing indiscriminately — wrong disavows do more harm than the spam.
- Clicking suspicious linking sites while investigating (security risk).
- Reporting genuine negative reviews instead of responding to them.
- Skipping prevention (canonicals, patching, CDN) — every defense here is cheaper than recovery.

## Key principles
- Assume incompetence before malice: most ranking drops are self-inflicted or algorithmic.
- Monitoring (links, index counts, security, brand SERPs) is the entire detection strategy.
- Prevention beats remediation for hacks and DDoS; devaluation handles most link spam for you.

## ARTICLE nofollow-links

### Nofollow vs. Follow Links: Index Creep & Link Control

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Nofollow (rel="nofollow") tells search engines not to vouch for a link or pass PageRank/anchor text through it — used originally against comment spam, now also for paid/affiliate links and untrusted content. Internal links should essentially never be nofollowed; paid and user-generated links always. A natural profile mixes both: roughly 60-90% followed links is normal.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**What it is:** `<a href="…" rel="nofollow">` — identical UX to followed links, visible only in HTML. A robots meta tag of `nofollow` applies to every link on the page.

**History that explains today's rules:**
1. 2005 — introduced to kill comment spam (no ranking credit for links in comments/trackbacks).
2. 2009 — Google ended "PageRank sculpting": PageRank now divides across ALL outbound links, nofollowed or not, so nofollowing internal links no longer channels more equity elsewhere. (Remaining legitimate use: crawl prioritization on links like "login"/"register".)
3. Paid-link enforcement — paid/sponsored links have long had to be qualified; since 2019 the modern practice is rel="sponsored" for paid, rel="ugc" for user-generated content, and nofollow as the general case (all three are treated as hints, not directives).

**Why nofollow links still matter indirectly:**
1. Profile diversity — an all-followed profile looks manipulated.
2. Traffic — nofollowed placements (Q&A sites, forums, communities) get content seen, which earns followed links downstream.
3. Penalty protection — properly disclosing paid links keeps you safe from manual actions (and competitor reports).

**5-step audit:**
1. Followed backlinks with keyword-rich anchors → investigate for paid/manipulated links (remove, nofollow request, or disavow).
2. Followed backlinks from sponsored posts → request nofollow.
3. Keyword-rich followed outbound links on YOUR site (guest posts, UGC) → nofollow or remove; big publishers have gone site-wide nofollow for the same reason.
4. Sponsored posts on your site with followed links → add nofollow (search `site:yourdomain "sponsored post"`).
5. Nofollowed internal links → remove nofollow except for truly unimportant pages (login, register).

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] All paid, sponsored, and affiliate links nofollowed (or rel=sponsored)
- [ ] Guest post bio links use branded anchors or nofollow
- [ ] Comment/UGC links nofollowed (CMS default — verify)
- [ ] Widget/sitewide outbound links nofollowed
- [ ] No unexplained nofollowed internal links between valuable pages
- [ ] Followed vs nofollowed ratio checked (roughly 60-90% followed is normal)
- [ ] Anchor-text distribution scanned for keyword-rich followed anchors (sort by commercial intent to surface spammy ones)

## Data points & benchmarks
- ~85% of referring domains to one large SEO blog are followed; 60-90% followed is the typical healthy band.
- In an older study of ~44,600 SERPs, total backlinks correlated with rankings slightly more strongly than followed backlinks alone — weak evidence that some nofollowed links may count.
- PageRank division: with 10 links, 5 nofollowed, each followed link still passes only 1/10 (not 1/5) — sculpting is dead.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Nofollowing internal links to "concentrate" PageRank — it just discards equity and can orphan pages.
- 100% followed backlink profiles — a manipulation tell.
- Buying followed links and relying on Google not noticing — competitors can report you.
- Disavowing exact-match anchors without investigating context — natural exact-match links exist.

## Key principles
- Nofollow = "I don't vouch for this link." Use it for anything paid, untrusted, or user-generated.
- Treat nofollow as a hint: search engines may still crawl or count the link.
- When link building, prioritize followed placements — but never dismiss high-traffic nofollowed ones (referral traffic converts into followed links).

## ARTICLE orphan-pages

### How to Find and Fix Orphan Pages

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Orphan pages have no internal links pointing to them, so crawlers and users struggle to find them and they receive no link equity. Find them by cross-referencing crawlable URLs against URLs that actually receive visits (logs, analytics, Search Console), then fix each one deliberately — link, noindex, merge, or delete — instead of blindly linking everything.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Finding orphans (3 steps):**
1. Collect crawlable URLs — run a site crawler with sitemap AND external backlinks as URL sources (backlink data surfaces unlinked pages a plain crawl misses).
2. Collect URLs with hits — server log files (most accurate), analytics exports (page paths + views), and Search Console landing pages (the Search Console UI export is capped at 1,000 rows — use the API or a connector for more). Combine sources, deduplicate.
3. Cross-reference — URLs with hits that never appeared in the crawl are orphans. (In a crawler export, URLs with zero incoming links are also orphan candidates.)

**Fixing orphans — decision tree per page:**
1. Intentional orphan (e.g., ad landing page)? → Keep unlinked, add noindex (must stay crawlable in robots.txt or the noindex is never seen).
2. Valuable page missed in migration/redesign? → Add internal links from relevant, high-traffic pages (crawled often, so changes get picked up fast).
3. Duplicates/similar content elsewhere? → Merge unique value into the canonical page, then 301 the orphan to it.
4. No value at all (test/dev pages)? → Delete; 404/410 drops it from results naturally. If it has backlinks, redirect to the closest relevant page to preserve equity.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Crawl includes backlinks + sitemap as URL sources
- [ ] Logs/analytics/Search Console URL exports combined and deduped
- [ ] Cross-reference run to isolate true orphans
- [ ] Each orphan classified: intentional / valuable / duplicate / worthless
- [ ] Backlinks checked in bulk on orphan URLs before deleting
- [ ] Noindexed pages kept crawlable (not blocked in robots.txt)
- [ ] Migration/redesign checklists include internal link updates
- [ ] CMS/template structure auto-links new content (archives, categories)
- [ ] Discontinued products: links removed + 404/410, or page kept with explanation if it has strong backlinks
- [ ] Monthly scheduled audits to catch new orphans

## Data points & benchmarks
- Search Console UI exports cap at 1,000 rows per report — use the API or a dashboard connector to pull more.
- Search engines' primary discovery method is following links from pages they already know.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Bulk-adding internal links to every orphan without judging intent/value.
- Blocking noindexed pages in robots.txt — search engines can't see the noindex, so the URL may still get indexed.
- Deleting an orphan that has backlinks without redirecting it — wasted link equity.
- Removing discontinued products but leaving internal links to them (a top cause of orphans on ecommerce sites).

## Key principles
- No internal links = no PageRank flow and poor discoverability, even if the URL is in a sitemap.
- More URL sources = more orphans found; sitemap+backlink crawls only find orphans that are in sitemaps or have backlinks.
- Prevention beats cleanup: process-driven migrations and auto-linking site structures stop orphans forming.

## ARTICLE pagespeed-insights

### Page Speed Reports for SEOs & Developers (Field + Lab Data)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
The standard free page-speed report combines two data types: field data (real-user experience, 28-day rolling average, 75th percentile — what rankings use) and lab data (a controlled Lighthouse-style test that pinpoints causes). Use field data to decide what to fix, lab data to diagnose and verify fixes — and never confuse the performance score with Core Web Vitals pass/fail.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Reading the report:**
1. **Field data (real users)** — Core Web Vitals pass/fail per URL or whole origin; a metric is "good" only if 75% of experiences hit the threshold. Also shows FCP, INP, TTFB distributions.
2. **Lab data (simulated test)** — 0-100 performance score plus metrics. No FID/INP (needs real input); use Total Blocking Time (TBT) as the lab proxy.
3. **Diagnostics** — Opportunities/Diagnostics sections identify the LCP element, CLS culprits, and main-thread blockers; filter issues by target metric; treemap view exposes unused code bytes.

**Thresholds (good / needs improvement / poor):**
- LCP: ≤2.5s / 2.5-4s / >4s
- INP: ≤200ms / 200-500ms / >500ms (replaced the retired FID metric in March 2024)
- CLS: ≤0.1 / 0.1-0.25 / >0.25
- FCP: ≤1.8s / 1.8-3s / >3s; TTFB: ≤0.8s / 0.8-1.8s / >1.8s

**Workflow:**
1. Monitor at scale (bulk API pulls or crawler integrations) + Search Console CWV report, which buckets URLs by template.
2. Fix the template once — resolves every page in the bucket.
3. Use report diagnostics per failing template to target the LCP image, CLS elements, or long tasks.
4. Verify with lab tests — field data lags 28 days.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Analyze mobile first (usually worse than desktop)
- [ ] Check origin-level field data if URL-level data is missing (low traffic)
- [ ] Run 3-5 lab tests per page and average — scores fluctuate with network/load
- [ ] Target the reported LCP element, CLS shifters, and main-thread blockers
- [ ] Use TBT in lab tests as the INP proxy
- [ ] Don't chase every passed audit — spend time on failing metrics
- [ ] Re-test lab metrics after fixes; wait out the 28-day field window before judging CWV status
- [ ] Pull data in bulk via the API (or crawler integration) for site-wide prioritization

## Data points & benchmarks
- In older data, only 2% of tested pages scored 100; a 50 performance score was in the top 25% of tested pages.
- Score bands: 90-100 good, 50-89 needs improvement, 0-49 poor.
- Field dataset covers ~29.5M origins (April 2023) — opted-in browser users only.
- Performance score weights differ between mobile and desktop; lab tests emulate a mid-range mobile device.
- Estimated savings shown per opportunity are optimistic — fixing one issue may yield nothing if other blockers remain.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Treating a good lab score as passing Core Web Vitals — they're different measurements.
- Comparing single lab runs; always average several.
- Expecting the estimated time savings per fix to materialize exactly.
- Checking only desktop; mobile is the default index and usually the weak spot.
- Judging fixes by field data immediately — it's a 28-day rolling average.

## Key principles
- Field data decides whether you pass; lab data tells you why you fail.
- Fix templates, not URLs — bucketed reports turn one fix into thousands.
- The performance score is a weighted composite, not a ranking input; the CWV metrics are.

## ARTICLE react-seo

### React SEO: Best Practices to Make It SEO-Friendly

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
React (and similar component libraries) default to client-side rendering (CSR) inside an app shell — content lives in the DOM, not the HTML source. Google renders JavaScript well now, but other engines and most crawlers don't, and CSR hurts first-load speed. Use SSR/SSG (e.g., via a framework like Next.js), serve correct status codes, avoid hashed URLs, keep real `<a href>` links, and don't inject essential HTML on interaction.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**How the pipeline works:** crawl (GET, save HTML/JS/CSS) → process (queue links from `<a href>`, resource URLs; stop at noindex) → render (headless Chromium executes JS, ~5s median queue) → index. Content only in the rendered DOM is at the mercy of the render step.

**The 6 best practices:**
1. **Pick the right rendering strategy** — prefer SSR (render on request) or SSG (pre-render at build; faster TTFB) over CSR: all crawlers see content, initial loads are faster, TTI improves. Frameworks let you mix (SSG for blog, SSR for account pages). Full hydration still hurts initial TTI; SSR/SSG wins for now. Also use built-in image/font/script optimization and dynamic imports.
2. **Use status codes correctly** — SPAs return 200 for everything (soft 404s) and use JS redirects instead of 3xx. If stuck on CSR: router framework + 404 component + noindex on not-found pages + visible "404" H1; JS redirects are followed but not ideal. SSR frameworks expose proper status-code helpers.
3. **Avoid hashed URLs** (#/path) — engines ignore the fragment; every hash route collapses to one URL. Use the History API for clean client-side URLs.
4. **Use `<a href>` links** — crawlers discover URLs from anchor elements; div/button navigation is invisible. Exception: deliberately hiding combinatorial faceted links from crawlers can conserve crawl budget.
5. **Don't lazy-load essential HTML** — content injected on click/hover (accordions, modals, tabs, mega/hamburger menus) is invisible until interaction. Audit those elements; ship content in HTML and toggle visibility with CSS instead.
6. **Keep the fundamentals** — canonicals, structured data, sitemaps, mobile-first, HTTPS, titles, semantic HTML all still apply.

**Audit technique:** compare raw HTML (view-source) vs rendered DOM (inspect); crawlers that store both make it easy to spot client-side-rendered titles, H1s, and content.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Choose SSR/SSG for indexable pages; reserve CSR for app-like areas
- [ ] Verify raw HTML contains titles, meta, canonicals, H1, primary content
- [ ] Return real 404/410 and 301/302 statuses (test with a bulk status checker)
- [ ] Replace #/ routes with History API URLs
- [ ] Inspect nav components for `<a href>` (not onClick divs)
- [ ] Check menus/modals/tabs for DOM injection on interaction; convert to CSS show/hide
- [ ] Enable framework image/font/script optimizations and code splitting
- [ ] Run CWV checks on both lab and field data — SPA initial loads are the weak point

## Data points & benchmarks
- Median crawl-to-render delay: ~5 seconds in one older study (reportedly up to a week before 2019).
- The rendering browser is evergreen Chromium — modern JS features render fine on Google; other engines (and most third-party crawlers) offer limited or no rendering.
- CSR apps can show positive field CWV for multi-page visits (client-side routing + diffing makes subsequent views fast) but fail on low pages-per-visit traffic.
- One audited major ecommerce brand had its entire mobile filter navigation injected on click — invisible to engines.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Assuming "Google renders JS" covers every crawler, tool, and AI retriever — it doesn't.
- Hash-based routing collapsing the site to one URL.
- Div/button navigation with no anchor tags — no discovery, no PageRank flow.
- Relying on full hydration for critical pages today (TTI penalty on first render).
- Soft 404s from always-200 SPA responses polluting the index.

## Key principles
- React is a tool, not an SEO verdict — configuration decides whether it helps or hurts.
- Content in the initial HTML is the only universally safe content.
- Real anchors and real status codes keep crawlers and audits accurate.

## ARTICLE redirects-for-seo

### 11 Types Of Redirects & Their SEO Impact

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Redirects forward users/bots from old URLs to new ones and are a canonicalization signal. Prefer server-side 301/308 for permanent moves (signals consolidate forward) and 302/307 for temporary ones (signals usually stay on the original URL). Avoid loops, overly broad rules, and expired SSL certificates on old domains.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Redirect taxonomy:**
- Permanent: 301, 308, meta refresh (=0s), HTTP refresh (=0s), JavaScript, crypto redirect
- Temporary: 302, 303, 307, meta refresh (>0s), HTTP refresh (>0s)
- Preferred permanent order: 308/301 > meta refresh 0/HTTP refresh 0 > JavaScript > crypto
- Preferred temporary order: 307/302/303 > meta refresh/HTTP refresh
- Implementation locations: server-level (Apache .htaccess, Nginx, IIS, CMS plugins), DNS-level, CDN-level (edge rules/workers), HTML, HTTP headers

**Key behavioral rules:**
1. Permanent redirects = strong canonicalization hint → link signals consolidate to the NEW URL (a page with 10 backlinks redirects → new page benefits as if links pointed at it directly).
2. Irrelevant redirects (e.g., blog post → homepage) get treated as soft 404s; signals do NOT consolidate.
3. Temporary redirects = weak hint → signals usually stay on the ORIGINAL URL, but Google may swap to treating them as permanent after days/weeks/months (faster when target is an established page).
4. Verify treatment: Search Console URL Inspection on the old URL ("not on Google" = treated as permanent); or Search Console Links report to see where link signals consolidated.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] HTTP→HTTPS redirect returns 301/308 (check with a free SEO toolbar; note HSTS sites show browser-cached 307 — check the server response in incognito)
- [ ] Implement HSTS + consider HSTS preload submission
- [ ] Redirect old URLs with backlinks: in a backlink tool, list your most-linked pages that return 404 → sort by referring domains → 301 to relevant current pages
- [ ] Keep redirect chains under 5 hops (Googlebot follows max 10; 5 per session)
- [ ] Fix redirect loops (remove the looping redirect; point to true final destination; update internal links)
- [ ] Match redirects 1:1 — avoid broad folder-level catch-alls (test with domain.com/random-garbage → should 404, not redirect)
- [ ] Renew/multi-domain SSL certs on old migrated domains (bots pass but users get errors)
- [ ] Keep redirects live at least 1 year (Google guidance), and indefinitely while the old URL still earns links or traffic
- [ ] During migration, redirect indexed files (images, PDFs) too — skip JS/CSS/fonts
- [ ] Update internal links to point directly at final URLs, not through redirects

## Data points & benchmarks
- Googlebot follows up to 10 redirect hops (5 per session); one practitioner's practical cutoff: fix chains >5 hops.
- Keep redirects at least 1 year for permanent signal transfer (per Google guidance).
- ~two-thirds of links on the web disappeared over a 9-year period (an older link-rot study) — old unredirected URLs lose real value.
- Google treats irrelevant redirects as soft 404s.
- Temporary redirects can be reinterpreted as permanent in days to months; international 302 homepage setups can stay "temporary" for years.
- 308 = 301 but preserves HTTP method (GET/POST); 307 = 302 with method preserved; 303 is form-resubmission prevention, treated like 302/307.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Using client-side (JS/meta refresh) redirects when server-side is possible — rendering can fail.
- Redirecting everything to the homepage — treated as soft 404, link signals lost.
- Overly broad redirect rules — can accidentally claim hacked/spam pages' links as yours.
- Letting old-domain SSL certs expire after migration — users hit errors, no redirect.
- Obsessing over 1 extra hop — chains <5 hops are fine.
- Trusting status codes blindly — a 200 OK page can still be redirecting.
- Removing redirects before 1 year or before checking whether users still hit old URLs.

## Key principles
- "Redirects are important for SEO" — they combine signals between pages and drive canonicalization.
- Permanent = consolidate forward; temporary = usually consolidate backward.
- Redirects are like postal mail forwarding for the web.
- Match redirects 1:1 and keep them as long as possible, ideally forever.

### Pagination SEO: Getting Paginated Series Right

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Google stopped using rel=prev/next years before announcing it in 2019 — leave existing markup in place (it still has accessibility uses, and Bing has said it may use it as a hint). The acceptable implementations are self-referencing canonicals per page, or canonicalizing to a view-all page. The four ways sites break pagination: canonicalizing to page one, noindexing, nofollowing internal links, and blocking crawling — all orphan content and cut PageRank flow.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**If you already have rel=prev/next + self-referencing canonicals:** change nothing. Treat each paginated page as a normal indexable page and keep internal links between pages in the series.

**Acceptable implementations:**
1. Each page self-canonical, crawlable, indexable, interlinked within the series.
2. Paginated pages canonicalized to a "view-all" page containing the complete content (users still get pagination; the index gets the full page).

**If you believe paginated pages are low-value:** replace the pagination crawl path with topic-grouped category pages linked from the homepage — categories can rank for their topics. Note this usually requires a redesign, and you may still need pagination within categories.

**How Google identifies a series without rel=prev/next:** consistent headings, titles (same or with page numbers), and internal links between pages in the set.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Each paginated page has a self-referencing canonical (or all point to view-all)
- [ ] No canonicals from page 2+ pointing to page 1
- [ ] No noindex on paginated pages (check meta robots AND X-Robots-Tag headers)
- [ ] No nofollow on internal links between paginated pages
- [ ] No robots.txt Disallow on pagination paths (e.g., /blog/page/)
- [ ] Existing rel=prev/next markup left in place
- [ ] Internal links connect the pages in each series
- [ ] Category/topic hub pages linked from the homepage where pagination is replaced

## Data points & benchmarks
- Google publicly confirmed in 2019 that it had ignored rel=prev/next for years; Bing has said it may still use it as a hint (older statement).
- Nofollow became a "hint" in 2019 — best case it's ignored, worst case it throttles crawling and signal flow.
- Long-standing guidance for paginated duplicate blocks: "do nothing" — repeated content across a series triggers no penalty.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Canonicalizing every page in a series to page one — orphans everything after page 1 and cuts PageRank (only hope is Google ignoring the tag).
- Noindexing paginated pages — they drop from the index and their links eventually stop being followed/crawled.
- Nofollowing pagination links "to save crawl budget" — same orphaning effect.
- Blocking pagination paths in robots.txt — content becomes undiscoverable.

## Key principles
- Paginated pages are normal indexable pages: crawlable, followable, self-canonical.
- Removing working markup out of panic after a search engine announcement usually does more harm than the announcement itself.
- Alternate crawl paths (categories, sitemaps) are the safety net for any pagination strategy.

## ARTICLE remove-urls-from-google

### How to Remove URLs From Google Search (5 Methods)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Pick the removal method that matches the goal: delete (404/410) to remove, noindex to remove but keep the page, access restriction to hide from engines entirely, the removals tool for urgent temporary hiding (PII/security), canonicalization/redirects to consolidate duplicates. Wrong methods (robots.txt blocks, nofollow, noindex-in-robots.txt) don't deindex anything — and can block signal consolidation.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**First, verify indexing correctly:** `site:` searches show pages that are merely KNOWN (including redirected/canonicalized URLs) — not necessarily indexed. Use the Search Console Page indexing report or the URL Inspection tool.

**The 5 methods:**
1. **Delete the content** — serve 404/410; drops from the index after recrawl. Cached copies may linger briefly.
2. **Noindex** (meta tag or X-Robots-Tag header) — page stays live for users, gets removed from the index; must stay crawlable (not robots.txt-blocked).
3. **Restrict access** — login, HTTP authentication, or IP allowlist; engines can't access at all. Best for staging/internal/member content.
4. **Removals tool** — hides the URL temporarily (~6 months; another major engine's tool lasts ~3 months), processes in about a day. For emergencies (PII, leaks). Always pair with a permanent fix.
5. **Canonicalization** — canonical tag for near-duplicates; 301 redirect to consolidate signals to a new URL (302 = keep original indexed). Google's URL Parameters tool was removed in 2022, so handle parameters with canonicals and clean internal links.

**Priority order when removing many pages:** high = security/PII/confidential; medium = member-only, staging, internal; low = parameter/duplicate variants.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Confirmed actual indexing via Search Console (not site: search)
- [ ] Method matched to goal (remove vs hide vs consolidate)
- [ ] Noindexed pages crawlable and in sitemap until deindexed
- [ ] Emergency removals paired with a permanent method
- [ ] 301s used when link equity should move; 404/410 when not
- [ ] Images removed via robots.txt disallow of the image crawler (works for images specifically)
- [ ] Old-domain URLs left to consolidate during migrations (don't panic-remove)

## Data points & benchmarks
- A robots.txt `noindex` line was never official; Google informally honored it until September 1, 2019 and now ignores it.
- Removals tool effects last ~6 months; requests process within ~1 day.
- Blocked-but-linked pages can still be indexed with a generated title built from anchor text.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Blocking URLs in robots.txt expecting deindexing — blocks crawling, not indexing.
- nofollow as a removal mechanism — it's a link hint, not an index control.
- noindex + canonical to another URL on the same page — conflicting signals; consolidation may fail.
- noindex-then-block sequencing — the final blocked state can leave pages indexed indefinitely.
- Removing old-domain URLs mid-migration because site: still shows them — you'd sever signal consolidation.

## Key principles
- Crawling ≠ indexing: a page can be indexed without being crawled (Google infers from links).
- Deindexing that must preserve signals uses redirects/canonicals; deindexing that must destroy access uses deletion/auth.
- For content you don't own: copyright takedowns (DMCA) and, in the EU, privacy-based removal requests are the levers.

## ARTICLE robots-txt

### Robots.txt and SEO: Everything You Need to Know

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Robots.txt controls crawling (not indexing) — one misplaced character can block your whole site from Google. Use User-agent + Disallow/Allow directives, keep rules specific, and never use robots.txt to deindex: use meta robots/x-robots-tag instead (a blocked page can't be seen to read its noindex).

## Framework / Step-by-step
**File basics:**
- Located at domain.com/robots.txt (root of each subdomain; separate file per subdomain)
- Format: `Sitemap:` line + `User-agent: [bot]` blocks with `Disallow:`/`Allow:` directives
- Supported by Google: Disallow, Allow, Sitemap. NOT supported: Crawl-delay (some other crawlers, such as Bing's, honor it), Noindex (informally honored by Google until Sept 1, 2019; now ignored), Nofollow (unsupported)
- User-agent names are case-insensitive, but paths in Disallow/Allow are case-sensitive (`/Photos/` ≠ `/photos/`); `*` = all bots; crawlers follow only the most specific matching user-agent block; new user-agent declaration = clean slate
- Conflicting rules (Google/Bing): longest directive (most characters) wins; if tied, least restrictive (Allow) wins. Other engines follow first matching directive.

**Core principle:** robots.txt prevents crawling, NOT indexing. Blocked pages can still appear in search results if linked externally. To exclude from index: use meta robots `noindex` or X-Robots-Tag — and the page must be crawlable for Google to see them.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Check domain.com/robots.txt exists and review all directives
- [ ] Verify no `Disallow: /` left from staging
- [ ] Use trailing slashes for folder blocks: `Disallow: /de/` not `/de` (avoids blocking /designer-dresses/, /delivery/ etc.)
- [ ] One directive per line; each user-agent declared once
- [ ] Use `*` wildcards for patterns (`Disallow: /products/*?`) and `$` for URL endings (`Disallow: /*.pdf$`)
- [ ] Add `Sitemap:` directive (helps other engines discover it; redundant but harmless for Google if submitted in Search Console)
- [ ] Add `#` comments explaining rules for humans
- [ ] Regularly check the Search Console Page indexing report for URLs blocked by robots.txt
- [ ] Use the robots.txt report (Search Console → Settings) to confirm Google fetched the file and see parse errors; use URL Inspection to check whether a specific URL is blocked (the old robots.txt Tester was retired in Dec 2023)
- [ ] If deindexing: remove robots block + apply noindex tag

## Data points & benchmarks
- Max robots.txt size: 500 KiB (Google).
- Crawl-delay of 5s caps a bot at ~17,280 URLs/day — dangerous for large sites.
- Google stopped honoring unofficial robots.txt rules such as noindex on Sept 1, 2019.
- Key user-agents: Googlebot, Googlebot-Image, Bingbot, Slurp (Yahoo), Baiduspider, DuckDuckBot.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Blocking URLs in robots.txt to remove them from Google — they can stay indexed ("Indexed, though blocked by robots.txt").
- Disallowing noindexed pages — Google can't crawl to see the noindex (per Gary Illyes).
- `Disallow: /de` without trailing slash — blocks every URL starting with "/de".
- Submitting sitemap URLs that are robots-blocked (Search Console "Submitted URL blocked by robots.txt" error).
- Setting crawl-delay for Google (ignored) or too high for Bing on large sites.
- Multiple directives on one line; re-declaring the same user-agent repeatedly (confusing).

## Key principles
- "Just one character out of place can wreak havoc on your SEO."
- Robots.txt = crawling control; meta robots/x-robots-tag = indexing control.
- Bots follow the most specific applicable user-agent rules — specificity overrides `*`.
- Longest directive wins in Google/Bing conflicts; first match wins elsewhere.
- Keep it simple and commented: fewer rules = fewer catastrophic mistakes.

## ARTICLE semantic-seo

### Semantic SEO: The Advanced Skill Most SEOs Pretend to Understand

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Semantic SEO is not a different kind of SEO — it's SEO done properly: optimizing for meaning so machines represent your brand accurately, not just rank it. It sits at the overlap of brand (machines understand you), content (you're trusted on the right topics), and technical (everything is machine-friendly). The goal shifts from *if* you appear to *how* you appear — including inside AI-generated answers.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**How retrieval works:** lexical search matches character strings; semantic search infers meaning and relationships; hybrid (lexical base + semantic reranking) is what major engines use. LLMs are almost purely semantic (embeddings, vector similarity). Full semantic search engines are unlikely: cost, exact-match demand, and reliability.

**The 10 expert practices:**
1. **Codify the brand** — a universal brand guide (correct names, short forms, logo files, media kit) so thousands of touchpoints and third parties describe you the same way. LLMs learn your brand from what others say.
2. **Connect the brand to attributes people care about** — pick the one attribute you want to own ("value for money", "innovative"), then prove it with indisputable data woven into homepage, service pages, pricing docs, listings, ads, and titles.
3. **Fix "alphabet soup" URLs** — replace CMS-generated gibberish slugs with topic-based slugs derived from each page's top keyword/entities, with 301 redirects; prioritize by traffic and keyword volume.
4. **Design semantic information architecture** — use the EAV model (Entity = listing, Attribute = searchable feature, Value = keyword-derived option) across collections, taxonomies, filters, navigation, and microcopy.
5. **Add information gain** — don't reverse-engineer and remix the top 10; contribute something new to the conversation.
6. **Close page-level topic gaps** — mine keywords the page used to rank for (especially pairs the content never connected), then merge those concepts explicitly in headings and sections.
7. **Build brand-relevant topical authority** — coverage depth matters only where it overlaps brand relevance; skip banal tangents ("what is a shoe?" for a shoe brand) and check SERP similarity before grouping keywords.
8. **Keep schema and semantic HTML clean** — schema translates context (Organization → Service → audience → geography); semantic HTML tags structure content. Never signal things that don't exist; polluted datasets get ignored. Schema is an add-on, not a prerequisite for entity recognition.
9. **Manage link/mention sentiment** — semantic systems read context and sentiment around mentions, so digital PR and reputation management are now part of link building.
10. **Optimize every local entity** — map the full entity web of a local business (property types, materials, conditions solved, staff credentials, research contributions) and build genuinely useful, unique landing pages around it (not near-duplicate templated pages).

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Publish a brand guide + media kit; enforce consistent self-reference and third-party descriptions
- [ ] Choose one attribute to own; back it with verifiable data and repeat it across every surface
- [ ] Audit for meaningless URLs; rewrite top-traffic pages first with 301s
- [ ] Map EAV for your listings; extend attributes into categories, filters, and navigation labels
- [ ] Compare your content vs top rankers for topic gaps; add unique data or perspectives
- [ ] Check historical keyword declines for unconnected concept pairs; merge them on-page
- [ ] Validate schema each deploy; use semantic HTML elements and proper heading/table/list markup
- [ ] For local: build the full entity map and expand pages beyond service × location

## Data points & benchmarks
- Named by experts in one poll of 100+ SEO practitioners as a top advanced SEO skill.
- Aged care case (one reported example; government data): #1 of 238 local facilities, top 1.26% citywide for resident experience, 50% more floor space vs 450 alternatives, up to 33% cheaper vs 148 competitors → became LLMs' #1 "value for money" recommendation without ranking organically or using that phrase on-site.
- One B2B ecommerce client reportedly grew organic visibility year-over-year for 4+ years with no algorithm-update losses after a semantic architecture rebuild — an anecdote, not a benchmark.
- "product design software" vs "product design tools": near-identical lexically, different intents (physical vs digital design) — one brand shouldn't target both.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Treating topical authority as "cover everything remotely related" — that's the content-spam recipe.
- Copying the top 10 and adding "10% originality" — keyword stuffing with extra steps.
- Using schema to claim things that don't exist; messy data gets excluded from knowledge graphs.
- Ignoring sentiment around mentions — negative-context links can hurt how machines describe you.
- Assuming LLM visibility requires ranking first — entity clarity and third-party consistency can win AI mentions pre-ranking.

## Key principles
- Search engines speak code; semantic SEO translates your meaning into their language.
- Hybrid retrieval (lexical + semantic) means keyword optimization stays the baseline; semantic work enhances it.
- Brand names have no inherent meaning — attributes and third-party consistency create it.
- How you're represented (accuracy, sentiment, authority) now matters as much as whether you rank.

## ARTICLE seo-for-static-sites

### SEO for Static Sites: 8 Problems to Watch For

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Static site generators (and AI-"vibe-coded" sites) remove the CMS safety net: canonicals, sitemaps, redirects, metadata, schema, and 404 handling all become your job. Going static doesn't remove SEO work — it moves it from plugins/defaults to your build process. Audit for eight recurring failure modes.

## Framework / Step-by-step
The 8 problems and their fixes:
1. **Trailing slash & canonical conflicts** — framework and host may disagree on /page vs /page/, splitting signals. Fix: pick one format, align framework, host, canonicals, and internal links; crawl for conflicts.
2. **Robots.txt / sitemap / noindex conflicts** — code-generated sitemaps don't know which pages are noindex, so drafts and thank-you pages leak into search. Fix: one rule (e.g., a noindex flag) that both the sitemap and RSS feed respect; verify robots.txt exists and points to the right sitemap.
3. **Image optimization gaps** — images added outside the framework's pipeline bypass resizing/compression (some frameworks copy files raw by default). Fix: always use the framework's image component (ideally outputting WebP/AVIF with fallbacks); check for oversized files and missing alt text before publish.
4. **Template-driven metadata & schema errors** — one bad default in a shared template replicates to every page using it (e.g., auto-appended brand name overflows titles; wrong schema type). Fix: define metadata/schema requirements per page type before building; test multiple pages after any template change; validate markup.
5. **Incorrect HTTP status codes** — missing pages can return 200 OK with a "404-looking" page (soft 404s) when SPA fallback files are served for unknown routes. Fix: configure real 404s; request known-bad URLs and check the actual status code.
6. **Internal linking inconsistencies** — different templates/components emit mixed URL formats (with/without slash, relative vs absolute, case). Fix: standardize www, slash, absolute/relative, HTTPS, lowercase, extensions, hyphens, parameters, index pages; centralize in one link helper.
7. **Technical performance** — static generation ≠ fast; excess JavaScript, sitewide CSS, heavy third-party scripts, poor font/image loading still hurt. Fix: code splitting, critical CSS, right rendering strategy per page, defer non-essential scripts, CDN caching; test the built site, not assumptions.
8. **Migration pitfalls** — moving CMS → static silently drops redirects, sitemap rules, metadata logic, schema, and link behavior. Fix: treat it as a full migration — map every old URL, 301 all changes, diff old/new canonicals + metadata + schema + sitemaps, crawl both versions.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Verify /page and /page/ resolve to one canonical version site-wide
- [ ] Exclude noindex pages from sitemap AND RSS/feed logic; recheck when adding page types
- [ ] Confirm robots.txt exists, references the correct sitemap, and doesn't block wanted sections
- [ ] Route every image through the framework's optimization pipeline; audit for oversized files and blank alt text
- [ ] Test metadata/schema templates across several pages, not one example
- [ ] Request fake URLs and confirm they return real 404 status codes (not 200)
- [ ] Standardize one URL format in a shared link component (slash, case, scheme, www, hyphens)
- [ ] Audit JS/CSS weight, third-party scripts, and font loading on the live build
- [ ] Before any CMS→static migration: full URL map, 301s, canonical/metadata/schema diff, old+new crawls

## Data points & benchmarks
- A comprehensive site crawl (100+ technical checks) is a sensible scope for a static-site audit.
- In one blog's framework migration, a single misconfigured internal-linking rule nofollowed ALL internal links — one config error can sitewide-neutralize internal linking.
- One international blog migration (subfolder → subfolder) confirmed: URL changes without 301s lose links and ranking signals.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Assuming the static build is fast without testing — static HTML can still ship heavy JS/CSS.
- Trusting a preview that "looks fine" — oversized images, 200-status 404s, and template errors all survive previews.
- Letting AI-generated components pick their own URL formats and metadata conventions.
- Treating a CMS→static move as a redesign instead of a full SEO migration.

## Key principles
- Going static doesn't remove SEO work; it relocates it from CMS defaults to your build pipeline.
- One template decision scales everywhere — both an efficiency and a blast radius.
- No framework is "best for SEO"; evaluate on URL control, canonical/metadata generation, noindex + sitemap exclusion control, image tooling, schema validation, and redirect/404 effort.
- Small, infrequently updated sites suit static setups; complex publishing workflows may still need a CMS.

## ARTICLE seo-friendly-urls

### SEO-Friendly URLs: How to Build Them

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
The slug (the part after the domain) is the piece you control on every new page: reduce your title to the core keyword, lowercase it, hyphenate it. For the rest of the URL: HTTPS, brandable domain, .com-style TLD, subfolders for hierarchy, no dates, no parameters, no keyword stuffing. Rarely worth changing existing URLs.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Building the slug (new pages):**
1. Start with the page title.
2. Remove special characters (commas, colons, semicolons, quotes — unsafe or hard to auto-link).
3. Remove numbers (they go stale when the content changes; titles are easy to update, URLs are not).
4. Remove superfluous words — but keep enough to describe the content.
5. Boil it down to the keyword people actually search (check what similar ranking pages target).
6. Optionally add 1-2 keyword modifiers for context (e.g., "seo").
7. Optionally make it readable (conjunctions, plurals).
8. Lowercase everything (case-sensitivity risks duplicate URLs on some servers).
9. Replace spaces with hyphens (not underscores; hyphens are the official recommendation).

**Rest-of-URL best practices:**
- HTTPS everywhere.
- Subdomains only when truly needed — many practitioners report traffic gains moving subdomain content into subfolders, despite official statements of equivalence.
- Memorable, brandable domain over keyword-rich domain.
- Generic TLD (.com preferred); some TLDs carry spam associations that hurt link building. ccTLDs for single-country targeting.
- Subfolders create hierarchy and context (`store/earbuds/apple/airpods` beats `buy-apple-airpods-earbuds`).
- Use as many subfolders as needed — depth itself isn't a ranking problem, but buried pages usually are; audit clicks-from-homepage instead.
- Avoid keyword repetition across path segments.
- Avoid dates in URLs (updates make them contradictory); set permalinks before publishing on a new site.
- Avoid URL parameters on indexable pages; canonicalize parameter duplicates.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Slugs: lowercase, hyphenated, keyword-first, no numbers/special chars
- [ ] Permalink structure set before publishing (no dates) on new sites
- [ ] Subfolders used for logical hierarchy
- [ ] No unnecessary subdomains
- [ ] Parameter-driven duplicates canonicalized
- [ ] Site crawl checked for deep pages (click depth, not folder count)
- [ ] Existing URLs left alone unless there's a strong reason

## Data points & benchmarks
- Hyphens are the officially recommended word separator; Google doesn't treat underscores as word separators.
- URLs get truncated in search results when long — keep slugs short.
- Spaces in URLs become %20 — never allow them.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Renaming slugs on a whim — redirects are easy to forget and usually not worth it.
- Keyword-stuffed slugs and repeated keywords across subfolders.
- Trusting subfolder count as a depth metric — measure internal link depth instead.
- Assuming a keyword-rich domain ranks itself — it hasn't worked that way for a long time.

## Key principles
- Describe the page in a few words, hyphenate — that's 90% of slug optimization.
- URLs are permanent; titles are mutable. Never bake mutable details (numbers, dates) into URLs.
- Subfolders are the default; subdomains are the exception.

## ARTICLE seo-issues

### 10 SEO Issues That Really Matter & How to Fix Them

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Based on an older industry study of 1M+ domains: the most common issues (missing meta descriptions on 72.9% of sites) aren't the ones that matter. Fix these 10 instead: indexability problems, broken pages, weak internal linking, mobile issues, HTTPS issues, Core Web Vitals, deep site structure, missing schema, duplicate content, and oversized HTML (2 MB Googlebot limit).

## Framework / Step-by-step
The 10 issues that actually matter, in order:
1. **Indexability issues** — Page must be (a) crawlable, (b) not noindexed, (c) canonical. Watch: canonical points to 4xx, canonical points to redirect.
2. **Broken pages** — 4xx/5xx pages waste link equity, crawl budget, and UX. Use referring-domain counts to prioritize: restore page or 301 redirect.
3. **Few or no internal links** — Orphan pages get no PageRank. Add relevant internal links (an internal link opportunities report helps).
4. **Mobile experience issues** — Mobile-first indexing + Page Experience signals. Fix viewport tags, tap targets, plugin dependencies; check with Lighthouse/DevTools (Search Console's Mobile Usability report was retired in Dec 2023).
5. **HTTPS issues** — HTTPS is a (small) ranking signal. 301/308 redirect all HTTP→HTTPS; fix mixed content.
6. **Performance & stability (Core Web Vitals)** — Goal is "good" score, not fastest: LCP, INP (replaced FID in March 2024), CLS.
7. **Unoptimized site structure** — Rule of thumb: pages >6 clicks from homepage get diluted link equity. Flatten structure or add links from higher pages.
8. **Lack of relevant schema markup** — Not a ranking factor, but enables rich results (recipes, jobs, videos, events).
9. **Duplicate content** — Google picks one version, maybe not yours. Consolidate to one canonical version (HTTPS).
10. **Oversized HTML pages (>2 MB)** — Googlebot only reads the first 2 MB of uncompressed HTML; everything after is ignored.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Site crawler indexability check: fix canonicals pointing to 4xx/redirects; remove unwanted noindex
- [ ] Crawl for broken pages (4xx/5xx): fix (restore or 301); prioritize those with referring domains
- [ ] Find and fix orphan pages (link or delete)
- [ ] Use an internal link opportunities report to add contextual links
- [ ] Check mobile issues in Lighthouse/DevTools device mode (viewport, tap targets, legible font size ≥12px)
- [ ] All HTTP pages 301/308 → HTTPS; check mixed content
- [ ] Add `Content-Security-Policy: upgrade-insecure-requests` header to auto-upgrade HTTP resources
- [ ] Search Console Core Web Vitals report: get all three metrics to "good"
- [ ] Crawl depth report: important pages ≤6 clicks from home
- [ ] Generate schema (generator or AI assistant, reviewed manually) + validate it (Rich Results Test / Schema Markup Validator)
- [ ] Check site resolves at only ONE of http/https × www/non-www (others 301 to canonical host)
- [ ] Check HTML page size against Googlebot's documented crawl limit (2 MB; see the data points)

## Data points & benchmarks
- Missing meta descriptions found on 72.9% of studied sites — common but doesn't impact rankings.
- Canonical pointing to a broken page: only 2.6% of sites — rare but severe.
- Googlebot crawls only the first 2 MB of uncompressed HTML (confirmed Feb 2026 doc update).
- Pages >6 clicks from homepage = too deep; link equity diminishes with each hop.
- INP replaced FID as CWV interaction metric in March 2024.
- Mobile legible-font guideline: at least 12 px (Lighthouse).
- HTTPS: small ranking signal since 2014.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Chasing common-but-harmless issues (missing meta descriptions) while ignoring rare-but-severe ones (broken canonicals).
- Deleting broken pages with backlinks — that link equity is wasted; redirect instead.
- Assuming schema improves rankings — it only unlocks rich result eligibility.
- Letting pages bloat past 2 MB with inline base64 images, embedded SVGs/JSON, inline CSS/JS — browsers render fine but Googlebot silently stops reading.
- Putting business-irrelevant pages in main navigation just for SEO (one site deliberately kept its glossary out of main nav).

## Key principles
- "The ones you see the most aren't necessarily the ones you should be losing sleep over."
- For a page to rank it must be crawlable, not noindexed, and canonical — three hard requirements.
- Page experience is a tiebreaker between pages of similar content quality, not a trump card.
- Duplicate content: "we won't show all of these pages" (John Mueller) — Google picks, possibly wrongly, unless you consolidate.

## ARTICLE seo-meta-tags

### Meta Tags for SEO: The Six That Matter

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Six meta tags earn their place in the `<head>`: title, description, robots, viewport, charset, and meta refresh (which you should avoid using). Titles and robots carry real SEO weight; descriptions affect clicks, not rankings; viewport signals mobile-friendliness; charset prevents broken text; meta keywords is dead and should be removed.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**1. Meta title** — the primary snippet headline.
- Unique per page, descriptive, click-worthy (not clickbait), matches search intent, includes target keyword where natural, under ~60 characters.
- Four failure modes: too long/short, missing, multiple `<title>` tags on one page, duplicated across pages.

**2. Meta description** — the snippet pitch.
- Unique, accurate summary, under ~160 characters, keyword where natural.
- Not a ranking factor — but influences click-through. Expect rewrites.

**3. Meta robots** — controls indexing and link-following (not crawling): `index, noindex, follow, nofollow` in any combination; `none` = `noindex, nofollow`; absent tag = `index, follow`.
- Only set it when you actually want to restrict a page. Nofollow is treated as a hint.

**4. Meta viewport** — required for mobile-friendly rendering: `<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1.0">`.

**5. Meta charset** — declare `<meta charset="UTF-8">` (HTML5 syntax) so browsers don't guess and garble text.

**6. Meta refresh redirect** — avoid; use a 301 server redirect instead.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Every page has exactly one unique title (<60 chars) and description (<160 chars)
- [ ] Site crawl checks for missing/duplicate/multiple titles and descriptions
- [ ] Meta robots set only where restriction is intended — audit for rogue noindex/nofollow
- [ ] No noindexed pages also blocked in robots.txt (noindex becomes invisible)
- [ ] No noindexed pages listed in the sitemap (except temporarily while waiting for them to drop from the index)
- [ ] Viewport tag present on all pages (usually theme-level); verify in Lighthouse/Chrome DevTools device mode (Search Console's Mobile Usability report was retired in Dec 2023)
- [ ] UTF-8 charset declared site-wide
- [ ] Meta refresh redirects replaced with 301s (add a canonical if one must remain)
- [ ] Meta keywords tags removed sitewide (usually one line in the CMS template)

## Data points & benchmarks
- Title tags: keep under ~60 characters; meta descriptions: ~160 characters.
- Google has ignored the meta keywords tag since 2009; one major alternative search engine has stated it uses keyword stuffing as a spam signal.
- Google rewrites meta descriptions for the majority of queries — write for the user, not the snippet.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Stuffing meta keywords — pointless at best, a spam signal at worst.
- Using meta refresh redirects — inconsistent browser support, confusing for users, slower than server redirects.
- Assuming Google always shows your title/description — it often substitutes its own.
- Setting `noindex` then blocking the page in robots.txt — the directive can't be seen, and the URL may still appear in results.

## Key principles
- Titles are the highest-leverage meta tag: they influence both rankings (as content/relevance signals) and clicks.
- Meta descriptions = click optimization, not ranking optimization.
- The viewport tag is a prerequisite for a usable mobile layout; mobile-friendliness was a ranking signal from 2015 (historical), and mobile-first indexing is now universal.

## ARTICLE speed-up-wordpress

### How to Speed Up Your CMS Website in ~20 Minutes

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Many CMS sites (e.g., WordPress) can go from mediocre to much better speed scores in under half an hour with 11 settings-level changes: remove dead plugins/extensions, move DNS to a fast provider, enable caching, minify, conditionally combine files, defer render-blocking assets, lazy-load media, optimize web fonts, preload key resources, add a CDN, and compress images. Test everything on staging — minify/combine steps can break code.

1. **Remove unused plugins/extensions** — every idle add-on can add load; deactivate and uninstall anything nonessential (leave anything you're unsure about).
2. **Switch DNS providers** — registrar-default DNS is usually slow; point nameservers to a fast free DNS provider.
3. **Install a caching plugin** — enables server caching (pre-built static pages) + browser caching (repeat-visit files); enable mobile caching too.
4. **Minify CSS/JS** — strips whitespace/comments, shrinking files; test before deploying (JS especially can break).
5. **Combine CSS/JS files** — helps only on HTTP/1.1 (files load consecutively); pointless on HTTP/2+ (parallel loading). Check your protocol first.
6. **Eliminate render-blocking resources** — defer non-critical JS and optimize CSS delivery (inline critical CSS) so above-the-fold content paints first.
7. **Lazy-load images and videos** — images lazy-load natively in modern CMS versions; extend to videos/iframes via plugin settings.
8. **Optimize web fonts** — every font costs a CSS request + download; use fewer fonts/weights and set font-display (swap or optional) so text renders immediately.
9. **Enable preloading** — tell browsers which resources matter most (`<link rel="preload">` for critical CSS/fonts) regardless of document order.
10. **Use a CDN** — global server network serves your site from near the visitor; enable in your plugin and point it at your CDN CNAME.
11. **Compress images** — bulk-optimize with a compression plugin and convert to WebP/AVIF (with fallbacks) where supported; choose a higher-quality setting if quality drops. (Lazy-loading doesn't help above-the-fold images.)

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Audit installed plugins/extensions; remove the unused
- [ ] Move DNS to a fast provider (swap nameservers at registrar)
- [ ] Enable server + browser caching, including mobile
- [ ] Minify CSS/JS on staging; verify nothing breaks
- [ ] Check HTTP version; combine files only if pre-HTTP/2
- [ ] Defer JS; inline critical CSS
- [ ] Lazy-load below-fold images, videos, iframes (never the LCP image)
- [ ] Set font-display (swap or optional) for web fonts
- [ ] Preload the hero/CSS/font resources
- [ ] Connect a CDN
- [ ] Bulk-compress images; re-check quality
- [ ] Measure before/after with a page-speed report and a site-wide crawl

## Data points & benchmarks
- One reported result of this stack: page size 1.89 MB → 695 KB (-63%); requests cut ~50%; full load 5.9s → 4s; site-wide average/95th-percentile load times improved ~40%; lab speed score went poor → near-perfect.
- ~20 minutes of settings changes; no code rewriting required.
- Remaining slowness after all this = custom work (clunky theme, slow hosting, heavy third-party scripts) — hire a developer.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Testing optimizations directly on production — minification and file combination can break styling/scripts.
- Judging changes in a cached browser window — clear cache and verify in incognito.
- Combining files when the server already speaks HTTP/2.
- Lazy-loading above-the-fold images.
- Assuming plugins are free of conflicts — speed plugins can clash; isolate changes.

## Key principles
- Settings-level wins (DNS, caching, compression, deferral) beat code-level heroics for most CMS sites.
- Always measure before and after — site-wide crawl metrics, not just one page's score.
- File count matters on HTTP/1.1; file size always matters.

## ARTICLE subdomain-vs-subfolder

### Subdomain vs Subdirectory: Subdirectories Are NOT Better for SEO

- **Updated**: 2026-10-04

## TL;DR
The belief that subdirectories (domain.com/blog) beat subdomains (blog.domain.com) is a myth from old, confounded case studies. Search engines treat both roughly equally — what matters is whether you treat the section as part of your site (internal links, shared signals), not which side of the root domain the path sits on. Migrating between them purely for SEO adds risk for no expected gain.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Why the myth is wrong:**
- Google has said since at least 2012 (and repeatedly) that both are fine; pick whichever is easier to configure and keep long-term.
- URL paths are virtual in modern systems — a subdomain can be re-served as a subdirectory in minutes without moving anything physical.
- Subdomains that are integrated with the main site (linked, branded) get treated as part of the site — they even appear in sitelinks for major brands. Subdomains that aren't integrated may be treated separately — and the same is true of subdirectories.
- Historically subdomains could earn extra SERP listings; that loophole was closed by host-clustering updates long ago.

**Why case studies show "subdirectory wins":** nearly all are confounded by simultaneous changes:
1. Temporary signal inheritance — new/moved pages briefly borrow folder/site-level signals, causing flux that settles.
2. Tracking/measurement errors — broken subdomain analytics, seasonal comparisons, core-update timing, or only measuring the new folder's growth.
3. Blocked/noindexed pages — signals couldn't consolidate before or during the move.
4. Redesign or platform change — speed, headings, URLs, tech all changed too.
5. **Internal linking changes** — the big one: moving from an unlinked subdomain to a subdirectory linked from every page obviously lifts traffic; the uplift is the links, not the path position.
6. Content added/removed during the migration.

**Decision rule:** choose based on infrastructure, CMS, analytics, and long-term maintainability — not SEO. If sections must live on different stacks, subdomains are fine. Whatever you pick, connect sections with internal links and keep the structure stable.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Don't migrate subdomain ↔ subdirectory for SEO reasons alone
- [ ] If sections must feel like one site: cross-link them in navigation, headers, or footers
- [ ] Ensure analytics tracks across hosts before judging any migration's impact
- [ ] When evaluating a migration case study, check for concurrent redesigns, content, or linking changes
- [ ] Compare equivalent pages' traffic before/after, not just folder totals
- [ ] Keep URL structure stable; every change takes time to settle in search

## Data points & benchmarks
- One social-listening brand's subdomain→subdirectory migration (Jan 2016) shows no traffic step-change; growth began only when they added more content afterward, and equivalent pages simply shifted traffic to new URLs.
- A major dev platform moved blog from subdirectory → subdomain → separate domain with only migration-period flux; no version was inherently better.
- Major brands' subdomains regularly appear as sitelinks (streaming, retail, and software examples abound).
- Google confirmed (2023 conference) that even subdirectories can be treated as separate sites if the site itself doesn't treat them as connected — common in non-English webs.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Risking a migration to "gain" something that isn't real.
- Isolating a subdomain (or subfolder) with no internal links, then expecting site-wide signals to flow.
- Attributing post-migration growth to the URL path when internal linking, content, or tracking changed simultaneously.
- Judging migrations during the temporary-flux window.

## Key principles
- Integration, not location, determines whether a section shares the site's signals.
- URL paths are virtual labels, not physical structures.
- Every URL-structure change introduces migration risk and settling time — spend it on changes with real payoffs.

## ARTICLE trailing-slash

### Trailing Slashes: When URL Slash Variants Matter

- **Updated**: 2026-10-03

## TL;DR
A trailing slash ("/") at the end of a URL is a separate URL everywhere EXCEPT directly after the root domain (domain.com = domain.com/). Decide one version per path, make every canonicalization signal (redirects, sitemaps, internal links, canonicals, hreflang) agree with it, and don't force a change unless your setup is actually broken.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**The rules:**
1. Root domain: slash irrelevant — identical URL.
2. All other paths: `/page` and `/page/` are DIFFERENT URLs to servers and search engines.
3. Files (.html, .pdf, .jpg, .js): adding a slash usually 404s (system assumes folder).

**Scenario handling:**
- Same content on both versions → duplicate-content risk; usually resolved by canonicals, but force your preferred version with a 301 for cleanliness. Align ALL signals: redirects, sitemap entries, internal links, canonical tags.
- Different content on each version (two systems sharing paths, some A/B testing tools) → pick one version, 301 the other.
- Hreflang: annotations must point to the INDEXED version; a slash mismatch between canonical and hreflang targets can cause hreflang to be ignored.

**Implementation:**
- Server rewrite rules (Apache): removal rule must exclude existing directories (`!-d`); addition rule must exclude real files (`!-f`) or you'll break images, PDFs, JS, CSS.
- CMS: trailing slash is a permalink setting (e.g., `/%postname%/` vs `/%postname%`).
- JS frameworks: configure the router or use a framework module.

**Reporting quirk:** Search Console URL-prefix properties and analytics folder reports treat slash variants differently — slashes in the property definition can exclude non-slash pages from reports; add an analytics filter (regex to force trailing slashes) to consolidate.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] One slash convention chosen for all paths
- [ ] 301 redirects from the non-preferred variant (excluding real files/directories)
- [ ] Sitemap contains only preferred-variant URLs
- [ ] Internal links use the preferred variant directly (no redirect hops)
- [ ] Canonical tags self-reference the preferred variant
- [ ] Hreflang targets match the indexed (canonical) variant
- [ ] Analytics/Search Console properties and filters configured for the convention

## Data points & benchmarks
- Root domain with/without slash: exactly equivalent; everything past it: distinct URLs.
- A/B testing tools and dual-system setups are the most common source of genuinely different content on slash variants.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Blanket slash-removal rewrites without directory exclusions — breaks category/folder pages.
- Blanket slash-addition without file exclusions — breaks every static file.
- Leaving both variants live with mixed signals — splits rankings and crawl attention.
- Restructuring a working setup for cosmetic reasons — change carries risk; benefit here is near zero.

## Key principles
- Consistency beats preference: either convention works if every signal agrees.
- Slash variants are duplicate content in the same way www/non-www and http/https are — same diagnosis, same fix.
- Reporting tools encode slash assumptions too; align convention across SEO AND analytics.

## ARTICLE url-parameters

### URL Parameters: A Complete Guide for SEOs

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
URL parameters (?key=value) are either active (change content: filter, sort, paginate, translate, search) or passive (tracking: affiliate IDs, utm tags, session IDs, video timestamps). Passive parameters create duplicates; active ones create near-duplicates or new content. Audit what each parameter does, then control with canonicals by default, noindex selectively, and robots.txt only for crawl-budget emergencies.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**SEO implications:**
- Passive parameters → duplicate content: keep crawlable, canonical each variant to the main version.
- Active parameters → near-duplicate or distinct content: verify intent per parameter.
- Never use tracking parameters on internal links — use analytics event tracking instead (parameter-laden internal links are an outdated practice).
- Infinite URL paths: keep parameter order consistent; depth reports showing 9+ levels signal infinite/combinatorial paths. Crawlers self-limit repetitive patterns.
- Internationalization via parameters: officially possible but discouraged — extra complexity and weak geo-targeting signals (use subfolders/ccTLDs plus hreflang instead).
- Using # fragments instead of ? for tracking: avoid — servers ignore fragments, many systems can't read them, and # is reserved for on-page anchors/routing.

**Audit process:**
1. Crawl the site; search URLs containing "?" ; use filters to isolate multi-parameter URLs.
2. Manually check what each parameter actually does to content.
3. Check duplicate clusters: are variants canonicalized to a preferred version?

**Control options (in order of preference):**
1. **Canonical tags** — default choice; consolidates signals but requires crawling each variant (crawlers reduce frequency over time).
2. **Noindex** — removes from index; blocks signal consolidation, so avoid when signals should merge.
3. **robots.txt blocking** — pages may still get indexed without content being crawled; no signal consolidation; reserve for crawl-budget problems.
4. Crawler settings can exclude parameterized URLs from your own audits.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Inventory every parameter in use; label active vs passive
- [ ] Remove tracking parameters from internal links; switch to event tracking
- [ ] Canonicalize passive-parameter variants to clean URLs
- [ ] Decide per active parameter: indexable unique content vs canonicalized variant
- [ ] Enforce consistent parameter ordering; block combinatory explosion paths
- [ ] Review duplicate clusters for missing/mismatched canonicals
- [ ] Only block via robots.txt when crawl budget is genuinely strained
- [ ] Avoid parameter-based locales and # fragment "parameters"

## Data points & benchmarks
- The Search Console URL Parameters tool (2009-2022) was removed because engines got good at guessing parameter value: only ~1% of user configurations were useful.
- One reported misconfiguration case: one wrong "content doesn't change" setting deindexed a few hundred thousand pages.
- Crawl depth of 9+ levels is a strong infinite-path indicator.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Blocking parameter URLs in robots.txt when you need their signals to consolidate.
- Using noindex where canonicalization is needed.
- Tracking parameters on internal links (splits and duplicates URLs site-wide).
- Assuming the engine always guesses right — audit and signal explicitly.
- Parameter-based international URLs (weak geo-targeting signals).

## Key principles
- Everything is situational: parameters aren't inherently bad; unmanaged ones are.
- Canonical = consolidate; noindex = remove; robots.txt block = starve (use last).
- Fewer URLs with cleaner signals beats endless parameter permutations.

## ARTICLE website-crawlers

### How Website Crawlers Work

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Web crawlers (search engine spiders) discover, download, parse, index, and rank pages by following links; site crawlers (audit tools) are your private bots that simulate how a search engine interprets your site. To earn more crawling: raise quality, keep content fresh, and maintain a logical structure with strong internal linking.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**The 7 stages of web crawling:**
1. URL discovery — sitemap/published URLs act as "seed" URLs.
2. Crawling — page is scheduled, then meta tags, images, links, and structured data are downloaded.
3. Parsing — the crawler analyzes what it downloaded.
3a. URL discovery loop — newly found links (including via redirects) join the crawl queue; stages repeat.
4. Indexing — data stored for retrieval.
5. Ranking — indexed pages ordered by quality and relevance.
6. Crawl ends — bounded by time allocated, page counts, and link depth followed.
7. Revisiting — pages periodically re-crawled for changes.

URLs discovered this way grow exponentially within a few link hops.

**Types of crawlers:** search engine crawlers (build the index, find new/changed content, monitor quality), site audit crawlers (map structure, flag technical issues, track links), plus academic, semantic, open-source, and visual scraping tools.

**How to get crawled in the first place:**
- XML sitemap listing important pages.
- The URL Inspection tool in Search Console to request recrawling (a hint, not a command).
- IndexNow-style pings (supported by several search engines, not Google) on add/update/remove/redirect.

**How to get crawled more, and more often:**
1. Prioritize quality — links (internal + external) and page experience determine importance (PageRank-style "votes").
2. Keep things fresh — regularly updated pages get revisited more; check crawl stats "by purpose" (refresh vs discovery split) to gauge this.
3. Refine site structure — logical sitemap + relevant internal links help crawlers navigate and prioritize.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] XML sitemap submitted and current
- [ ] New/updated key pages submitted for recrawling (Request Indexing / IndexNow — hints, no guarantee)
- [ ] Declining top pages refreshed with meaningful content updates
- [ ] Internal links support navigation to valuable pages
- [ ] Site crawler audit run regularly; errors triaged before warnings before notices
- [ ] Fixes prioritized by traffic impact (e.g., missing metas on high-traffic pages first)
- [ ] Crawl segments/filters used to audit the most valuable site areas first

## Data points & benchmarks
- Recrawl rates vary from multiple times per day to once every few months, depending on freshness and importance.
- One leading SEO industry crawler reportedly visits billions of pages every 24 hours (older figure) — crawl capacity is finite and prioritized, so crawlability is competitive.
- Every crawler visit pings your server — crawl budget exists because unchecked bots get blocked for overusing resources.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Treating Request Indexing and IndexNow as commands — they are hints; search engine crawlers decide when and whether to visit.
- Fixing issues in report order instead of impact order (traffic-weighted prioritization).
- Ignoring site structure: crawlers (and users) rely on links, not luck.

## Key principles
- Crawlers follow links — discoverability is a function of internal linking, sitemaps, and external links.
- Crawl decisions are dynamic: quality, freshness, and structure drive how much crawl attention a site earns.
- A site crawler is a simulation of search performance: run it before search engines find problems for you.

- [ ] Staging site blocked from indexing
- [ ] Crawl staging before pushing live
- [ ] Old URLs 301 (or 308) redirect to new — not 302/307
- [ ] Internal links and canonical/hreflang tags updated to new URLs

- [ ] Redirects and content verified on most-linked pages

- [ ] Old domain renewals monitored so nobody else registers them

- Mid-size site migrating to a new domain: expect a few weeks of flux.
- Merging into an existing site: often no traffic disruption at all.
- Redirect only file types that get indexed (HTML, images, PDFs) — skip JS/CSS/fonts.

- Running a migration without a rollback plan or baseline crawl.

## ARTICLE what-is-https

### HTTPS for SEO: Setup, Migration Mistakes & Fixes

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
HTTPS (HTTP over TLS encryption) is a lightweight ranking signal, a trust requirement, and the prerequisite for modern speed protocols (HTTP/2, HTTP/3). Migrate with a TLS certificate + 301 redirects, then audit for the five classic mistakes: leftover HTTP pages, mixed content, unupdated internal links, unupdated canonical/OG tags, and broken redirect chains.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Certificates:**
1. Get a TLS certificate from a trusted Certificate Authority and install it on the server.
2. Validation tiers: DV (domain validation — free, fine for blogs), OV (organization validated), EV (extended validation — browsers no longer show special EV indicators, so it rarely adds value). All tiers encrypt equally; the choice is about organizational assurance, not ranking.
3. Wildcard certificates cover unlimited subdomains; SAN certificates can also cover separate domains.
4. Use TLS 1.3 with 1.2 fallback; disable TLS 1.0/1.1 (deprecated, dropped by browsers).

**Migration (HTTP → HTTPS):**
1. Install certificate; 301-redirect all HTTP URLs to HTTPS.
2. Update internal links, canonical tags, and Open Graph tags to HTTPS URLs.
3. Fix mixed content (HTTP resources on HTTPS pages) — a CSP of `upgrade-insecure-requests` fixes most of it.
4. Implement HSTS as the final step (prevents SSL-stripping downgrades).
5. New sites: launch on HTTPS from day one.

**Post-migration audit — the 5 mistakes:**
1. HTTP pages left → crawl with all URL sources; redirect stragglers (check ad-platform URL exports for unlinked PPC landing pages).
2. HTTPS pages loading HTTP resources → update resource URLs.
3. Internal links still HTTP → rewrite (avoids pointless redirect hops).
4. Canonical/OG tags still HTTP → rewrite; HTTP canonicals are usually ignored.
5. Failed redirects (chains/loops) → split chains into single 301 hops; update internal links to the final 200 URL.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] TLS certificate valid and unexpired; renewal automated
- [ ] TLS 1.3 enabled, 1.0/1.1 disabled
- [ ] All pages 301 HTTP→HTTPS, no leftover HTTP URLs in crawl
- [ ] No mixed content warnings
- [ ] Internal links point directly to HTTPS 200 URLs
- [ ] Canonical + OG tags use HTTPS URLs
- [ ] HSTS header implemented (last step)
- [ ] Redirect chains collapsed to single hops
- [ ] Ad campaign destination URLs updated

## Data points & benchmarks
- HTTPS has been a lightweight Google ranking signal since 2014 — a tiebreaker, not a rocket booster.
- ~89% of websites default to HTTPS (one 2026 web technology survey; approximate).
- HTTPS→HTTP referral data is stripped: HTTP sites lose referral source visibility in analytics (shows as "direct").
- HTTP/3 (QUIC-based): ~35% global adoption in one survey; requires TLS 1.3; brings 0-RTT connection setup.
- A leading non-profit certificate authority issues free DV certificates and held ~64% CA market share in one survey.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Letting certificates expire because renewal isn't automated.
- Forgetting pages that exist only in ad platforms (no sitemap/links = invisible to crawls).
- Updating links but not canonical/OG tags (or vice versa).
- Endless redirect chains from stacking migrations — both users and crawlers pay the cost.

## Key principles
- HTTPS is table stakes: browsers label HTTP sites "Not secure," and major browsers are moving to HTTPS-by-default with explicit warnings for HTTP.
- The migration itself is routine; the damage comes from the half-finished edges (links, tags, redirects).
- External backlinks don't need updating — 301s consolidate signals; spend that effort internally.

### 8 Fixes For When Your Website Isn't Showing Up In Google

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
When a site is invisible in search, work an 8-step diagnostic ladder: indexed at all? → sitemap submitted? → noindex blocking? → robots.txt blocking? → orphans (no internal links)? → enough backlinks vs competitors? → matching search intent? → penalty? The first five are mechanical checks; the last three are strategic gaps.

## Framework / Step-by-step
1. **Check indexing** — `site:yourwebsite.com` gives a rough view of what's indexed; the Page indexing report and URL Inspection tool confirm per-page status and offer "Request indexing" for misses (a hint, not a guarantee).
2. **Submit your sitemap** — sitemaps flag important pages even when internal links are missing. Find the URL in robots.txt; submit in Search Console. No sitemap? Create one.
3. **Check for noindex** — `<meta name="robots" content="noindex">` blocks indexing regardless of sitemap. Common trap: CMS "discourage search engines" checkbox adds it sitewide. Crawl for "noindex page in sitemap" warnings — near-certain problem.
4. **Check robots.txt crawl blocks** — Search Console's "Submitted URL blocked by robots.txt" error means either unblock pages you want indexed or remove unwanted pages from the sitemap. If unsure, hire an expert — one bad rule hides the whole site.
5. **Check internal links / orphans** — pages with no internal links (orphans) are findable only via sitemap/backlinks and are rarely deemed important: if visitors can't reach a page, why index it? Crawl with sitemap + backlinks as URL sources to surface orphans; link, merge, or delete them.
6. **Check backlinks** — indexed ≠ visible; few-link pages rarely rank for searched terms (clear links↔traffic correlation in one large dataset). Compare your linking sites (and third-party authority estimates, which are not Google metrics) vs competitors; benchmark needed links by counting linking domains of the top 3 results for your target keyword. Quality beats count: one example page has 421 linking domains but only ~50 strong ones — that's the real competitive bar.
7. **Check search intent** — if top results for your keyword are all listicles and you're shipping a product page, you won't rank. Match the dominant format/type of page-one results.
8. **Check for penalties** — manual actions appear in Search Console (Security & Manual Actions); algorithmic hits show as traffic cliffs aligned with known update dates on a traffic-over-time graph. Either → get expert help.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Run site: search; inspect and request indexing for key URLs
- [ ] Submit XML sitemap (URL usually in robots.txt)
- [ ] Verify no noindex tags — especially CMS visibility settings
- [ ] Clear robots.txt blocks flagged in the Page indexing report (check the robots.txt report in Search Console → Settings)
- [ ] Crawl for orphan pages; add internal links to anything important
- [ ] Compare linking domains vs the top 3 ranking pages for your keyword
- [ ] Align page format with the dominant content type on page one
- [ ] Check manual actions and traffic-vs-update alignment

## Data points & benchmarks
- Brand-new sites commonly take days to several weeks to be indexed, and longer to rank for non-brand searches; check indexing before assuming a problem.
- Example competitive bar: a ranking listicle with 421 linking domains had only ~50 high-quality ones when filtered for link quality — quality filters reset the real target.
- Clear positive correlation between backlinks and organic traffic across one large dataset.
- CMS "search engine visibility" checkbox = the most common accidental sitewide noindex.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Submitting a sitemap full of noindexed or robots-blocked URLs.
- Assuming indexing = visibility; without competitive links, nothing ranks for searched terms.
- Ignoring intent and trying to outrank listicles with product pages.
- DIY surgery on a misunderstood robots.txt — one directive can hide everything.

## Key principles
- Visibility = crawlable + indexable + internally linked + competitive authority + intent match.
- Orphan pages signal unimportance to both users and engines.
- Benchmark link needs against quality-filtered competitor links, not raw counts.
