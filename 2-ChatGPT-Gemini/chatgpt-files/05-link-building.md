3. **Apply the playbook to the user's situation.** Each article has a TL;DR, framework, checklist, data points, mistakes and principles. Turn these into concrete actions for the user's site, niche and resources. Don't recite the article.

6. **Stay white-hat.** Don't promise rankings or timelines. Flag risky tactics (link buying, scaled AI spam, cloaking) and explain the risk.
7. **End with a deliverable:** a prioritized action plan or checklist (with impact and effort), a draft, or a specific diagnosis with next steps.

## ARTICLE anchor-text

### Anchor Text: A Data-Driven Guide (384,614 Web Pages Studied)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Anchor text is the clickable text of a link, and search engines have used it since day one to understand what a page is about — but an industry study of 384,614 pages found only a *weak* correlation between keyword-rich anchors and rankings. Most ranking pages have zero exact-match anchored backlinks, so don't engineer anchor ratios; keep anchors natural and branded, and spend the effort on topics and content instead.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Know the anchor types** (for a page targeting "backlink checker"):
1. **Exact match** — anchor is exactly the keyword ("backlink checker")
2. **Phrase match** — anchor contains the keyword phrase ("best backlink checker")
3. **Partial match** — anchor has all the words, unordered ("checker for backlinks")
4. **Branded** — your brand name
5. **Naked URL** — the raw URL as the anchor
6. **Random** — "click here", "this article"
7. **Image link** — the image's alt text acts as the anchor

**History that shapes today's practice:**
- Original search algorithm treated link text as a ranking signal — anchors could describe pages better than the pages themselves (and rank uncrawlable files like images).
- Heavy reliance enabled "Google bombing" — spammy exact-anchor campaigns ranking irrelevant pages.
- The 2012 Penguin update specifically targeted over-optimized anchor text; affected ~3.1% of queries and tanked aggressive exact-match sites overnight.

**Study design (why to trust it):** top 20 results for 19,840 keywords (2-5K monthly searches, 2-4 words) = 384,614 pages; keywords chosen so top-10 results had similar page-authority scores, isolating the anchor variable; reported medians as well as averages because extreme values skew averages.

**Results (Spearman correlation with position):**
- Exact-match anchor share: 0.14-0.19 — weak positive; median share of exact-match anchors was 0% at every position
- Phrase-match: ~0.11-0.14 — very weak
- Partial-match: ~0.11-0.14 — very weak
- Random anchors: ~0.01 — none
- Surrounding link text (per a second study of 16K pages with only random anchors): 0.02-0.06 — no effect, even though 20-25% of links contained at least one keyword word nearby

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Audit your profile for exact-match anchor clusters (clusters of one keyword across many sites = manipulation pattern)
- [ ] Use branded or natural anchors wherever you control them (e.g., guest-post author bios)
- [ ] Write descriptive image alt text for linked images (it becomes the anchor)
- [ ] Let organic anchors be whatever linkers naturally write — usually branded or random
- [ ] If copying competitor anchor ratios: stop — you can't control anchors with legitimate tactics anyway
- [ ] Optimize pages for topics and semantic variants (on-page) rather than engineering anchors per keyword

## Data points & benchmarks
- 384,614 pages / 19,840 keywords analyzed; exact-match anchor correlation only 0.14-0.19 (weak)
- Median exact-match anchor share at every ranking position: 0%
- Penguin (2012) hit anchor over-optimization; ~3.1% of queries affected
- Top 10 ranking pages also rank for 400-1,300 other queries on average (3M-query study) — one anchor can't (and shouldn't) target them all
- On one large SEO blog, only ~22% of a page's traffic comes from its main target keyword (one example: 17%)

## Mistakes to avoid
- Building exact-match anchor links to rank for one keyword — ignores the long tail and invites Penguin-style risk
- Assuming high average anchor percentages matter — averages hide that most winners have zero
- Using PBNs or paid links to place keyword anchors — rare, unnatural, and a spam-policy violation (paid links must be rel="sponsored" or nofollow)
- Copying the anchor distribution of currently ranking pages — no legitimate tactic gives you that control

## Key principles
- Anchor text gives search engines context; it is a weak ranking signal, not a dial to turn.
- Natural anchor profiles are mostly branded, random, and naked URLs — because that's what real linkers write.
- Topics beat keywords: optimize for the cluster of queries a page deserves, not the one keyword an anchor could hit.
- The main legitimate place you choose the anchor (an editorial guest post author bio, never a paid placement) is exactly where you should choose branding over keywords.

## ARTICLE audit-brand-mentions

### How to Audit Brand Mentions for Modern SEO

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Brand mentions are to AI search what backlinks are to traditional SEO: links tell search engines who to trust, mentions tell AI systems who you are. Run a recurring mention audit — benchmark, recover lost mentions, upgrade weak ones, fix misinformation, and close gaps against competitors — to shape how both rankings and AI-generated answers describe your brand.

## Framework / Step-by-step
The 8-step brand mention audit (tool-agnostic — use a brand/web monitoring tool or content research tool with mention search):

1. **Benchmark existing mentions & web visibility.** Record unique pages mentioning you, estimated traffic to those pages, and referring domains linking to them. Compare cumulative vs. month-by-month views to get mention velocity and correlate spikes with campaigns/launches.
2. **Check how mentions shape AI results.** AI answers mirror the dominant language patterns found online. Review mentions (exclude your own site; keep only core-topic pages) and ask: predominant sentiment? which brand elements (sub-brands, execs, frameworks) appear most? which publication types and formats mention you? which topics/entities are you paired with? do mention spikes align with performance lifts?
3. **Discover lost mentions.** Mentions vanish when publishers update, redesign, or prune content — sometimes replacing you with a competitor. Export mentions quarterly (filter by publication date) into a spreadsheet/pivot table; highlight pages that dropped off, verify what changed (e.g., with a web archive), then prioritize high-traffic, authoritative pages and ask the publisher to restore the mention.
4. **Improve weak mentions.** Weak = generic anchor text ("click here"), links with no brand name nearby, references buried outside main content, negative/neutral sentiment, one-line passing mentions, or mentions only on dead pages. Add fixable ones to a prospect list and pitch context upgrades (branded anchor, brand name alongside the link).
5. **Fix misinformation before it spreads.** Common issues: hallucinated URLs to your site, inaccurate claims from community/UGC sites repeated as fact, misrepresenting low-quality sites. Update assets you control first (citations, directory listings, unclear on-site content); request corrections from external publishers; escalate to legal only for deliberate disinformation or negative SEO.
6. **Gap-check against competitors.** Benchmark rivals on the same metrics. Export their mentions (excluding their own sites) into a comparison sheet; pages mentioning multiple competitors but not you are quick wins. Reverse-engineer their strongest mentions: formats, publications, campaigns, and outreach channels behind them.
7. **Exploit untapped opportunities.** Search core topics and exclude pages mentioning your brand AND all competitors (using search operators like `-site:`, `-"brand"`, `inurl:`, `intitle:`). The remaining neutral pages are outreach targets — assess authority and whether a natural brand mention fits.
8. **Monitor and report.** Monthly: mention alerts, triage each new mention (link ask, correction, strengthening), track velocity. Quarterly: re-benchmark competitors, spot emerging publications/partners, report insights and action items to stakeholders.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Baseline: pages mentioning the brand, their traffic, their referring domains
- [ ] Note mention velocity (monthly + cumulative) and tie spikes to campaigns
- [ ] Review sentiment, formats, publications, and co-occurring topics in mentions
- [ ] Quarterly export of mentioning pages; flag and investigate drop-offs
- [ ] Reclaim lost mentions on high-traffic, authoritative pages first
- [ ] Prospect list for weak mentions; pitch branded anchors/context upgrades
- [ ] Correct misinformation on owned assets immediately; outreach for external fixes
- [ ] Comparison sheet: pages mentioning competitors but not you → outreach
- [ ] Neutral-topic page list (no brand or competitor mentions) → insertion pitches
- [ ] Monthly mention alerts + triage; quarterly stakeholder report

## Data points & benchmarks
- One brand fixed recurring AI hallucination about its product by clarifying its own post — the brand then held the #1 organic spot plus the AI Overview citation for that concept
- Mention audits parallel backlink audits: track acquisition rate, losses, and competitor gaps the same way

## Mistakes to avoid
- Treating mentions as PR fluff: they can shape how AI systems describe your brand
- Paying for mentions or links without a sponsored/nofollow tag and clear disclosure — earn them or label them
- Ignoring small inaccuracies — isolated errors compound into dominant (wrong) language patterns
- Only auditing your own mentions and never benchmarking competitors
- Chasing every weak mention instead of prioritizing high-traffic, authoritative pages
- One-off audits: mentions fluctuate, so cadence (monthly/quarterly) is the value

## Key principles
- Mentions = the AI-search counterpart of backlinks; audit them with equal rigor
- AI search is probabilistic: the context, frequency, and sentiment of mentions shape answers

## ARTICLE backlink-audit

### How to Do a Basic Backlink Audit (in Under 30 Minutes)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Run a backlink audit before taking on any site (like a home inspection before buying): check for spam/penalty risks AND mine opportunities. Three phases: a site-level health check (growth curve, spikes, country-code TLDs, anchors), deeper dives (wasted links, worst/best referrers, anchor spam, PBN footprints), then a page-level backlink review to find linkable patterns you can replicate.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Setup**: a backlink analysis tool, set to the whole domain including subdomains (or a URL prefix for a subfolder).

**Phase 1 — High-level view (site-level overview):**
1. Compare backlinks + referring domains vs competitors (RDs = key metric; e.g., one competitor's blog ~8x another's RDs)
2. Check RD growth curve over time vs competitors (cumulative slope + absolute gains — e.g., competitor A +7,230 RDs/2yr vs competitor B +1,811)
3. Spot link SPIKES — they can signal a negative SEO attack or a paid campaign (e.g., one blogger's mid-2014 spike+drop); Google usually ignores such links, but extreme cases may warrant disavowing
4. Country-code TLD distribution: disproportionate .cn/.gq-type links look unnatural (context matters — .cn is normal for Chinese businesses; ~2% is a rough comfort level, not a rule)
5. Anchor profile: natural usually means plenty of branded anchors (roughly a fifth of referring domains for an established brand, as a rough heuristic); watch for crude language or heavy keyword-rich anchors

**Phase 2 — Deeper:**
1. Wasted links: top pages by links + 404 filter (fix/redirect); top pages by links with ~no traffic (e.g., 296 RDs → 16 visits/mo) → refresh + reoptimize
2. Best/worst referrers: referring domains, followed only; sort by the tool's authority score ascending = fishy sites (a rough screen — review manually); sort by followed-link count descending = sitewide/sidebar/footer (often paid) links; sort authority descending = your "superfans" → nurture relationships
3. Full anchor list: search for spam terms; bulk-anchor trick — export anchors → paste into a keyword research tool → sort by CPC descending → high-commercial anchors reveal over-optimized links built by past agencies (check the top ~100 rows first; some tools charge per row)
4. Referring IPs (advanced): many domains on the same c-class IP = possible PBN → remove/disavow (only for experienced SEOs)

**Phase 3 — Page-level backlink review:**
1. Pick a page (top by links, or top by link growth over the last 30 days) — not the whole domain (mixed anchors/targets muddy insights)
2. Filter to followed links; optionally sort by linking-page organic traffic (best referral links)
3. Find COMMONALITIES between top links (anchor text, platform/site type, surrounding text) → understand WHY people link → find more of those prospects (e.g., guide earns links from "best SEO posts" listicles + content-writing articles → systematically target both via a content research tool)
Bonus: run the same audit on competitors to steal strategy.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Site level: benchmark RDs vs 2-3 competitors
- [ ] Check RD growth curve for spikes/dips (possible negative SEO or paid campaigns)
- [ ] Check country-code TLD distribution for spam-TLD concentration
- [ ] Check anchor distribution for branded vs keyword-rich
- [ ] Fix 404s with backlinks; reoptimize high-RD/low-traffic pages
- [ ] Identify superfans (high-authority frequent linkers) and worst referrers
- [ ] Run the anchor → CPC export trick if agency-history suspected
- [ ] Analyze 2-3 top pages' backlinks for linkable commonalities → build prospect lists from patterns

## Data points & benchmarks
- Rough heuristic (not a Google rule): ~**20%+ of referring domains** use branded anchors on an established brand
- Rough heuristic: ~**2%** unusual-TLD share is unremarkable in a healthy profile
- Case: page with **296 RDs but 16 organic visits/mo** = wasted-link reoptimization target
- Reciprocal/absolute growth comparison example: +7,230 vs +1,811 RDs over 2 years

## Mistakes to avoid
- Auditing the whole domain at once at link level — no actionable signal
- Disavowing everything on shared c-class IPs without experience
- Assuming a low authority score = bad site (review firsthand) or every .cn link = spam (context)
- Ignoring the audit's opportunity side — superfans and linkable patterns are the growth insights
- Skipping competitor audits — same effort, extra strategy intel

## Key principles
- Audit before you build: don't renovate a house (or site) with hidden damp (penalty risk).
- Referring domains growth vs competitors is the headline health metric.
- The "why people link" commonalities found in Phase 3 feed your next campaigns.

## ARTICLE backlink-growth-study

### How Many New Backlinks Do Top-ranking Pages Get Over Time (New Data)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Top-ranking pages attract new links faster than the pages below them — the "vicious circle of SEO": high rankings → exposure and credibility → more links → even stronger rankings. A study of 200,000 pages found #1 pages typically gain +5% to +14.5% new followed referring domains per month, so challengers must out-build not a static target but a moving one. Pace of acquisition alone, however, showed no measurable ranking effect — quality still decides.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Why the circle spins:**
1. **Exposure** — most searchers click the first few results; when they later need a reference to cite, they link to the page they actually read. Lower-ranking pages never got read, so they never get cited.
2. **Credibility** — a #1 position functions as an endorsement ("this is the best page for the topic"), making people more comfortable citing it.

**Study design:** 10,000 non-branded keywords (2-5K monthly searches) → top-20 pages each → 200,000 pages; measured followed referring domains acquired in the preceding 3 months by ranking position.

**Findings:**
- Top-3 results attract more new followed referring domains than the rest of the SERP; the median pace of link growth declines steadily with ranking position.
- #1 pages typically grow their followed referring domains +5% to +14.5% per month.
- Compounding math: if the leader has 55 referring domains and you need 3 months to match that, their profile will have grown +17-50% in the meantime (to ~64-83) — you must build up to ~83 links in 3 months just to keep pace.
- Counter-test: correlating link acquisition *rate* with 3-month SERP movement showed no clear relationship — pace alone doesn't move rankings (link quality, content, and behavioral factors confound it; SERPs regularly show pages with fewer RDs outranking pages with more).

**How to apply:** before targeting a keyword, open the referring-domains growth graph of the current #1 page (rough gauge — includes nofollow). If it's climbing fast, either budget serious link velocity or choose a different battle.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] For each target keyword, chart the #1 page's referring-domain growth over 12 months
- [ ] Calculate the leader's monthly growth % and project it across your expected campaign timeline
- [ ] Set your monthly link target above the leader's projected pace, not their current count
- [ ] Prefer keywords where the #1 page's growth is flat or slow (weaker circle)
- [ ] Track your own pages' RD growth % — early momentum compounds
- [ ] Don't interpret slow acquisition as automatic decline — pace ≠ ranking factor

## Data points & benchmarks
- 200,000 pages / 10,000 keywords analyzed
- #1 ranking pages: +5% to +14.5% followed-referring-domain growth per month
- Worked example: 55-RD leader → 64-83 RDs after 3 months of compounding
- Median link-growth pace declines monotonically with ranking position

## Mistakes to avoid
- Planning link counts against the leader's *current* profile instead of their projected one
- Assuming raw acquisition speed itself is a ranking factor — the SERP-movement test found no such link
- Ignoring the compounding advantage of early ranking wins (get to page one, then the circle helps you)
- Giving up on keywords where leaders are strong everywhere — check flat-growth leaders first

## Key principles
- Rankings and links form a feedback loop: visibility earns links, links defend visibility.
- SERP leaders are moving targets — model growth rates, not snapshots.
- The practical use of this data is keyword selection and velocity planning, not proving causation (it is one correlational study, and tool link counts are estimates).
- Plan for earned links only: buying links to hit a velocity target is a spam-policy violation (paid links must be rel="sponsored" or nofollow).
- Quality links + genuinely useful pages remain the strategy; velocity math just sizes the budget.

## ARTICLE bad-links

### 8 Types of Bad Links to Avoid

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Bad links are links intended to manipulate rankings — from dangerous (penalty bait) to pointless (wasted spend). Learn the eight types, avoid acquiring them, and only clean up (audit + disavow) if you have a manual action or have inherited a site with a shady link history. Otherwise search engines discount most spam links on their own.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**When you actually need to act:**
- You received a manual action for unnatural links, OR
- You bought/took over a site known or suspected to have done shady link building (PBNs, bought links).
Otherwise: don't hunt down every sketchy link, and never disavow haphazardly — you risk disavowing links that are helping you.

**The 8 types:**
1. **PBNs (private blog networks)** — networks of sites built solely to link to each other and fake "earned" links. Building an undetectable one requires real content, separate ownership/hosts/IPs — effort better spent on legitimate links. Detection: many referring sites on the same IP, near-zero traffic, thin content, interlinking, default themes.
2. **Paid links** — money, goods, or services for followed links (advertising/sponsorship links are fine if tagged sponsored/nofollow). Mass-sold placements dilute equity and invite penalties. Detection: exact-match anchors from low-quality, low-traffic sites.
3. **Hacked links** — injected into compromised sites. Illegal in many jurisdictions; the hacked site's owner can report you and get your domain/hosting taken down. Agencies have sold "guest posts" that turned out to be hacked placements — control your own link building or vet providers hard.
4. **Hidden links** — white-on-white text, CSS off-screening, zero-size fonts, or single-character links. One known pattern: an agency with site access hid footer links to all its clients. Detection: page is topically irrelevant with clusters of unrelated keyword anchors (e.g., "we buy houses" on a history site); inspecting the page source or using a browser extension reveals dozens of hidden outbound links.
5. **Link exchanges** — reciprocity is natural and fine at small scale; "excessive" exchanges and partner pages exist purely to cross-link and violate guidelines. Detection: merge your referring-domains export with your outbound-links export and find duplicates. A handful is fine; 30-40+ reciprocal links with one site needs trimming.
6. **Automated link building** — tiered blasts (links to your links) from cheap services marketed as "foundation links." Zero benefit, clear policy violation, riskiest when someone else runs the automation. Detection: mass referring domains with identical titles/anchors.
7. **Forum and comment spam** — profile links, signature blasts, bot comments. Identical comments across sites leave a footprint search engines penalize. Not worth checking for; just never buy.
8. **Low-quality directories** — bulk profile creation on junk directories (often sold as services). Hasn't worked for ~15 years. Stick to a few well-known listing sites.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Audit prospect lists against the 8 types before any campaign
- [ ] If you took over a site: check referring IPs, anchors, and traffic for PBN/paid patterns
- [ ] Export referring domains + outbound links, dedupe to measure reciprocal exposure
- [ ] Give editors/site access only to trusted partners (hidden-link risk)
- [ ] Require sponsored/nofollow attributes on anything paid
- [ ] Disavow ONLY after a manual action or confirmed toxic history — investigate each link first
- [ ] Never buy "foundation links," directory blasts, or comment/posting automation

## Data points & benchmarks
- In an older industry poll, 65% of SEOs agreed that "all link building is manipulation" — the line is tactics that make the web worse vs. legitimate promotion (search reps have explicitly endorsed digital PR-style earning)
- Reciprocity threshold: a handful of reciprocal domains is natural; ~30-40+ with a single site is a red flag
- Hidden-link tell: one keyword anchor surrounded by other sites' keyword anchors on an off-topic page
- Bulk directory submission has been ineffective for ~15 years

## Mistakes to avoid
- Disavowing links just because they "look spammy" — search engines likely already ignore them, and you may remove real value
- Assuming paid links are only cash deals — paying in products, services, or favors counts too
- Outsourcing link building to unvetted vendors (hacked and hidden links both trace back to agencies)
- Ignoring an inherited site's history — new ownership doesn't reset link-based risk

## Key principles
- Two failure modes: dangerous links (penalties, takedowns) and useless links (wasted budget). Both come from buying scale.
- Prevention beats cleanup — avoiding bad links is proactive; disavowal is a last resort.
- If a tactic only works when search engines fail to detect it, it's a bad link.
- Natural web behavior (some reciprocity, some directories, occasional forum links) is fine; industrialized versions of any of them are spam. Buying or repurposing expired domains to pass ranking signals is also a spam-policy violation.

## ARTICLE blogger-outreach

### Blogger Outreach: How to Do It and Scale It Without Feeling Like a Jerk

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Blogger outreach is personalized email to relevant bloggers — and the difference between outreach and spam is relevance, effort, and honesty. Use the "sniper" approach: a carefully segmented prospect list, one genuinely useful angle per group, real email addresses, and a pitch that starts a conversation rather than demands a link.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Sniper vs. shotgun.** The math can tie (100 personalized emails at 10% conversion ≈ 1,000 generic at 1%), but shotgun loses because: (1) prospects are finite — alienate them and your ceiling collapses; (2) spam complaints destroy deliverability; (3) only weak/new sites still respond to template blasts. Choose sniper.

**Step 1 — Find prospects (four sources, best first):**
1. People featured in your article (you already link to them — extract external links from your post)
2. People who wrote on the same topic — search Google (export the SERP via a browser extension or manual copy) or use a content database with "in title" + one-page-per-domain filters (example: 6,026 broad results → 385 hyper-relevant); flag never-linked domains for extra care
3. People who linked to same-topic articles — find articles with 10+ referring domains, then vet their linkers (filters: language, followed, in-content)
4. People who shared the topic on social — weakest source; only approach if the share is <7 days old or you'll look like a stalker

**Step 2 — Segment by influence (four tiers):**
- Sharks (authority 80+, celebrities) — don't email; only intros or remarkable work reach them
- Big Fish (authority ~50-80) — email yes, but never templates; ask for critique/validation, not links — they'll link if it's worth it
- Small fish (authority ~20-50) — yes; respond well to personalized, value-adding pitches
- Spawn (<20) — skip; links carry little value
Practical filter: keep authority 20-80, split at 50.

**Step 3 — Find the RIGHT contact details.** Real, personal addresses (a guessed info@ tells recipients you didn't care). Method: start from name + domain, not domain alone — bulk email-finder tools accept name/site lists; content databases export author names. ALWAYS verify emails before sending; bounces wreck deliverability.

**Step 4 — Craft the pitch (to ONE person first, templatize later):**
- Subject: short, specific, curiosity-provoking; never salesy ("You've GOT to see this!")
- First two lines must show you actually know them (visible in inbox previews) — one personalized cold email referencing a recipient's known interest led to a column mention AND a link
- No fake flattery: specific and real, or nothing
- Explain WHY them — "I wrote a similar article" is a poor excuse. Better: different opinion on something they shared, a topic your post covers that theirs doesn't, or their linked information is now outdated. Use "prospect bucketing": group linkers by the reason they linked, then write one strong angle per bucket
- End with a conversation-starter CTA ("What do you think?" / "Did I miss anything?") — asking for the link in email #1 is proposing on the first date
- Only outreach your best content (rank your posts by social traction; use those numbers as social proof)

**Step 5 — Scale with custom merge fields.** Convert the winning pitch into a template with rich fields — not just %first_name% but %nugget_of_wisdom% and %something_they_didnt_mention%. Fill them in a spreadsheet (outsourceable), review every merged email before sending, then follow up exactly ONCE, politely.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Build prospect lists from all four sources; tag never-linked domains
- [ ] Filter to authority 20-80; treat 50+ as high-effort Big Fish targets
- [ ] Find named contacts via name+domain email lookup; verify all addresses
- [ ] Write one perfect pitch to one real person before touching templates
- [ ] Bucket prospects by linking reason; one angle per bucket
- [ ] Add custom contextual merge fields; human-review each send
- [ ] Follow up once; never more
- [ ] Set up alerts (new mentions/links on your topic) to refill prospect lists on autopilot

## Data points & benchmarks
- Shotgun/sniper parity math: 1,000 emails @1% = 100 emails @10%
- Prospect narrowing example: 6,026 topic results → 385 with "in title" + one-per-domain
- Tier thresholds: skip authority <20 and >80; Big Fish cutoff ~50
- Content qualification: only pitch posts with proven social traction
- "Best email subject lines" is a heavily searched query (older figure) — which is exactly why those generic subject lines don't work

## Mistakes to avoid
- Broadcasting "I published a similar article, please link" emails
- Offering payment, free product, or other value in exchange for a link without disclosure — such links must be tagged rel="sponsored" or nofollow
- Sending to guessed addresses (info@) or unverified lists
- Asking for the link, a tweet, AND a call in the first email
- Multiple follow-ups — you burn bridges you'll want later
- Running outreach for every new post instead of your best work

## Key principles
- Personalization correlates directly with success rate; anything detectable as a template reads as selfish, lazy, and indifferent.
- The first email's goal is a reply, not a link.
- Prospects are a finite, shared resource across your whole industry — protect the ecosystem.
- Treat people like humans, let the content do the talking.

### Broken Link Building: The Complete Guide

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Broken link building = find a dead page with backlinks, build a replacement, then ask linkers to swap their dead link for yours. It ranked as the 5th most used tactic in one industry survey of 250+ marketers (older survey). Success hinges not on finding broken links but on vetting WHY people linked to the dead page, so your replacement and outreach match those reasons.

## Framework / Step-by-step
1. **Find broken pages with backlinks** — four methods:
   - a) Competitors' dead pages: a backlink analysis tool → top pages by links → filter to "404 not found" → sort by referring domains (e.g., one large industry site had 134 dead pages, some with 50+ RDs)
   - b) Broken pages on a topic: a content research tool → title search → filter to broken pages with ≥20 referring domains (e.g., 188 broken pages with 20+ RDs on one broad topic)
   - c) Competitors' broken outbound links: a backlink analysis tool's broken-links view → export → a bulk URL analysis tool to sort dead targets by RDs
   - d) Broken links on resource pages (works without paid tools): Google operators like `KEYWORD intitle:resources inurl:links.html` → check links free with a browser extension or link-checker; batch-analyze pages for referring-domain counts
2. **Vet the link prospects** — two checks:
   - a) Link quality: list backlinks, one link per domain; filter to followed + exclude subdomains + a low authority floor (e.g., 5+) + some real domain traffic (e.g., 20+). Example: 100 backlinks dropped to 29 after filters — enough good links remain to proceed.
   - b) Link reasons: distinguish **general links** (no visible reason) from **deep links** (reason visible in anchor/surrounding text). Search backlinks for "footprints" (e.g., "grow", "increase") to count how many linked for the same reason.
3. **Create a replacement page** — outline the dead page via Wayback Machine; bake in every deep-link reason; then improve generally (Simplify, Visualize, Templatize, Rectify).
4. **Do outreach — hybrid approach** — segment deep linkers by reason (one template each, citing the exact dead link + screenshot) vs. general linkers (one generic template selling the improvements, e.g., "includes a free template").

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Pull 3-5 competitors' top-pages-by-links → 404 filter → shortlist relevant dead pages
- [ ] Run a topic search in a content research tool for broken pages with 20+ RDs
- [ ] Check the traffic history of dead pages — past traffic implies quality links
- [ ] Apply quality filters (followed, no subdomains, low authority floor, some traffic) before committing
- [ ] Use Wayback Machine to map the dead page's structure and topics
- [ ] List deep-link reasons; note count per reason; skip opportunities built on unreplicable original research
- [ ] Write one outreach template per deep-link segment + one for general linkers
- [ ] Always include a screenshot showing where their dead link is

## Data points & benchmarks
- 5th most popular tactic in one older industry survey (250+ marketers)
- Quality filter example: 100 backlinks → 29 after followed/authority/traffic filters
- Example opportunities found via competitors' broken outbound links: a since-retired Google testing tool (2,654 RDs), an old algorithm-explainer article (462 RDs), a local SEO statistics page (276 RDs)
- Opportunity threshold used in the guide: **≥20 referring domains** (an adjustable rule of thumb)

## Mistakes to avoid
- Jumping straight to creating a replacement page before vetting link quality and link reasons
- Pursuing dead pages whose links all cite original data/research you can't replicate
- Pure shotgun outreach (burns bridges, gets domain blocked) or pure sniper outreach (too slow — a whole day for ~12 emails); use the hybrid segmented-template approach
- Assuming the tactic is dead because one guru failed at it — failure is usually poor execution

## Key principles
- "Link building tactics are just streamlined ways of finding link prospects with a reason for contact."
- Match content AND outreach to why people linked to the original — that sync is what raises success rates.
- Improve the replacement (template, visuals, accuracy) so general linkers get a "why" too: dead link → replacement → why it's a good replacement.

### Should You Buy Backlinks? It Depends

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Buying links violates search engine spam policies: at best they're silently ignored (money wasted), at worst they trigger manual actions that demote your whole site. In older surveys most link builders admitted to buying (74%+), paying ~$83 on average — but for any business that depends on organic rankings, the recommendation is don't buy; earn links with link-worthy content plus vetted outreach instead.

## Framework / Step-by-step
The two risks of paid links:
1. **Ignored links** — since 2016-era algorithm updates, search engines neutralize many spammy/paid links: no help, no harm, pure waste
2. **Penalties** — human reviewers can impose manual actions; parts or all of your site vanish from results (visible in the manual actions report in Search Console)

Why people buy anyway:
- Many site owners now demand payment for any placement — outreach alone often hits a paywall
- Paid links look identical to earned links and can move rankings when undetected (e.g., a casino comparison page with suspicious anchors thrives at ~54K visits/month — presumably high affiliate revenue funds the risk, and such pages often get hit eventually)

Market prices (older outreach studies across 450+180 sites in 9 niches; prices drift over time):
- **Niche edits** (paid link inserted into existing page): $361 average; only 12.6% of sites were willing to sell; price rises with domain authority
- **Paid guest posts**: $78 average; of the 25.5% of sites that replied to guest-post pitches, ~half quoted a fee; sellers skew to low-authority domains (and you still pay to produce the content)
- Cheap marketplace bulk links (thousands for a few dollars) never move rankings

The safe alternative — earn links:
1. Create content that deserves links
2. Do outreach so people know it exists
3. Vet every prospect with 5 questions:
   - **Does the site look legit?** (real design, links within content, images, real author bios — their absence suggests a link farm)
   - **Does it get consistent search traffic?** Check the traffic-over-time graph, not a snapshot — one example still had 4.8K monthly visits after a ~95% overnight crash aligned with a core update; avoid
   - **Has it been selling links?** Check outgoing followed anchors for odd exact-match commercial anchors
   - **Has it been buying links?** Check inbound anchors to its homepage for unnatural exact-match patterns
   - **Would your link be contextually useful?** Pitch unique value into a real gap, not "any link will do"

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Decide a written policy: paid links off the table for revenue-dependent sites
- [ ] Budget the true cost of "cheap" links: $78-361+ per placement plus content production
- [ ] Before ANY pitch (paid or free): run the 5-question prospect vetting
- [ ] Check traffic-over-time graphs for update-aligned crashes
- [ ] Audit outgoing + incoming anchors of every prospect
- [ ] Build unique data/content worth linking to; pitch it into contextual gaps
- [ ] Monitor Search Console for manual actions after any risky campaign

## Data points & benchmarks
- 74.3% of link builders surveyed admit buying backlinks
- Average self-reported link price: $83 (survey of 755 link builders)
- Niche edits: $361.44 average; 12.6% of 450 sites willing to sell
- Paid guest posts: $77.80 average; ~half of responding sites quoted fees; sellers mostly low-authority sites
- Rankings payoff example: ~54K monthly visits to one commercial comparison page (survivorship example, not proof the tactic is safe)
- If you do pay for placement, it is advertising: tag it rel="sponsored" (or nofollow) and disclose it

## Mistakes to avoid
- Buying bulk links from marketplaces — ignored at best
- Assuming a current traffic number equals health; always check the trendline
- Getting links from obvious link sellers — their penalty devalues or poisons your links
- Ignoring what a prospect's own link profile says (they buy links → avoid)
- Risking a livelihood business on a tactic that can erase visibility overnight

## Key principles
- Paid links are a risk-transfer: upside if undetected, catastrophic if caught
- Value drives price: links to pages that mint money cost the most
- Most sites won't sell (87%+ refused in studies) — the market is smaller than it seems
- Earned links compound; bought links depreciate
- Same due-diligence vetting applies to every link prospect, paid or free

## ARTICLE competitor-backlink-analysis

### How to Perform a Competitor Link Analysis in 3 Steps

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
A competitor link analysis reveals which sites commonly link to sites like yours — those shared links are likely what helps competitors rank. Basic version: identify competitors → link-intersect report → spot patterns. Scaled version (~2-4 hours): merge referring-domain exports across dozens of competitors (niche or local) and count overlaps to surface opportunity categories nobody in your market uses.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Basic (3 steps):**
1. **Identify competitors** — use the organic-competitors view in a backlink or SEO tool (or a keyword tool's domain-share view if you don't have a site yet); you can also just list who ranks in the SERPs you want
2. **Find common links** — run a link-intersect (common backlinks) comparison: enter your site + competitors → referring domains linking to multiple competitors but not you; open the actual links to see context
3. **Identify strategies** — work through missing common links, and/or categorize patterns (niche sites, local listings, etc.)
Bonus: check competitors' **top pages by links** for tools/studies/quotes driving links that a link-intersect view misses.

**Scaled niche process:**
1. List competitor domains (own list or a competing-domains view)
2. Export referring domains for each (a backlink analysis tool, or its API at larger scale)
3. Merge CSVs (Windows: `copy *.csv combined.csv`; Mac: `cat *.csv > combined.csv`)
4. Excel: Insert Table → Count column `=COUNTIF(B:B,[@[Referring Domain]])` → paste as values → Remove Duplicates → sort by Count desc
5. Categorize top referring sites (organizations, trade shows, suppliers, niche directories) → study how they link (what the linking pages say and where the link sits)

**Scaled LOCAL process (differentiators):**
- Build keyword list "[service] + [top 50 cities]" → a keyword research tool → export WITH SERPs → keep organic niche sites (filter out high-authority domains, roughly 50+ on the tool's scale, to drop directories/aggregators) → export each site's referring domains → same merge/count flow
- Or reverse: "[city] + [popular local niches]" → analyze same-city site overlaps
- Categorize local patterns (Raleigh example): colleges (jobs/scholarships/discounts/alumni), city directories, local news, suppliers/partners (testimonials), churches, business groups, events/meetups, sponsorships/charities, podcasts, local awards, coupons, job postings; wedding vendors interlink; realtors link to "things to do". Caution: links obtained in exchange for a scholarship, discount, sponsorship, or free product are paid placements — tag them rel="sponsored"/nofollow.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Identify 3-10 organic competitors
- [ ] Run a link-intersect comparison; export missing-link domains
- [ ] Check top pages by links per competitor for asset-driven links
- [ ] (Scaled) merge referring-domain CSVs, COUNTIF, dedupe, sort
- [ ] Tag categories; note HOW each category links
- [ ] For local: run the city x service and city x niche matrices
- [ ] Turn each category into an outreach play; reuse across clients

## Data points & benchmarks
- Scaled process time estimate: **2-4 hours**
- Local cleanup filter: excluding high-authority domains (roughly 50+) removes most directories/aggregators
- Common local link categories observed: 14 patterns (colleges → job postings)

## Mistakes to avoid
- Only looking at link OVERLAP — unique per-competitor assets (tools, studies) get missed
- Analyzing only direct competitors in weak local markets — stronger markets elsewhere reveal untapped opportunity categories
- Keeping directory/aggregator "best-of" sites in local analysis — filter them out
- Forgetting that findings are reusable — agencies should systematize across same-area clients

## Key principles
- Links many competitors share are likely the links helping them rank — prioritize those.
- Local sites link to local sites: mine same-city overlaps, not just same-niche.
- The categorization step turns raw data into strategy — patterns become campaigns.

## ARTICLE earn-more-links

### How to Earn More Links: Add Context to Content Analysis

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
"Most-linked content" lists are dominated by mediocre posts on big sites (native audience, fast rankings, huge social, paid distribution) while great posts on small sites under-appear. Fix: score each post as the PERCENTAGE of its site's total referring domains it earned — the "overachievers" reveal the topics that win on merit, and almost nobody else analyzes this way.

## Framework / Step-by-step
The analogy: ranking players by total wins puts bit-part role players on great teams above all-time greats; switching to winning percentage fixes the list. Content analysis has the identical flaw when ranked by raw link counts.

**Method A — find overachieving content by topic:**
1. Search a topic in a content research database (one query returned ~77,000 pages; the top 10 were all very high-authority sites (roughly 77-90+ on the tool's scale) — several generic few-hundred-word posts)
2. Tailor the dataset with multi-metric floors: recent (last ~2 years), your language, shares ≥ 10, domain authority ≥ 15, referring domains ≥ 5, organic traffic ≥ 30 (→ ~900 pages). Multiple simultaneous floors filter spam — faking ONE metric is easy; faking several is hard
3. Pull domain-level metrics for every URL (a bulk URL-profiling tool or API)
4. Compute context: referring domains to URL ÷ referring domains to entire site = the post's share of the site's links
5. Sort by share. The overachiever list's average domain authority drops (one case: 90 → 56), and topic conclusions change: the raw list suggested "how to save for retirement"; the overachiever list revealed "average net worth" as the differentiated play

**Method B — find overachieving content by competitor (when topic is unknown):**
1. Identify competitors broadly (anyone competing for your audience's attention): use a competing-domains view or the domains ranking for your head keywords
2. Export each competitor's top content (prefix mode if content lives on a subdomain)
3. Merge exports, tag rows by company, compute each post's share: `=post RD / SUMIF(all rows for that site)` (works for social shares too)
4. Sort by share — small-site winners surface: for one custom-apparel store, the raw list pointed to a big brand's mega-listicles; the overachiever list revealed corporate outings/team-building content as the real link-earning niche
- Works even comparing only large sites against each other (recipe publishers example: overachievers per brand)

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Pull your topic's page list; apply the six floors (recency, language, shares, domain authority, RD, traffic)
- [ ] Export; VLOOKUP domain-level RD from a bulk URL-profiling tool
- [ ] Add the URL-RD-to-site-RD share column; sort by it
- [ ] Analyze overachievers' common words/phrases for topic strategy
- [ ] Repeat per competitor when the topic isn't obvious (SUMIF version)
- [ ] Set your own "win" floor (5 RDs? 10?) to filter noise before crowning overachievers

## Data points & benchmarks
- One raw topic export: 77,000 pages, top 10 all very high authority (77-90+ on the tool's scale), several near-contentless
- After flooring: ~900 relevant pages (~1% of raw)
- Raw-list average authority score 90 vs overachiever-list average 56 (same topic)
- A 900-word generic post on a major publisher earned 51 RDs; a 5,000-word comprehensive post on a mid-size site earned 21 — the latter is the learning opportunity
- Big-site "top posts" often account for ~0.03% of their site's total links — they aren't game-changers even for their own sites

## Mistakes to avoid
- Skyscraping the raw "most linked" list — you're copying domain power, not content lessons
- Single-metric filtering — one faked metric slips through; several together don't
- Comparing absolute link counts across sites of wildly different sizes
- Ignoring overachievers on small sites because their absolute numbers look small
- Defining competitors too narrowly (media sites and adjacent businesses count)

## Key principles
- Links measure content + distribution; divide out distribution to isolate content quality
- A post earning 20 links on an unknown, unsupported site outperforms one earning 50 on a giant
- Multi-metric entry bars are cheap spam insurance
- Overachiever analysis yields a dataset most competitors never look at — a durable insight edge
- Reusable across topics, competitors, and even social-share analysis

- [ ] List product categories; find 10 listicles missing you; send feedback pitches

- [ ] List stocked brands; find their stockist pages; request inclusion
- [ ] Validate and publish one statistics page; build 20-30 links to it
- [ ] Internally link statistics/content pages → category and product pages

- Easiest e-commerce links go to content and brand mentions, not product pages — then internal links move the equity

- Manufacturers want buyers to find stockists — their pages are free link inventory
- Statistics pages turn your niche knowledge into a passive link magnet

(One example: 180K mentions → 2,268 best prospects.) Set up alerts for fresh mentions.

- Believing recipients read the article you pitch — they don't
- Polishing template copy instead of improving the asset
- Trying to convert "deal-seekers" with cleverer emails

## ARTICLE enterprise-link-building

### Enterprise Link Building & The Power of Links

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Enterprises earn many links naturally but leave huge value on the table: unredirected 404s, unlinked mentions, cannibalized content, and un-coordinated teams. Enterprise link building is as much politics, prioritization, and education as it is tactics — and links demonstrably move rankings (in one controlled experiment, disavowing good links caused traffic and rankings to drop).

## Framework / Step-by-step
Three organizational hurdles, then the tactic menu:

1. **Buy-in & budget.** Many companies ban employee outreach; legal/compliance review kills most projects. Get approved by framing projects in revenue terms — e.g., value each recovered referring domain using an agreed internal figure (e.g., ~$400, an illustrative budgeting assumption, not a market price): a redirect project fixing 250 dead URLs averaging 10 RDs each pitches as 250 × 10 × $400 = $800K in reclaimed value.
2. **Prioritization.** Huge site, limited team. Align link building to the products/pages company leadership already prioritizes — someone's bonus is tied to those goals, so resources follow.
3. **Coordination.** Most enterprise links happen via other teams (PR, social, paid, events, partnerships, affiliates). Train them: internal presentations, best-practice docs, internal courses. Making many teams slightly better at linking beats doing everything yourself.

Tactic menu:
- **Create linkable assets** — surveys, studies, tools/calculators, awards, definitive guides, coined terms, infographics, map-o-graphics; quote industry-famous executives. High-ranking pages attract more links over time (the "vicious circle of SEO").
- **Combine similar content** — fix cannibalization by merging near-duplicate pages into one comprehensive guide/pillar and redirecting; stronger single page ranks and attracts links better.
- **Promote selectively** — ask other teams to push only your best assets; request everything and they'll push nothing.
- **Convert unlinked brand mentions** — enterprises get mentioned constantly; also check mentions of key employees, their famous quotes, and your study statistics.
- **Reclaim lost links** — an older industry study found ~two-thirds of links to pages on the web disappeared within nine years. Find 404s with backlinks (top pages by links filtered to 404, sorted by referring domains), redirect to current equivalents. Automate the pipeline: flag URLs with >3 RDs or >5 monthly hits for redirect. Keep the redirects for at least a year, and indefinitely while the old URL still earns links or traffic.
- **Copy competitor links** — link intersect is noisy at scale; instead study competitors' top pages by links and site structure (sorted by referring domains) to see which formats win links in your niche, then build your version.
- **Build internal links** — powerful and cheap but politically slow when sections have different owners. Use a crawler's internal-link-opportunity and anchor-text exports to fix generic anchors ("learn more", "click here").
- **Cross-link owned sites** — if the company runs multiple websites, link naturally between relevant pages; fine in moderation, but large-scale cross-linking just to pass ranking signals can be treated as a link scheme.
- **Acquire websites** — acquiring a real business inherits its content and links; consolidate both into your strongest pages. Buying expired or lapsed domains purely to pass ranking signals violates spam policies.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] List 3 revenue-framed project pitches (e.g., redirect recovery valued at $400/RD)
- [ ] Map which internal teams touch channels that earn links; book training slots
- [ ] Align target pages with leadership's prioritized products
- [ ] Run a 404 + referring domains export; build the redirect queue (automate thresholds)
- [ ] Audit unlinked mentions of brand, execs, quotes, and study stats
- [ ] Identify cannibalizing page clusters; merge into pillars with redirects
- [ ] Pull competitors' top pages by links; list linkable asset formats to replicate
- [ ] Review internal anchor text; replace generic anchors on key pages
- [ ] Inventory sister sites for natural cross-links

## Data points & benchmarks
- Disavowing good links in one controlled experiment → pages lost both rankings and traffic (recovered when the disavow was removed)
- ~2/3 of links to pages on the web disappeared over a nine-year period (older link rot study)
- Budget math that works: 250 redirects × 10 RDs × $400/RD = $1M pitched value (the $400 is an illustrative internal assumption)
- Redirect value can take a while to consolidate; keep redirects at least a year

## Mistakes to avoid
- Sending link projects through legal/compliance unnecessarily — approval odds collapse
- Working on pages nobody in leadership cares about (no buy-in, no budget)
- Asking promo teams to amplify everything — selectivity is the price of support
- Treating redirect fixes as one-off cleanup instead of an automated, recurring process
- Ignoring internal links because ownership is fragmented

## Key principles
- Links feed trust systems (PageRank-style signals); proven ranking impact, even for big brands
- In enterprises, influence multiplies link output more than direct outreach (which is often banned)
- Redirect reclamation is the highest-ROI, least political link "building" there is
- Consolidation beats duplication: one strong page collects links many weak ones split
- Rank high → attract links → rank higher: linkable assets compound

4. **Outreach** — find authors/websites writing about such events (content research tool, query like `"SEO" AND ("conferences" OR "events")`, in-title mode); pitch why their readers benefit
5. **Promote** — social events pages, profile links, email list, plus a "coming soon" teaser landing page to collect pre-launch links
6. **Paid social in the final days** — social activity before an event rivals activity during it; boost anticipation for last-minute registrations

## Actionable checklist

- [ ] Add a "sponsored events" page with contact info for organizers
- [ ] For hosted events: build the event page with all 8 key details
- [ ] Add event schema (JSON-LD); validate it with Google's Rich Results Test
- [ ] Submit listings to 3-5 event platforms
- [ ] Build an outreach list of event-covering authors; send benefit-led pitches
- [ ] Launch teaser page + social/email promotion
- [ ] Schedule paid social pushes for the week before the event

- Event structured data case (one platform's own report): ~100% increase in typical YoY growth of Google Search traffic for a major event platform
- Social posting split around events: ~40% of posts happen BEFORE the event vs. ~42% during — pre-event promotion matters almost as much as live coverage
- Sponsor links sit on high-authority, topically (and often locally) relevant pages

## Mistakes to avoid
- Sponsoring anything and everything instead of quality events with real media pull
- Limiting sponsorships strictly to your own industry
- Listing only on one big event platform and ignoring other listing sites
- Publishing event pages without structured data (missing event search experiences)
- Starting promotion on event day instead of weeks before

## Key principles
- Hosted-event links are earned and editorial — low risk, hard for competitors to replicate; sponsorship links are paid placements, so expect (and accept) sponsored/nofollow tagging
- Sponsor = fast, host = scalable link surface (listings + press + local links)
- Popularity compounds: events that attract media and influencer links pass more equity
- Local events feed local SEO (geographic relevance for local queries)
- Inbound beats outbound: a sponsorship page brings events to you

## ARTICLE find-backlinks

### How to Find Backlinks (That You Can Replicate)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
The most reliable way to find links you can actually get: research where competitors got theirs — page by page, not domain-wide — filter the list down to in-content editorial links, then work out *why* each link was given. Only links you understand (and can legitimately replace or complement) are worth pursuing.

## Framework / Step-by-step
1. **Identify competitors** — the sites already ranking for the queries you want. Find more via a tool's competing-domains view (works best for established sites).
2. **Find a relevant page with lots of backlinks.** Always research at PAGE level:
   - For new linkable content: pull a competitor's most-linked pages (top pages by links, live pages only, restricted to their blog section) and study which formats win. Example found: a competitor page with 2,500+ backlinks from 1,021 referring domains.
   - For existing content: check backlink counts for the top 10 ranking pages at once (a SERP overview), or search a content research tool for your topic and sort by referring domains.
   - You don't need to clone the page — same asset type with a different angle (new study population, deeper subtopic) attracts the same linkers.
3. **Filter and sort the page's backlinks.** Apply: your language, followed links only, blog platform (≈ in-content editorial links), then sort by referring-page traffic high→low. Worked example: 4,485 links filtered down to 795 worth vetting.
4. **Classify why each link exists** — the four common reasons:
   - **Recommendation** (contextual endorsement in the flow of an article) — best links, hardest to replicate. Legitimate swap pitches: the linked resource is broken, outdated/inaccurate, or drastically worse than yours. Otherwise do a "soft pitch": point out your article's unique angle without asking for a link; aim to start a relationship.
   - **Source link** (quoting a statistic, embedding a graphic, citing a definition or idea) — replicate with a newer statistic, a better graphic, a clearer definition. Smarter: if many of a page's links share one cause (e.g., an embeddable image), build that element into your asset.
   - **Existing relationship** — spot repeat linkers in the competitor's referring-domains list sorted by number of links (e.g., one fan site linking 2,500+ times). These prolific linkers are the people to genuinely build relationships with.
   - **Paid link** — telltale signs: exact-match anchors, "guest post"-style placement, few other outbound links on the page, shoehorned context. Don't replicate. If a site asks you for money, blacklist it and move on.
5. **When context is unclear** ("here" as anchor), open the linking page and find the anchor — the real reason usually becomes obvious.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] List 3-5 real SERP competitors; pull each one's most-linked blog pages
- [ ] Pick the page you want to "inherit" links from (similar topic + strong RD count)
- [ ] Filter its links: language + followed + blog/in-content, sort by page traffic
- [ ] Tag every vetted link: recommendation / source / relationship / paid
- [ ] For source links: note the recurring cited element; add a better one to your page
- [ ] For recommendation links: soft-pitch your unique angle, no link ask
- [ ] Blacklist any domain that demands payment
- [ ] Find topics needing few links: filter a content tool for pages with 500+ monthly visits and fewer than 5 referring domains

## Data points & benchmarks
- Example filter effect: 4,485 links → 795 after language/dofollow/blog filters
- Example linkable page: 2,500+ backlinks from 1,021 referring domains
- Paid placements are costly (in older outreach studies, paid niche edits averaged a few hundred dollars each) and violate spam policies unless tagged rel="sponsored"/nofollow — why paid-link prospects aren't worth chasing
- A ~1B-page study (older) showed a positive correlation between referring domains and number of keywords ranked (correlation, not proof of causation)
- Low-competition screen: pages with 500+ monthly visits and <5 referring domains = rankable without link building

## Mistakes to avoid
- Analyzing a competitor's domain-wide links instead of page-level links — you learn almost nothing replicable
- Blanket "I published a similar guide, please link to it" outreach to every linker — low conversion, burns prospects and bridges
- Asking for like-for-like swaps without a compelling reason (broken/outdated/clearly better)
- Wasting outreach cycles on paid-link placements you mistake for earned ones
- Faking relationships purely to extract links — build real ones with repeat linkers

## Key principles
- Replicability is decided by WHY a link was given, not by where it sits.
- In-content editorial links on blogs are the prize; everything else filters out first.
- Prioritize high-traffic referring pages: suspected stronger signals, more referral traffic, and more future linkers see you.
- Great links usually come from relationships, which are a long-term play — there is no legitimate shortcut.

## ARTICLE fix-broken-links

### How to Find and Fix Broken Links (to Reclaim Link Equity)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Two separate problems: broken links ON your site (internal and outgoing) that waste equity and hurt UX, and broken backlinks TO your site (dead inbound links) whose equity "spills" on a 404. Fix outgoing links by replacing/removing; fix broken backlinks with a priority ladder ending in 301 redirects to relevant pages. One audited site had 80 referring domains pointing at a single dead URL.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Part 1 — Broken links on your site:**
- Causes: link rot (the site you linked to deleted its page) or your own URL typos
- Find: crawl with a site audit tool or desktop crawler (a broken-external-links view works for small sites; full crawl for larger ones); check internal pages with 4XX status filters; the Page indexing report in Search Console also lists "Not found (404)" URLs Google has seen
- Fix: replace with a live equivalent (use a web archive to identify what the old target was) or remove the link outright. Tedious by design — delegate to a VA.

**Part 2 — Broken backlinks to your site:**
- Causes: you deleted/moved a page that had links, or the linking site made a URL typo (e.g., a stray bracket inside the href)
- Find: a backlink tool's top pages by links, filtered to 404 and sorted by referring domains (prioritizes the biggest leaks); a broken-backlinks view sorted by linking-page strength also works
- Fix, in priority order:
  1. **Outreach** — if the linker's typo caused it, ask them to correct the URL
  2. **Redirect to the new location** — if you moved the page (find it via `site:yourdomain.com "page title"`)
  3. **Recreate the content** at the broken URL
  4. **Redirect to the most similar relevant page** (find via `site:yourdomain.com + topic`) — the usual fix, since pages are usually deleted for a reason
  5. **Leave it as a hard 404** — acceptable last resort
- Recovering what a dead page WAS: web archive first; fallback = anchor/surrounding text of its backlinks; read a few linking articles for context
- Rules: redirects must preserve relevance (anchors describe the old page); never bulk-redirect dead pages to the homepage (search engines treat it as soft-404 behavior); 301s pass page-level equity; never serve soft 404s (200-status "not found" pages)
- Make it recurring: scheduled crawls as part of routine audits — new breakage accumulates constantly

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Crawl the site; export all 4XX internal + external links
- [ ] Fix or remove every broken outgoing link (delegate if large)
- [ ] Export 404 pages with backlinks, sorted by referring domains
- [ ] For each: typo outreach → moved-page redirect → recreate → similar-page redirect → hard 404
- [ ] Verify no soft 404s and no homepage bulk-redirects
- [ ] Schedule automated re-crawls monthly/quarterly

## Data points & benchmarks
- One popular blog's single dead URL still held 80 referring domains — pure evaporating equity
- 301 redirects pass page-level equity (confirmed by Google representatives); keep them in place for at least a year, longer while the old URL still gets links or traffic
- Redirect relevance matters: anchor text of old links should still make sense on the redirect target

## Mistakes to avoid
- Confusing this with broken link BUILDING (exploiting competitors' dead pages — different playbook)
- Redirecting dead pages to the homepage "to be safe" — worst target, treated as soft 404
- Serving pretty 404 pages with 200 status codes (classic soft 404)
- Fixing once and never again — link rot is continuous
- Deleting/moving URLs without checking their backlink counts first

## Key principles
- Broken backlinks are free equity you already earned — reclaim before you build new
- Match the fix to the cause: their typo → outreach; your move → redirect; your deletion → recreate or re-route
- Relevance beats convenience when choosing redirect targets
- Hard 404 is honest; soft 404 confuses crawlers and users
- Treat link hygiene as scheduled maintenance, not a project

6. **Reputation.** Testimonials from recognizable industry professionals (not anonymous nobodies), case studies with known brands, awards. Go beyond their site: check social, community forums, and ex-employee reviews.
7. **Pricing.** Expensive ≠ better; geography legitimately shifts rates. ### How To Get Backlinks: Add, Ask, Earn, Buy

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Every link building tactic falls into exactly four buckets: Add, Earn, Ask, Buy. Adding links yourself is worthless; earning them via remarkable assets is best; asking works only with a genuine exchange of value; buying is risky and mostly discouraged. Start by reverse-engineering competitors' links (homepage links, most-linked pages, and links to pages you want to outrank).

## Framework / Step-by-step
The 4-bucket model (from interviews with dozens of practitioners):
1. **Add** — place links yourself on sites that allow user-generated content (forums, comments). Easy but near-worthless; usually nofollow'd; auto-plugin backlinks are pure spam.
2. **Earn** — create something so notable people link without being asked: industry surveys (ego bait), free online tools, original research, thorough tutorials, news. Best links, hardest to get.
3. **Ask** — outreach. Works only as an exchange of value: guest articles (fair trade: content for link), link exchanges (risky at scale, "link spam" per Google guidelines), or a truly outstanding resource.
4. **Buy** — risky; this library advises against it. If you do pay for anything link-related: never buy from spam email sellers; the least risky "bought" links come as side benefits of real business interactions (e.g., event sponsorship), and any paid link should be rel="sponsored" or nofollow. Safer alternative: pay agencies/freelancers to execute legit tactics.

3-step competitive research process to start:
1. **Study competitors' homepage links** — in a backlink analysis tool, list the backlinks to the exact homepage URL. Find patterns (e.g., integration listings) you can replicate.
2. **Study competitors' most linked pages** — view their top pages by links. Free tools and research studies top the list; if competitors have linkable assets and you don't, they earn links "on autopilot" while you hustle.
3. **Study pages you want to outrank** — check the backlinks of the top-ranking pages for your target keyword; infer the tactic that earned links (e.g., ego-bait awards) and replicate it.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Classify every planned tactic as Add / Earn / Ask / Buy; drop pure "Add" tactics
- [ ] Build at least one linkable asset (free tool, original-data study, or thorough tutorial)
- [ ] Run competitor homepage backlink analysis for integration/listing opportunities
- [ ] Run "top-pages-by-links" on 3-5 competitors; identify asset gaps
- [ ] Reverse-engineer backlinks of pages outranking yours for target keywords
- [ ] If outreach fails, ask linkers of competitor pages directly why they linked
- [ ] If outsourcing, demand past work examples + client references

## Data points & benchmarks
- 111 personalized outreach emails (strong content, strong brand) → 19 links = **17% success rate**; anything above **10%** is considered amazing
- Expect roughly **5 links per 100 emails** for an unknown site using "my page is awesome" pitches (a rough figure from one practitioner's experience)
- Average price of a decent bought backlink: **$200–300** (older industry poll; prices drift); $3/link = spam neighborhood → penalty risk
- Ego-bait example: one industry survey report earned links from **571 websites** including several big-brand homepages

## Mistakes to avoid
- Buying links from "quality high-authority backlinks" spam emails
- Scaling guest posts with cheap/AI-written content — nobody will publish it
- Scaling link exchanges (even three-way/indirect) — Google link spam penalty risk
- Believing your outreach success is all skill — brand affinity does heavy lifting (a no-name site gets 2-3x lower rates)
- Buying 1,000 links/month at $3 each — a likely penalty (one reported case: traffic collapsed)

## Key principles
- "The strategies are secondary. What comes first is your understanding of why people link, and how to persuade them to link to you."
- Link building is dealing with humans, not hacking a technical process — deserve the link.
- A link request must be an exchange of value; "my content is awesome" alone is asking for a favor.
- If a site easily sells you a followed link (price list ready), that's a red flag — editorial sites don't sell untagged links.

## ARTICLE international-link-building

### International Link Building: 9 Surprising Aspects

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Non-English markets play by different rules: paid links and paid guest placements are far more common (and often untagged), large sites sell formal "media packages", followed links can be rare due to local penalty history, and outreach medium/formality varies by culture. Success = local experts, local language, and local channels. Local norms don't change Google's rules: untagged paid followed links can trigger manual actions anywhere.

## Framework / Step-by-step
The 9 aspects (from practitioners across 20+ markets in Europe, Asia, and South America):

1. **Buying links is very common.** Fewer prospecting options in smaller markets make paid links tempting: older surveys report 31% of SEOs and up to 74% of link builders buy links, and practitioners report it is more open in many non-English markets. Most bought links are NOT tagged sponsored/nofollow (against Google's guidelines); in some markets you pay EXTRA for a followed link — a risk you should not take on.
2. **Guest posting is often paid placement.** Outreach with "paid post" in the subject line converts at 20%+ in some markets, including high-authority sites — but paid placements must be tagged rel="sponsored" or nofollow to stay within guidelines. Free guest posting splits into: prove niche credibility, or use UGC sections of major media (nofollowed, but exposure and trust signals still pay).
3. **Large sites sell "media packages."** Formal offers with strict terms: link live for 1 year only, sponsored tag, nofollow, no homepage/category placement, prepayment, ~1-month turnaround. Largely guideline-compliant (white hat) but limited value.
4. **Relationships are massive leverage.** Small markets = everyone knows everyone. Three-way (ABC) link exchanges are widespread in some regions (site owners often run multiple sites) — still classified as link schemes. Personal relationships with senior editors land top-tier coverage (media visits work best). Hiring local link builders with existing connections beats foreign skill.
5. **Followed links are a miracle in some countries.** Germany and Austria: practitioners report many sites still nofollow external links by default — reportedly a legacy of a major 2014 link penalty wave.
6. **TLD choice affects link building.** In South America, a gTLD (.com) earns cross-border links far more easily than a ccTLD (a Peruvian site will more readily link to example.com than example.ar). Add authority consolidation on one domain and simpler management: a gTLD is often a good fit for international expansion (though a ccTLD can be a stronger local-trust signal in some markets, so weigh both).
7. **Localize language AND register.** Write outreach in the local language except where English is standard (e.g., multilingual countries, agency deals). Formality norms differ sharply: formal in the Balkans, casual-OK in Czechia/Slovakia, honorific complexity in Japan/Korea, casual-friendly in Romance-language countries. Learn local terms and spellings.
8. **SEO knowledge varies by country.** Advanced markets (e.g., Poland, Czechia, Slovakia) understand jargon like "authority-40 3-way exchange"; moderate markets tolerate some SEO terms; low-knowledge markets (e.g., Balkans, Germany) — assume no SEO literacy and pitch value, not metrics.
9. **Email isn't always the best medium.** Indonesia: email for intro, then messaging apps. Thailand/Vietnam: email barely works — use professional social messaging. China: messenger apps, often voice notes. Germany/Poland: phone calls can outperform everything.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Map the target market: is paid placement the norm? What do links cost locally?
- [ ] Decide policy on paid/media-package links (disclosure, nofollow expectations)
- [ ] Hire or partner with a local link builder with existing relationships
- [ ] Check followed-link norms for the market before setting link specs
- [ ] Choose gTLD vs ccTLD strategy before cross-border outreach
- [ ] Localize outreach: native writer, correct formality, local terminology
- [ ] Test the local channel mix (email vs. messenger vs. phone) in the first 50 pitches
- [ ] Gauge recipient SEO literacy; adjust pitch language accordingly

## Data points & benchmarks
- 31% of SEOs / up to 74% of link builders admitted buying links (two older industry surveys)
- In one older price study, the average paid-link price exceeded €100 in every monitored European country; some markets (e.g., Austria) ran high from limited site supply
- "Paid post" outreach in one Asian market: 20%+ conversion including authority sites
- Typical media package terms: 1-year link lifespan, nofollow, no homepage placement, prepay, ~1 month turnaround

## Mistakes to avoid
- Copying English-market playbooks (link bait at scale often isn't viable in small markets)
- Assuming local disclosure norms protect you — many paid links abroad are sold untagged, and untagged paid followed links put YOU at risk of a manual action
- Using English or the wrong formality register in outreach
- Pitching SEO jargon to non-SEO webmasters
- Insisting on email where the market lives on messaging apps
- Skipping local hires and burning months rebuilding relationships locals already have

## Key principles
- Market size shapes tactic viability: small pools push markets toward paid links
- Local relationships are the moat; hire the network, not just the skill
- Penalty history leaves cultural scars (nofollow-only markets) — know the market's history
- A gTLD can lower friction for cross-border link acquisition in some regions (not a universal rule)
- Universals still apply: great linkable assets and ego bait work everywhere

5. **Make it newsworthy** — ride what's top of mind (a remote-work survey during the shift earned 103 links from 80 sites plus major press) or newsjack live stories with expert commentary.
6. **Make it a story** — humans think in stories; a couple's personal debt story out-linked any logical debt lecture (85 links from 62 domains, including major media).
7. **Include what people already link to** — mine a competing page's backlink anchors for the recurring reason people link (e.g., "benefits," "definition"), then bake that element into your version.

**Find ideas:** pull a competitor's most-linked pages; note the dominant format in your niche (listicles in design, data studies in SEO) and build your own at that standard.

- [ ] Choose one share trigger as the design spine (practical, emotional, story...)
- [ ] If data-led: create a striking, quotable statistic your industry can reuse in pitches
- [ ] If tool-led: free, no email-wall, 10x better than the current options
- [ ] Add one visual or interactive element (map, animated diagram, quiz)

- [ ] Budget as much for promotion as production
- [ ] For index/ranking studies: update annually to re-trigger viral coverage

- Opinionated rant post: 2,500 backlinks / 533 domains

## Mistakes to avoid
- Publishing link bait without a promotion plan — discovery never happens by accident
- Imitating a format after its moment has passed (timing was decisive in several wins)
- Faking depth or fabricating data — the winning creators deliberately "designed for the toughest crowd first" and sourced everything
- Treating these outliers as typical: most link bait earns far fewer links than the examples above
- Skipping the update cycle on research that went viral once (annual refreshes re-trigger links)
- Forgetting the audience must be people who CAN link (bloggers/journalists), not just buyers

## Key principles
- People link to content that makes THEIR point better — striking stats, embeddable visuals, definitive resources.
- Original research is the most reliably linkable format; a single reusable number can carry thousands of links.
- Novel format × hot topic is the viral recipe; evergreen format compounds longer.

## TL;DR

3. **Narrow the prospect list.** From ~1,986 raw URLs: deduplicate domains, drop nofollow/UGC/sponsored linkers, remove sites without meaningful traffic (batch traffic lookups) → 902 vetted prospects.

5. **Find contacts and vet.** Scrape author names (~741 from 902 URLs), run them through an email-finder tool (~60% hit rate → 452), verify deliverability (168 valid / 92 catch-all / 178 dead), manually vet pages a few hours/week → 515 sites ready to pitch.

[Custom pitch: that stat is X years old; newer research says Y]. I published this and other fresh stats here: [link]. Not sure if you're actively editing posts, but might be worth an update if you are? No pressure." Write one custom pitch paragraph per statistic (8 in this campaign) and merge.

- [ ] Build the stat-by-stat sheet (link counts, ages, replacements, pitch angle)
- [ ] Clean prospect list: dedupe, followed-links-only, traffic floor
- [ ] Publish page: proven stats updated, categorized, top-stats section
- [ ] Automate contacts: scrape → email finder → verifier → manual catch-alls
- [ ] Write one pitch paragraph per statistic; merge and send
- [ ] Reply to every response; follow up once with promising non-responders; decline payment demands and link swaps

## Data points & benchmarks

- Blasting every linker of a stats page with "ours is better, swap the link" — the win is the specific outdated/missing statistic

- Stats pages are link magnets: journalists cite them constantly and rarely re-check

- Segment by statistic, personalize the pitch paragraph, keep the ask soft

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

- Google actively works to "neutralize the impact of unnatural links" — intentional links only

- Using metrics alone to qualify sites — manipulated authority scores are common among link sellers

- Mixing prospect types across tactics (guest post lists ≠ skyscraper lists)
- Pitching irrelevant sites because competitors link there — relevance still rules

- "Without prospecting, every other aspect will likely fall to pieces."

## ARTICLE link-reclamation

### Link Reclamation: How to Find and Reclaim Lost Backlinks

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Hard-earned links disappear constantly (most big sites lose thousands monthly). Reclamation = finding lost links in a backlink analysis tool and acting on the ones worth saving — but only the right loss reasons. "Link removed" and accidentally deleted pages are pursuable; redirects, canonicals, noindexes, crawl errors and "dropped" mostly aren't. One case study: 31 links from 166 emails = 18.67% conversion (though those links were originally built through exchanges or paid deals, so don't expect that rate for earned links, and don't repeat the paid approach without sponsored/nofollow tagging).

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Step 1 — Find lost links**: in a backlink analysis tool, open your domain's lost-links list (past 30 days). Filter to the best links only (e.g., followed, authority 30+, traffic thresholds, language) — one large SEO site: 6,285 lost → 594 worth review.

**Step 2 — Triage by loss reason:**
- **Link removed (OFTEN WORTH PURSUING)** — compare the page's HTML before and after (a web archive or a tool's page-diff view):
  - Complete content rewrite (lots of red/green) → check if your link fits the refresh; pitch re-addition
  - Link replaced by competitor → ask why, get feedback, improve content (may win reinstatement; prevents future losses)
  - Mass external-link removal = new links policy → don't pitch
- **Not found / 404 (SOMETIMES)** — page deleted. If it has many backlinks (e.g., 109 RDs), deletion was probably accidental → "noticed your page is broken, did you mean to delete it?" email. Better: broken link building — recreate the dead page and pitch all its linkers.
- **Broken redirect (RARELY)** — usually your own intended changes. Real fix: top pages by links + 404 filter → 301 your own dead pages with backlinks to relevant pages.
- **Noindex (RARELY)** — check with a browser extension or view-source; reach out only if accidental (homepage also noindexed = sitewide accident; page shows SEO effort = likely accident). They'll usually self-fix.
- **Not canonical anymore / 301-302 redirects (RARELY)** — usually HTTPS/URL-standardization; link survives on the destination. Check the destination's source code for yourdomain.com (a spreadsheet or script can bulk-check this); pursue only unlinked mentions or clear value-add opportunities on the destination.
- **Crawl error / Dropped (NEVER)** — reappear next crawl or link still exists.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Monthly: review the lost-links list with quality + language filters
- [ ] Diff the page before/after for every "link removed" to classify: rewrite / replaced / policy
- [ ] Pitch re-addition where the refresh still warrants your link
- [ ] Ask for feedback when outplaced by a competitor; fix the content gap
- [ ] Check 404 referring pages' own RD counts — accidental deletions get a friendly heads-up email
- [ ] Fix own-site dead pages: top pages by links + 404 → 301 redirect
- [ ] Skip crawl errors, dropped, and routine canonical/redirect churn
- [ ] Track reclamation conversion separately — it's your cheapest link source

## Data points & benchmarks
- One case study: **31 links reclaimed / 166 emails = 18.67%** conversion (links originally built via "win-win collaborations," i.e., paid/exchanged, so not representative of earned links)
- One large SEO site lost **6,285 links in 30 days**; only **594** survived the quality filter (~9.5%)
- Accidental-deletion signal example: dead page still holding **109 RDs**

## Mistakes to avoid
- Trying to reclaim links lost for good reason (policy bans, intentional redirects)
- Confusing unlinked-mention claiming with reclamation — "you can't reclaim a link you never had"
- Wasting time on crawl errors and "dropped" links — they self-resolve
- Pitching re-addition without checking whether the refreshed content still fits your link

## Key principles
- Reclamation is often easier, faster, and cheaper than building new links — consider reallocating resources.
- Diagnose the loss reason before writing any email.
- Lost-link reports are also a feedback loop: content that gets replaced tells you where to improve.

## TL;DR
People rarely link to commercial pages — you need content deliberately built to attract links ("linkable assets"). Six proven types: infographics/graphics, online tools & calculators, awards & rankings, studies & research, definitive guides, coined terms. Assets don't attract links out of thin air — each type has its own promotion playbook.

- Highest ceiling (one headline-analyzer tool: 16K+ links/3.6K RDs; one color-palette tool: 154K links/5K RDs) but highest barrier (need dev skills/budget)

- Original data attracts links "like CRAZY" (4 of the 5 most-linked pages on one large SEO blog are studies); personal case studies work if you lack data
- Earn links: (a) re-conduct outdated studies and steal their links (one outdated ranking-factors study: 68K links/5K RDs, never updated — a content research tool: `[keyword] "study"/"survey"/"research"` sorted by RDs); (b) find pages asserting claims your data proves/disproves → pitch your study (worked: link added 2 days later)

**Type 5 — "Definitive" guides & tutorials**
- Experts only; bloggers/journalists prefer linking to ONE definitive resource
- Earn links: (a) update + re-promote on a schedule — email list (one brand used ~46K subscribers), a few hundred dollars of social ads, fresh outreach round each update → visible RD spike per refresh; (b) steal links from less-definitive guides: backlink tool → export referring domains of the weaker guides ranking for your topic → "just found your post, we published a more comprehensive guide" (no hard sell)

**Type 6 — Coined terms**
- Hardest to pull off, biggest passive payoff (one famous named technique still draws 50-100 new RDs/month years later)
- Earn links: (a) mention alerts for RELATED terms (people mentioning the term itself usually already link); (b) popularize the term via guest posts on high-traffic blogs (find via `author:"Name" -site:theirsite.com` in a content research tool)

- [ ] Schedule periodic updates + re-promotion for guides
- [ ] Notify every featured person/brand when awards/lists publish

- "SEO infographic" prospect search: ~130 active publisher sites in 12 months
- Famous coined-term page: 50-100 new RDs/month passively

- Creating linkable assets without a promotion plan — "assets don't attract links out of thin air"
- Using infographics where the content doesn't benefit from visual format
- Running awards without existing credibility (or without the 100-expert-vote hack)
- Guest posting only for the byline link instead of popularizing your term
- Hard-selling in "better guide" outreach — inform, don't push

## Key principles
- Linkable assets have much higher ROI on promotion than commercial pages.

### 4 Reasons to Build More Links From the Same Website

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Unique referring domains matter most, but repeat links from the same website are natural (79% of referring domains linked to the same site multiple times in one older study) and valuable. Don't skip a prospect just because they linked once — additional links add page authority, hit more target pages, drive referral traffic, and are far easier to earn from existing fans.

## Framework / Step-by-step
The four reasons to keep working a domain that already links to you:
1. **Increase page authority** — PageRank flows page to page, so links from multiple pages on the same site stack authority on the linked pages (page-level authority correlates with organic traffic, though correlation is not proof of causation).
2. **Get links to more of your pages** — the referring-domains/traffic correlation is at PAGE level, and link magnets (studies, tools) are rarely the pages you need to rank. If a site that linked to your study could also link to your money page, pitch it — internal links distribute some authority, but direct links are better.
3. **Get more referral traffic** — one study drew 1,200+ referring domains in 36 months (~34/month) while getting almost no organic traffic: linkers discovered it from OTHER linking pages with traffic (4 of the 6 highest-traffic referring pages came from a single domain). Traffic compounds links. Workflow: find your best referral sources in analytics (sort by conversions/users) → check their top-traffic pages → pitch a contextual link where your resource fits.
4. **Build links more easily** — repeat linkers are warm. Find them: export your backlinks, group by referring domain, followed only, and sort by link count (e.g., a fan site with 10 links to your posts). When you publish something relevant, share it with them directly — or use `site:theirsite.com [topic]` searches to find contextual placement opportunities and pitch those.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Stop filtering prospects by "already has one link from us"
- [ ] Segment referring domains by link count; build a "serial linkers" list
- [ ] For each serial linker, match a NEW target page (not the one they already linked)
- [ ] Pull top referral sources from analytics; pitch contextual links on their top-traffic pages
- [ ] Note the context of each previous link in your CRM/spreadsheet and reference it in pitches
- [ ] Give serial linkers special, non-generic outreach — they're fans, treat them like partners

## Data points & benchmarks
- 79.23% of referring domains linked to the same website more than once in one older study; most linked 2-5 times
- Caution: repeated links should be earned editorially; recurring paid or swapped links from one site are a link-scheme risk
- Case: a single data study earned 1,200+ referring domains over 36 months (~34/month) with near-zero organic traffic of its own — discovery happened via referring pages' traffic
- Page-level authority metric correlates with organic search traffic (public PageRank scores discontinued in 2016)

## Mistakes to avoid
- Assuming multiple links from one domain is unnatural or wasteful — the data says it's the norm
- Always pointing new prospects at the same linkbait page instead of pages you want to rank
- Sending your standard mass-pitch template to serial linkers — they've earned personal outreach
- Ignoring referral traffic value because a link is "only" from an already-linking domain

## Key principles
- Referring domains are the KPI; repeat links from quality domains are the compounding interest.
- Links do three jobs at once — authority, distribution to the right pages, and discovery by future linkers.
- Your warmest audience is people who already linked to you; mine them before cold prospects.
- Traffic on the linking page is a link multiplier: eyeballs today, backlinks tomorrow.

### Listicle Outreach: How One Brand Built 96 Links in 3 Months (Case Study)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Getting your product into "best X" and "X alternatives" listicles is a repeatable, high-yield link tactic: one B2B SaaS brand earned 96 links from 54 domains, 33 new listicle mentions, and 17 position upgrades in under 3 months with a single person running the process. ## Framework / Step-by-step
1. **Find relevant listicles.** Target three types: (a) "best options" lists, (b) "alternatives" lists, (c) guides that contain tool/resource lists. Brainstorm seed keywords and combine with listicle modifiers: *best, top, review, list, tools, free* (e.g., "best email finder", "[competitor] alternatives"). Search each query, export the SERPs (a browser extension or keyword tool with SERP export), merge everything into one CSV.
2. **Clean and segment.** Remove duplicates and sites below authority ~30 (and optionally low-traffic pages). Manually verify remaining pages are real listicles. Then tag every prospect into one of three segments:
   - Product not mentioned at all
   - Product mentioned but not linked
   - Product mentioned but ranked below competitors
   (Speed tip: use search operators — `"your product"` vs `-"your product"` — to pre-segment while prospecting.)
3. **Find contact information.** Blog editors and content managers respond best — often the listicle's actual author. Find names on LinkedIn, then use an email-finder/enrichment tool (bulk lookup or author lookup by URL) to get addresses.

An affiliate/revenue-share offer is common, but it makes the placement commercial: the editor should disclose it and tag affiliate links rel="sponsored" (or nofollow), and the list should still rank products honestly. If their page mentions you without linking, or its info is dated and traffic is declining, say so with evidence. Two sending modes: fully manual (up to 30 min/email, >50% response rate observed) or automated with custom attributes and icebreakers. Always follow up — max two short follow-ups per prospect.
5. **Respond proactively.** Editors often ask for equal or greater value back. Tips for closing: be flexible, reply same-day, do something genuinely useful for them (share the updated article, advise in your expertise area, contribute a real guest post on its own merits), and keep following up on interested threads. Decline straight link swaps and payment for placement; reciprocal or paid links without sponsored/nofollow tags are link-scheme risks.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Build keyword × modifier query list (best/top/review/list/tools/free + "alternatives" queries)
- [ ] Export and merge SERPs into one master CSV
- [ ] Filter to authority 30+, dedupe, manually remove non-listicles
- [ ] Tag prospects: not mentioned / unlinked mention / ranked low
- [ ] Identify the listicle author or content editor for each target
- [ ] Write one value-led pitch template per segment (what THEY get)
- [ ] Send, follow up twice maximum, respond same-day to replies
- [ ] Set up an alert for newly published listicles (keyword in title, authority 30+, traffic 1,000+) to keep the campaign evergreen

## Data points & benchmarks
- Case study results (<3 months, one person): 96 links from 54 domains, 33 new mentions, 17 position upgrades
- Manual, deep-personalized emails took up to 30 minutes each but saw >50% response rate
- Prospect floor: domain authority ~30; optional traffic floor 1,000+ monthly visits
- Evergreen alerting example: new pages with "best [category]" in title, authority 30+, 1,000+ monthly search visits

## Mistakes to avoid
- Pitching "my tool is great, please add it" with zero value exchange
- Paying for listicle placements: pay-to-list sites don't care about quality, paid links violate search guidelines, and such sites often demand payment again to keep the placement
- Contacting generic info@ addresses instead of the author/editor
- Sending more than two follow-ups (reads as desperate) or letting replies sit for days
- Skipping segmentation — the same email to all three prospect types wastes the unlinked-mention and position-upgrade opportunities

## Key principles
- "Contact the right people, with the right offer, at the right time" — people and offer matter more than the template.
- Ask for one thing (the mention/link), give two or three things (free product access, accurate data, free labor on the update) — disclosed, and never as payment for a link.

- A listicle outreach campaign is a system, not a blast: prospect → segment → personalize → respond → monitor forever.

### 9 Easy Local Link Building Tactics

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Links are among the factors that influence local-pack rankings, and local competition is often modest — in one older study, first-page local businesses rarely exceeded ~150 referring domains. 4. **Create locally relevant content.** "Best grass for [area]", "common local pests", "[service] prices in [city]". Rarely attracts links naturally — plan to earn them via outreach.
5. **Get local citations.** Start with the major data aggregators and the big maps/review/business platforms (US examples; use your country's equivalents) since they feed data to hundreds of other sites, then expand to niche and city directories.
6. **Pursue other local opportunities:** colleges (job posts, scholarships, club sponsorships, discounts, alumni pages), city-specific sites/directories, local news/magazines/podcasts, state/regional sites, community groups, sponsorships (sports leagues, races, meetups, charities), local awards. Links given in exchange for a scholarship, discount, sponsorship, or donation are paid placements: expect or request rel="sponsored"/nofollow, and don't run scholarship programs just to farm links.
7. **Claim unlinked mentions.** Best in news-covered niches (e.g., legal) or when your team is active in the community.
8. **Acquire real businesses.** Rare but valuable: acquiring a genuine competitor or local business brings its customers, content, and links — either run both sites or consolidate with proper redirects. Buying expired or lapsed domains purely to pass ranking signals violates Google's spam policies; don't.
9. **Add internal links.** Your own site is a local site you fully control; internal links are the cheapest link tactic you have.

- [ ] Watch for local businesses that are genuinely for sale (real operating businesses only, not expired domains)
- [ ] Add internal links from high-authority local pages to money pages

- Local acquisitions are rare (a handful per career) but can be high-value when the business is real

## Mistakes to avoid
- Skipping basics to chase trendy tactics — riskier and rarely pays off locally
- Buying expired domains or paying for untagged local links — spam-policy violations
- Building links from non-local, non-relevant sites when local relevance is the point
- Ignoring citations because they're low-authority — they feed data to many other sites
- Forgetting internal links entirely even though they're fully within your control
- Not redirecting dead URLs after redesigns

## Key principles
- Local link building is a volume-of-basics game: directories, citations, reclaim, repeat
- Rank where your customers search: get listed ON the pages that rank for your terms
- Local relevance beats raw authority for map-pack performance
- Community involvement converts to links (sponsorships, schools, awards)

## ARTICLE nofollow-links

### Nofollow vs. Follow Links: Everything You Need to Know

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
A nofollow link carries a `rel="nofollow"` tag telling search engines not to pass PageRank or anchor text — so when building links, prioritize followed ones. But nofollow links still matter: they diversify your link profile, drive referral traffic that converts into followed links, and tagging paid/sponsored links (rel="sponsored", or nofollow) protects you from penalties. Audit both directions (inbound and outbound) regularly.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Core mechanics:**
- Followed: `<a href="https://example.com">text</a>` — passes PageRank + anchor text.
- Nofollowed: same HTML plus `rel="nofollow"` — "in general" not followed, no PageRank/anchor credit. (Since March 2020, Google treats nofollow as a *hint* it may use or ignore for ranking; `rel="sponsored"` is preferred for paid links and `rel="ugc"` for user-generated content.) Check any link with right-click → Inspect, or a browser extension that highlights nofollow links.

**History that explains today's rules:**
1. **2005** — nofollow introduced to kill comment spam (CMS platforms apply it to comment links by default).
2. **2009** — PageRank "sculpting" killed: a nofollowed link still consumes its share of PageRank, so nofollowing internal links no longer concentrates flow to money pages. Nofollowing internal links now only makes sense for crawl prioritization (e.g., login/register links).
3. **2013 onward** — paid-link enforcement: any paid or sponsored link should be tagged rel="sponsored" or nofollow. Search engines want to credit *earned* links, not bought ones.

**Five-step nofollow audit of your site:**
1. **Find followed backlinks with keyword-rich anchors** (an exact-match anchor pattern, e.g., 19 domains all linking with "payday loans", signals bought links). Remediate by type: bought links → request removal or nofollow, else disavow; guest-post bio keyword anchors → swap to branded; widget links → nofollow in the widget HTML; sitewide links → nofollow or branded anchor. Pro tip for large sites: export followed anchors, run them through a keyword tool, sort by CPC high-to-low — high-CPC anchors surface the spam.
2. **Find followed links from sponsored posts** — search your backlinks for "sponsored" with a follow filter; ask for nofollow.
3. **Find keyword-rich followed outbound links on your own site** — check outgoing anchors if you accept guest posts or user-generated content (one major publisher blanket-nofollowed all outbound links in 2017 after contributors sold links).
4. **Check your own sponsored posts** — `site:yourwebsite.com "sponsored post"`; verify the sponsor's link is nofollowed.
5. **Find nofollowed internal links** — only login/cart-type pages should carry them; remove nofollow from any content link that was accidentally tagged.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] When link building, filter prospects to followed links first (but don't discard strong nofollow prospects)
- [ ] Check your followed/nofollow ratio; flag 100%-followed or near it
- [ ] Tag every paid, sponsored, or affiliate link you place or sell with rel="sponsored" (or nofollow), and user-generated links with rel="ugc"
- [ ] Audit inbound followed anchors for exact-match clusters; investigate before disavowing
- [ ] Fix guest-post bio anchors (yours on other sites; others' on yours)
- [ ] Remove stray nofollow tags from internal content links
- [ ] Use nofollow on internal login/register links for crawl prioritization only

## Data points & benchmarks
- An older study of 44,589 SERPs found the correlation between rankings and *followed* backlink count was slightly weaker than for *total* backlinks — possible (not conclusive) evidence that some nofollowed links from strong pages are counted
- Rough heuristic (not a Google rule): a followed-share of roughly 60-90% of referring domains is typical; near-100% followed can look manipulated
- Common nofollow sources: social networks, forums, press releases, Q&A sites, wikis, pingbacks, directories
- PageRank splitting: 10 units + 10 outbound links = 1 unit each, regardless of nofollow on some of them

## Mistakes to avoid
- Buying followed links — competitors can report you, triggering manual review and penalties
- Disavowing or removing exact-match links without investigating context first (some occur naturally)
- Trying to sculpt internal PageRank with nofollow — obsolete since 2009
- Treating nofollow placements as worthless (traffic from them produces followed links)
- Leaving sponsored content on your own site followed (no rel="sponsored"/nofollow) — the risk runs both directions

## Key principles
- Followed links pass ranking power; nofollow links pass visibility, traffic, and profile diversity.
- A natural link profile is mixed — purity in either direction is a red flag.
- Link attributes are a disclosure device: use rel="sponsored" or nofollow honestly on anything paid, and expect the same from partners.
- Nofollowed mentions are a discovery channel: see → enjoy → recommend is how followed links actually happen.

5. **Do genuinely interesting stuff in the real world.** Sponsor a local team (sponsor links may be tagged sponsored/nofollow, which is fine), launch a quirky low-cost product stunt, host an event. These earn links and positive signals that competitors can't replicate through conventional SEO.

- [ ] Identify 3-5 niche directories/industry search engines with real traffic; get listed
- [ ] Build a promotion checklist for every new post: notify mentioned people, pitch 5 newsletters/resource pages, post a TL;DR to communities

## ARTICLE outreach

### Why Your Outreach Email Got Deleted (And How to Write Ones That Don't)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Templated outreach IS spam, and recipients can spot it instantly. Segment prospects by influence, use their real email, lead with value and specificity, time your pitch to their recent work, ask with an easy out, and follow up at most once. One practitioner doubled results (7-10 links per 50 emails vs. per 100) by applying these rules.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Segment prospects into four groups and treat each differently:**
- **Sharks** — famous names with huge audiences. Emails from strangers don't get read; only personal introductions or genuinely outstanding, creative work get through. Huge payoff if you land one.
- **Big Fish** — smaller-but-meaningful audiences. Reachable with a personal email, never a template. Don't ask them for links/tweets — ask for critique or idea validation; if your work deserves it, they'll share it anyway.
- **Small fish** — growing sites actively promoting themselves in communities and via guest posts. Your ideal outreach targets.
- **Spawn** — brand-new sites. They'll reply to anything, but their links carry almost no value. Skip.

**The 9 rules:**
1. **Use the correct email address.** Research the real address; guessed addresses landing in catch-all inboxes get ignored almost always (one practitioner's estimate: ~99%). Wrong email = "you didn't care."
2. **Think what's in it for them.** Show something genuinely new (state the actual takeaways, not "check my amazing research"), or feature them (quotes, lessons learned, results with their product) — nobody can resist content about themselves.
3. **Only pitch your best work, with social proof.** One first impression; attach evidence it's not just you who thinks it's good (comments, community upvotes, notable tweets/shares, citations). Get initial traction first, then use it.
4. **Re-think your outreach excuse.** "You wrote X, I wrote something similar" is anti-value. Use: a different opinion, the one important aspect they missed, or a better resource BECAUSE {specific reason}. This requires actually skimming their article.
5. **Templates that don't look like templates.** Short, curiosity-gap subject lines. Break automation-shaped patterns (the "I saw your post / you linked to X / mine is better / please link" skeleton). Flattery must be specific ("your advice on X is going straight into my workflow this month") or absent.
6. **Show you know them.** Reference their actual recent work and preferences ("I knew he'd just published an epic guide, so…"). Demonstrated effort obliges a reply.
7. **Timing is your friend.** Authors are most receptive right after publishing — one blogger added 22 suggested tools to a fresh post within days of launch. Old posts rarely get revisited. Set alerts for new relevant articles and reach out immediately.
8. **Ask wisely.** Never ask for tweets (it converts your email into a favor request and forces awkward refusals — good content gets shared unasked). Do ask for links, softly, with an easy out: "Maybe it's worth mentioning in your article — or in a future one?"
9. **Follow up once.** Short, polite, explicitly final ("won't bug you again"). Multiple follow-ups = you become the cautionary tale.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Bucket every prospect: Shark / Big Fish / Small fish / Spawn; target the middle two
- [ ] Manually verify each email address before sending
- [ ] Lead with 2-3 concrete takeaways of your asset
- [ ] Add one credibility proof point (traction metric or citation)
- [ ] Write a specific, non-generic hook referencing THEIR work
- [ ] Pitch only fresh articles (alerts, weekly)
- [ ] Ask for feedback or a soft link; never a tweet
- [ ] One follow-up, then stop

## Data points & benchmarks
- Applying these rules roughly doubled one practitioner's efficiency: from 7-10 links per 100 emails to 7-10 per ~50 (a single anecdote, not a benchmark)
- Guess-emails to catch-all domains: ignored ~99% of the time (practitioner estimate)
- Freshly published posts are dramatically more link-amendable than old ones (22 additions to a day-old post)

## Mistakes to avoid
- The 4-link template skeleton — instantly recognizable, instantly deleted
- Generic flattery ("great article, learned a lot") — worse than nothing
- Pitching average work to top people — burns your one first impression
- Mass-emailing the most famous people in your niche
- Asking for tweets/shares explicitly
- Endless automated follow-up chains
- Offering money or gifts for links without disclosure — paid links must be tagged rel="sponsored" or nofollow

## Key principles
- There's a human on the other side; demonstrated effort is the personalization that matters
- Value first: their audience's needs, not your link count
- Fame is inversely proportional to template tolerance
- Timing beats persuasion: catch authors while they still care about the piece
- A good ask leaves the recipient a graceful exit

## ARTICLE reciprocal-links

### Reciprocal Links: Will They Hurt Your SEO?

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Reciprocal links are a natural byproduct of the web — in older studies, 73.6% of traffic-winning sites had some, and 43.7% of top-ranking pages did too — so organic reciprocity is generally safe. What's not safe: systematic "you link to me, I'll link to you" exchanges, especially sitewide sidebar swaps, which violate search guidelines and don't work anyway. Link out generously; let reciprocity emerge.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**What two large studies found:**
1. **Prevalence among successful sites** — 140,592 sites each drawing 10K+ monthly search visits: 73.6% had at least one reciprocal followed-link domain; 27.4% had ≥15% overlap between the domains they link to and domains linking to them. (Benchmark: on one large SEO blog, 19.25% of the domains it links to link back.)
2. **Prevalence among top rankings** — 112,440 top-10 pages for ~10K non-branded mid-difficulty keywords: 43.7% had some reciprocal links. On that same blog's top 10 pages, 7 of 10 had 5-100% overlap.

**How to read the data honestly:**
- Study 1 has survivorship bias — penalized sites (if any) were excluded because only traffic-winning sites were sampled.
- Study 2 shows correlation, not causation — pages may rank because of early reciprocal links, or despite them.
- Safe conclusion: some reciprocal linking is normal and compatible with strong performance; it is not proof that exchanges are a tactic.

**The verdict on deliberate exchanges:**
- Sitewide link swaps (blogroll/sidebar style): dead and against webmaster guidelines — no quality site does this anymore. One practitioner's early experiment hiding exchanged links in iframes was followed by a year of zero organic traffic.
- Page-level, occasional, organic reciprocity between genuinely related sites: normal and fine.
- "Will you link to me if I link to you?" outreach emails: stop — recipients dislike them, and they document intent to manipulate.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Measure your reciprocity: overlap between dofollow outbound domains and referring domains
- [ ] Treat <~15-20% overlap as the natural zone; don't engineer toward it
- [ ] Link out generously to the best resources (this builds relationships and future one-way links)
- [ ] Audit for sitewide exchanged links (sidebar/footer patterns) and remove them
- [ ] Never send link-for-link proposal emails
- [ ] If a partner naturally links back to something you published — accept it, don't reciprocate on cue

## Data points & benchmarks
- 140,592-site study: 73.6% have reciprocal links; 27.4% have ≥15% link overlap
- 112,440 top-ranking pages: 43.7% have some reciprocal links
- Single-site benchmark: 19.25% reciprocal share on one large SEO blog; 7 of its top 10 pages show 5%+ overlap
- Case: a naive sitewide exchange scheme (hidden in iframes) coincided with 12+ months of zero search traffic

## Mistakes to avoid
- Treating the prevalence data as proof that exchanges are safe — survivorship bias and correlation caveats apply
- Arranging sitewide reciprocal links with a group of similar sites
- Hiding exchanged links (iframes, cloaking) — trivially detectable, guideline-violating
- Sending explicit link-exchange outreach
- Being stingy with outbound links for fear of "leaking" reciprocity

## Key principles
- Reciprocity is a symptom of a healthy, connected niche — not a tactic to scale.
- Deliberate exchanges at scale are a link scheme; incidental reciprocals are unavoidable.
- Generous outbound linking is relationship capital that returns as one-way links later.
- When evaluating any link pattern, ask what it looks like at the traffic-winning sites — then remember survivorship bias.

- [ ] Open with connection reminders and small asks; personalize every send
- [ ] Track relationships, not just links — revisit partners for future placements

## Data points & benchmarks
- Case: a single competitive page required ~200 acquired links to reach page one; relationship-sourced links moved rankings most
- Nearly all authority-90+ links in that campaign came via partners and personal connections
- Prospecting filter used in the example: authority 50-90 + niche relevance + meaningful organic traffic
- Free-link sweet spot: prospect's keywords ranking positions 4-10 (they want top 3, so the link is genuinely welcome)

## Mistakes to avoid
- Sending template "great post, here's my URL" outreach and expecting links
- Asking for the link, a call, AND time in the very first email to a stranger
- Over-personalizing to the point of creepiness
- Turning a relationship into a link swap or paid arrangement — keep every link editorial (or tagged rel="sponsored"/nofollow)
- Automating follow-ups on relationship prospects
- Investing relationship effort in low-authority targets — save it for prospects where the link matters

## Key principles
- Links follow trust; trust follows demonstrated value with no immediate strings.
- A relationship converts one prospect into a recurring link source — the compounding asset of link building.
- Give-first (especially free links aimed at the recipient's own ranking goals) is the fastest trust builder.
- Relevance + personal + human beats volume in modern outreach.

## ARTICLE seo-outreach

### Shotgun or Sniper? Choosing Your Outreach Approach

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Three outreach styles: shotgun (mass, zero personalization), sniper (100% manual and customized), and scoped shotgun (templated email with dynamic merge fields per audience segment). The hybrid scoped shotgun wins for most teams — near-sniper personalization at near-shotgun scale. All three fail without something genuinely valuable to offer.

## Framework / Step-by-step
Prerequisites before any approach:
1. **Create something valuable** — new industry research, new statistics, survey results, expert quotations, updated content, a new course, guides/learning resources, videos, or something entertaining. Value is what stops the instant delete.
2. **Find a suitable audience** — use a content research tool to find sites that already wrote about your topic; track prospects in a spreadsheet; set topic alerts for new mentions.
3. **Pitch with a compelling angle** — then choose your delivery style:

**Approach 1 — Shotgun ("spray and pray")**
- Identical email, no personalization, not even the recipient's name
- Pros: fastest, zero expertise needed, good for beginners/quick blasts
- Cons: reads as spam, mostly ignored, damages brand reputation with repeated poor targeting

**Approach 2 — Sniper (fully personalized)**
- 100% manual research per prospect. Answer three questions first: Why should they open it (subject line)? Why should they care (the hook)? Why should they link (the pitch)?
- The 9-point email checklist: personalized, engaging subject line, human tone, clear purpose, professional, clear CTA, gratitude, proofread, legally compliant
- Pros: highest success rate; best for strong brands seeking a steady trickle of top-tier links
- Cons: nearly impossible to scale; needs negotiation skill and follow-ups; very time-consuming

**Approach 3 — Scoped shotgun (hybrid; recommended)**
- One template per audience segment with dynamic variables (e.g., {{value_topic}}, {{first_name}}, {{their_article}}) merged at send time via an outreach tool
- Value-topic variable examples: "created new industry research benchmarking 1,000+ sites' speed" / "curated 79 statistics on the state of the industry" / "compiled controversial expert opinions"
- Pros: sniper-like personalization, scalable, efficient once running
- Cons: setup takes time — segment research and template crafting; more variables ≠ more personalization, so start simple and refine

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Pick the value asset: research, stats, survey, quotes, updated content, course, guide, video
- [ ] Build a prospect list of sites that already covered the topic; add alerts for new mentions
- [ ] Choose approach by goal: speed → shotgun, top-tier trickle → sniper, scale + quality → hybrid
- [ ] For sniper: write per-prospect emails passing the 3 questions + 9-point checklist
- [ ] For hybrid: define 3-5 audience segments; build one template each with 3-5 merge fields
- [ ] Pilot on a small batch; iterate subject lines and hooks before scaling
- [ ] Always include a clear, single call to action

## Data points & benchmarks
- Sniper typically beats shotgun on success rate, according to practitioner reports (no controlled benchmark)
- Hybrid reportedly achieves similar response rates to sniper at a fraction of the per-email effort
- Merge-field rule of thumb: 3-5 well-chosen variables; more adds complexity, not personalization

## Mistakes to avoid
- Pitching "link plz" with nothing of value in return — guaranteed radio silence
- Offering money or other compensation for a link without rel="sponsored"/nofollow and disclosure
- Using shotgun repeatedly on poorly targeted lists — reputation damage compounds
- Over-stuffing templates with merge fields that break or feel robotic
- Skipping the follow-up on sniper outreach (where links are negotiated)
- Sending before proofreading or checking compliance rules for your region

## Key principles
- Outreach trades value for links; the asset is the campaign, the email is just delivery
- A little personalization is the difference between reply and trash
- Match the method to the goal: learning, precision, or scale
- Segment-level personalization (hybrid) captures most of sniper quality at scale
- Subject line, hook, and ask must each earn their place

- Sending the original 2015 template word-for-word — instantly deleted
- One generic pitch to all linkers — people link for different reasons
- Only replicating one page's backlinks — too small a pool; link building is a numbers game
- Arms-race padding (25 → 50 → 100 items) — "curation is where the value is"
- Improving for improvement's sake (bumping publish date, adding mediocre tips) — ask "does this give more value?"

## Key principles
- "Better does not occur in a branding vacuum" (a well-known link-building saying) — people often link because of WHO published it, not having read it.

### The Tabloid Technique: Landing Local Links With Digital PR

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Local newspapers link to almost any story that compares how their area stacks up on something — taxes, potholes, driving-test pass rates. ## Framework / Step-by-step
1. **Find a newsworthy topic.** The hardest part. Two buckets:
   - **Evergreen topics** — taxes, potholes, commute times: things locals always care about (bonus if slightly aggravating)
   - **Trending topics** — local elections, seasonal events: things journalists need to cover right now
   - Mine ideas from a keyword tool's trending metrics, news search, and AI-assisted brainstorming; study past hit campaigns with a content research tool (e.g., pages about "tax" with links from 100+ domains). Quantity beats quality here — brainstorm broadly with the whole team; even bad ideas get written down.
2. **Pull local data.** Sources:
   - Government/statistical databases (national statistics portals, data.gov-style catalogs, EU/canada/world statistics, central bank and UN data)
   - Topic specialists: crime stats, health data, public-opinion research centers
   - **Freedom of information (FOI) requests** — cheap, underused, and produce exclusive numbers
   - Merge multiple sources when no single dataset suffices
   - Publish findings on a journalist-friendly page: full data, sources, methodology, and ready-to-use visual assets.
3. **Send it to local journalists.**
   - Match data to place: journalists in the areas that top (or bottom) the ranking are your targets
   - Find outlets via `[place name] newspaper` searches; identify journalist names on staff pages; emails are usually listed on profiles — otherwise use an email lookup tool
   - Prefer currently active writers (sort a publication's authors by recent output) so you don't pitch people who've left
   - Email pattern that works: personalized, leads with the local hook ("Your city ranked X for Y"), includes the key stat and a link to the full data page

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] List 10+ evergreen local topics (tax, traffic, crime, schools, weather) + current trending hooks
- [ ] Validate topics against past campaigns that earned 50+ referring domains
- [ ] Identify the dataset(s); file FOI requests if public data doesn't exist
- [ ] Build the local ranking/statistic; document methodology and sources
- [ ] Create embeddable maps/charts journalists can reuse
- [ ] Build a target list: one journalist per ranked area, prioritizing top/bottom areas
- [ ] Verify each journalist is still active at the outlet
- [ ] Send localized pitches (area-specific stat in the subject/first line)
- [ ] Track coverage and referring domains earned per campaign

## Data points & benchmarks
- UK driving-test wait-times index: links from ~58-59 websites, mostly local newspapers
- Lifetime tax paid per US state (multi-source merge): links from 188 websites
- Illegal vape hotspots investigation (built from FOI requests): 72 referring domains, heavy newspaper pickup
- Even mediocre rankings (e.g., a city placing 37th "best for millennials") got local coverage — the bar is low
- These are one practitioner's best examples; results vary widely by topic and market

## Mistakes to avoid
- Waiting for a brilliant idea: hit campaigns are usually simple local comparisons executed well
- Pitching national outlets when the data is local — local papers are the link source
- Sending journalists raw data without a page containing methodology and visuals
- Publishing unverified or cherry-picked numbers — check data quality and be transparent about limitations before pitching
- Contacting inactive journalists or generic newsroom inboxes
- One idea per campaign: run several — no technique works every time

## Key principles
- Local journalists need constant copy about their area; give them a pre-packaged story
- The more areas your data ranks, the more outlets you can legitimately pitch
- Exclusive data (FOI, proprietary surveys) beats recycled public stats
- Journalist-friendly = data + sources + methodology + visuals, all citable
- Volume of ideas and pitches drives hit rate, not one perfect campaign

## ARTICLE the-trust-formula

### Earning High-Quality Backlinks With the TRUST Formula (Case Study)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
TRUST = Trending topic + Research and analysis + Unique takeaway + Simple visual + Tactical promotion. One practitioner used it to turn public sports-earnings data into a US-state map that earned links from high-authority publications (authority 73-92 on the tool's scale), coinciding with a new site rising from ~0 to 350-400 organic visits/day. Then a second related study ("1-2 punch") re-hit the same journalists for another batch of trust links.

## Framework / Step-by-step
Why high-authority links: page-level strength passes immediate "juice", but links from strong DOMAINS may build lasting trust — published search patents describe ranking value by closeness to trusted "seed pages," so links from authority sites could lower your distance (a theory, not a confirmed Google mechanism).

The formula:
1. **Trending topic** — journalists already covering it are warm prospects. Find via news search or a content research tool filtered to the last 7 days (one example: a golf star's comeback dominated the news cycle).
2. **Research and analysis** — take free public data (100+ open data sources exist) and extract something new: the case merged public tournament records into earnings-per-US-state figures.
3. **Unique takeaway** — one line in the pattern "We worked out {X}. Did you know that {Y}?" (case: the athlete had earned prize money in exactly half of the 50 states). The second clause measurably lifts pickup.
4. **Simple visual** — usually a map. Three build options: a point-and-click design tool (heat maps, legends, embeds), a custom design on a stock blank map, or a coded interactive map (responsive but technical). Publish with a short write-up.
5. **Tactical promotion:**
   - Get social traction first (relevant communities, small paid pushes; disclose your affiliation and follow each community's rules)
   - Build the prospect list from news search + past-week web search (200+ recent articles on the trending topic) and a real-time mention alert for new ones
   - Direct writer emails only (generic contact forms = last resort)
   - Pitch short: first name → mention you read their piece → what you made → attach the graphic + link to the full piece → NO explicit link ask. If they say "cool!" but don't act in days, then ask directly
   - Follow up ONCE, citing other publications that picked it up
- **1-2 punch:** repeat with a second related study weeks later. Journalists who linked once link again far more easily — consistency is what builds trust signals. For holdouts, deploy FOMO: "Our last study was picked up by [competitor outlets] — heads up, we've just published a new one" (optionally offer an exclusive).

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Set a 7-day news/content watch on your niche; shortlist trending topics
- [ ] Match each topic to a free dataset; compute a state/category breakdown
- [ ] Distill the "We worked out X / Did you know Y?" takeaway
- [ ] Produce a simple map/heat-map visual + brief write-up
- [ ] Warm it up socially, then export the week's writers on the topic
- [ ] Send short, attach-the-graphic pitches; one follow-up max
- [ ] Log every linker; plan the related follow-up study (1-2 punch)
- [ ] Re-pitch previous linkers first; use competitor-pickup FOMO on holdouts

## Data points & benchmarks
- Case placements: multiple links from publications with authority scores of 73, 74, 74, 75, 81, 89 (and a 90-92 network)
- Traffic effect: new site went from ~zero to 350-400 organic visits/day around when the first campaign's links landed (a single case; other factors may have contributed)
- Prospect supply: 200+ articles written about the trending topic in 7 days
- Takeaway rule: "earned money in exactly half the states" style specifics outperform topic-only headlines

## Mistakes to avoid
- Clever long pitches — journalists are busy; get to the point in 3-4 sentences
- Asking for the link in the first email — let the asset do the asking
- Multiple automated follow-ups — one, referencing other pickups, is the limit
- Pitching through generic newsroom addresses instead of the writer
- Running the formula once: the repeat study on the same journalist list is where the trust compounding happens

## Key principles
- You can't fake data journalism — which is why it resists being spammed to death
- High-authority domains build trust; strong individual pages deliver quick juice; you want both
- Trend-jacking converts cold journalist outreach into warm pitches
- One strong visual + one sharp takeaway + the right 100 writers beats 10,000 generic emails
- Consistency signals trust: two linked studies beat one viral hit

## ARTICLE toxic-backlinks

### Toxic Backlinks: Real Risk or Overblown Fear?

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
"Toxic backlinks" is a label invented by SEO tools, not a search engine concept. Search engines ignore most low-quality spam automatically; what actually hurts is a PATTERN of manipulative links you built or bought. Disavow only after a manual action or a genuinely manipulative large-scale profile — in one reported case, blanket-disavowing tool-flagged links was followed by a 60% traffic drop.

## Framework / Step-by-step
Three definitions that change everything:
- **Spammy links** — low-quality links every site attracts through no fault of its own
- **Manipulative links** — links built or bought deliberately to game rankings
- **"Toxic" links** — whatever an automated tool score flags; a marketing construct

Risk logic:
1. **Spammy links: ignore them.** Since the 2016 Penguin 4.0 update, Google devalues spam rather than demoting whole sites, and its reps consistently say to ignore drive-by spam links. Even a veteran specialist who sold disavow services for years now says the algorithms already ignore these and disavowing them doesn't lift rankings.
2. **Manipulative links: yes, they can hurt.** But search engines look for PATTERNS of manipulation (profile-level), not one dodgy link. Consequences: an unnatural-links manual action, or algorithmic demotion when trust in the site is lost ("we've lost trust… we take a conservative side" per Google reps). One reciprocal link with a friend won't trigger it; hundreds of paid links will.
3. **Finding genuinely harmful links — manual audit, not tool scores:**
   - Start with the anchor text distribution: unnatural, over-optimized, exact-match commercial anchors signal paid/manipulative links
   - Dig into individual backlinks for context; paid and unnatural links are usually obvious in context
   - Look for patterns, not isolated links
   - If unsure what you're looking at, hire an experienced auditor

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Check for a manual action before anything else
- [ ] Review anchor text distribution for exact-match commercial over-optimization
- [ ] Inspect the surrounding context of suspicious links, not just the linking domain
- [ ] Judge the profile as a pattern: scale + intent, not individual links
- [ ] Disavow ONLY if: manual action, or a large volume of knowingly built/bought links
- [ ] If a traffic drop coincides with a sudden unexplained link influx, investigate (possible negative SEO)
- [ ] Ignore tool-generated "toxicity scores" entirely

## Data points & benchmarks
- ~38% of SEOs in one older survey still disavowed low-quality links (a habit, not evidence of harm)
- One site owner blanket-disavowed tool-flagged "toxic" links → traffic reportedly fell 60% (anecdote)
- One controlled experiment disavowing GOOD links caused measurable ranking/traffic loss — reversed after removing the disavow
- Penguin 4.0 (Sept 2016): the shift from site-wide demotion to devaluing spam signals
- Google's own criteria for disavowing: "considerable number of spammy, artificial, or low-quality links" AND a manual action caused or likely

## Mistakes to avoid
- Treating a tool's "toxicity score" as ground truth — the truly harmful links are rarely the ones tools flag
- Disavowing links that are actually helping (traffic loss has been documented)
- Buying or building manipulative links and planning to "disavow later" — disavow is a cleanup tool, not permission
- Worrying about every drive-by spam link every site naturally attracts
- Assuming negative SEO behind every traffic drop — usually it's an algorithm update or site issue
- Doing a link audit without knowing what manipulative patterns look like

## Key principles
- Name the distinction: spammy (ignore) vs. manipulative (fix) vs. "toxic" (marketing)
- Search engines punish patterns and intent, not stray links
- Disavow is a last resort for manual actions and large-scale self-made spam
- Disavowing is not risk-free: you can accidentally cut off link equity that's working
- When in doubt, get an experienced human audit — automation miscalculates both directions

## ARTICLE types-of-backlinks

### Get These 6 Types of Backlinks (+ 4 Types to Avoid)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Only a handful of link types are worth deliberate effort — editorial citations, guest posts, relationship links, business profiles, speaking engagements, and embedded assets (widgets/badges). Four categories actively hurt or waste money: link schemes, junk directories, forum spam, and non-newsworthy press releases. Vet every link against five quality markers before pursuing it.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**The 6 types to build:**
1. **Editorial backlinks** — cited as an authoritative source inside content. Earn via cite-worthy content (studies, infographics), digital PR/newsjacking, and reactive PR (HARO-style journalist-request services). Often from high-authority sites and drive real referral traffic.
2. **Guest blogging backlinks** — among the most-used tactics in industry surveys, but often outsourced to low-quality sites; do it in-house, keep it editorial (no paid placements without rel="sponsored"/nofollow), and use natural anchors. Target sites in the authority 30-65 band (high enough to matter, realistic to land). Pitch 2-3 ideas as a detailed content brief: title options, similar SERP posts, post goal, format/intent, target audience, 3-5 keywords with volume + difficulty, "people also ask" questions, word count, and a heading-level outline.
3. **Relationship-based backlinks** — thank journalists/webmasters who linked to you and offer future input; become their trusted on-call source. Don't turn relationships into link swaps: reciprocal and three-way exchanges done at scale are link schemes, wherever you operate.
4. **Backlinks in business profiles** — minutes each on established listing sites; builds a digital footprint. Stop at the big, credible listings — mass profile creation is spam.
5. **Backlinks from public speaking** — seminars, webinars, podcasts, conferences, courses: event sites link to speaker bios. Also builds demonstrated expertise signals (E-E-A-T).
6. **Embedded asset links** — (a) free tools/widgets (e.g., calculators) with embed code that carries your brand + link (keep the anchor branded, not keyword-stuffed, and consider nofollow on widget links); (b) "best of" awards: publish a roundup (ideally poll-backed, with honest criteria), give winners a badge with your link — winners proudly display it.

**The 4 types to avoid:**
7. **Link schemes** — buying/selling links, excessive exchanges, forced ToS links, mass-distributed footer/template links, followed ad links, keyword-stuffed guest-post/comment anchors. Includes PBNs (fake "earned" links) and automated link software sold cheaply as "foundation links" — tiered spam networks that risk penalties.
8. **Low-quality or irrelevant directory links** — quality listings good, mass submission spam.
9. **Low-quality forum backlinks** — links belong in forums only when they're genuine advice within a discussion.
10. **Non-newsworthy press releases** — real announcements (launches, acquisitions, rebrands) fine; releases manufactured purely for links are spam.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Pick 2-3 of the six "good" types and run them as programs, not one-offs
- [ ] Subscribe to journalist request digests for editorial citations
- [ ] Build a guest-post pipeline with brief-style pitches to authority 30-65 sites
- [ ] Send thank-you + "contact me anytime" emails to everyone who links to you
- [ ] Claim the major business listings once; skip mass directory submission
- [ ] Pitch podcasts/webinars in your niche
- [ ] Test one embedded asset: a calculator/widget or an awards roundup with badges
- [ ] Never buy links, PBN placements, or cheap automated link packages
- [ ] Only issue press releases for genuinely newsworthy events

## Data points & benchmarks
- Guest-post sweet spot for newer sites: referring domains with authority ~30-65 (a rough heuristic; scales differ by tool)
- Guest-post briefs that convert: 3-5 target keywords with volumes + difficulty attached
- Six good types vs. four bad types — a complete classification for auditing a vendor's proposal

## Mistakes to avoid
- Outsourcing guest posts without vetting (the default outcome is low-quality sites)
- Paying sites that misapply nofollow and then charge to "upgrade" the link to followed — that's a paid link
- Overdoing keyword-rich anchors in guest posts — reads as manipulation
- Confusing "more profiles/directories/forums" with progress — scale in those channels equals spam
- Sending press releases about nothing to farm links

## Key principles
- The best links are editorially given by humans who chose to cite you.
- Links should ideally move rankings AND build brand exposure/referral traffic at the same time.
- Any link you can buy in bulk at a low price is, by definition, one search engines can discount or punish in bulk.
- Vet every prospect: trusted domain, niche-relevant page, natural anchor, followed, and a page that itself has links.

- Being pushy when the mention doesn't warrant a link (or the site already links to you everywhere)

- Assuming 100% success — even big brands fail on some perfectly good pitches
- Copying the sample email verbatim — write your own, personalize per site

- An unlinked mention means you're "already halfway" — familiarity exists; the ask is small.

## ARTICLE what-are-backlinks

### What Are Backlinks in SEO? Everything You Need to Know

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
A backlink is a link from one website to another — a trust signal that helps pages rank and get discovered. Quality beats quantity: one link from a strong, relevant, editorially-placed page can outweigh hundreds of weak ones, and the easier a link is to get, the less it's usually worth.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Why backlinks matter:**
1. Ranking — an older industry study of ~14 billion pages found pages with more referring domains get more organic traffic (correlation, not proof of causation). One controlled experiment also showed traffic fell when a page's links were disavowed and recovered when the disavow was removed.
2. Discoverability — more quality links = search engines find and crawl your content faster.

**The six attributes of a good backlink (vet every opportunity against them):**
1. **Authority** — links from pages that themselves have strong backlinks pass more strength. Authority is diluted: a page linking out to four sites passes less per link than one linking to two (PageRank splitting).
2. **Relevance** — links from sites on the same subject likely count more (a plumber's link from a boiler-installation page beats one from a cat blog).
3. **Anchor text** — the clickable words influence what the linked page ranks for (confirmed in the original search patent).
4. **Placement** — links in main content, styled to be clicked, pass more than footer/sidebar links (the "reasonable surfer" concept).
5. **Destination** — links should point at the page you want to rank. Since commercial pages rarely attract links naturally, earn links to informational content and channel authority to money pages with internal links (the "middleman" pattern).
6. **Follow vs. nofollow** — nofollow/sponsored/UGC attributes hint that authority shouldn't pass; prioritize followed links, but a nofollow from a strong relevant page is still worth having.

**How to check & analyze backlinks:**
- Free baseline: the Search Console's links report (shows up to ~1,000 links).
- A backlink analysis tool shows all links plus follow status, link type (in-content vs sidebar/footer), domain authority, domain traffic, and anchor/surrounding text filters.
- Worked example of competitor analysis: open a competitor's links → filter to the highest-quality ones → spot that a high-authority link came from original research with dated data → publish fresher research to win that link. Also look at top pages by links to see which content formats attract links, and use a link-intersect comparison (links competitors share but you lack) to find gap opportunities.

**Four ways to get backlinks:** Add (directories, citations — easy, low value), Ask (outreach: guest posts, broken links, skyscraper, unlinked mentions), Buy (don't — spam-policy and penalty risk; any paid link must be rel="sponsored" or nofollow), Earn (link bait).

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Audit a target link for all six attributes before pursuing it
- [ ] Check whether prospect links sit in main content (filter out sidebar/footer)
- [ ] Run a link-intersect against 2-3 competitors to build your first prospect list
- [ ] Identify competitors' most-linked content formats and plan one linkable asset
- [ ] Map internal links from newly linked pages toward pages you want to rank
- [ ] Keep anchor text natural — mixed anchors, no exact-match saturation
- [ ] Avoid: PBNs, paid links, link exchanges, automated links, forum/comment spam

## Data points & benchmarks
- ~14B-page study (older): referring domains correlate with organic traffic
- Disavow experiment: removing links via disavow dropped traffic; restoring links recovered it (one experiment)
- One link bait data study earned 5.7K backlinks from 2.9K unique referring domains
- Ranking for head terms is link-hungry: one head term ("tea") needed ~756 unique links for a top-10 shot in one analysis; long-tail equivalents need near zero
- A page with zero backlinks can still rank for 84 keywords in positions 1-3 (~2.5K monthly visits) when competition is low
- Nofollow became a "hint" rather than a directive (announced 2019, applied to ranking from March 2020)
- 2024 re-analysis: link-ranking correlation is weakening, especially for low-volume queries — but remains stronger for high-volume, competitive queries; external links correlate with rankings far more than internal links

## Mistakes to avoid
- Chasing link count over link quality — a single link from a major news site's homepage can outweigh thousands of weak links
- Ignoring where on the page the link sits (footer/sidebar links pass little)
- Over-optimizing anchors: if ~90% of anchors to a page are the exact target keyword, that's a manipulation red flag
- Worrying about random low-quality inbound links — normal; only large-scale spam or link schemes warrant action
- Confusing buying links with hiring an agency that uses white-hat tactics

## Key principles
- Google ranks pages, not websites — build links to the specific URL you want to rank.
- The easier a link is to acquire, the less valuable it typically is.
- Links matter less than they once did but still differentiate on competitive queries; treat any prediction about their future weight as speculation.
- Quality attributes (authority, relevance, placement, anchor, destination, follow status) — not raw counts — decide a link's worth.

## ARTICLE white-hat-link-building-techniques

### 7 Popular White Hat Link Building Techniques

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
One industry survey of 270 SEOs ranked seven white-hat techniques as the ones practitioners most often use (keep any placements editorial, and tag paid ones rel="sponsored" or nofollow): link-bait content, competitor link analysis, guest posting, reactive PR, broken link building, brand-mention chasing, and brand news. Pick two or three that match your strengths and run them as repeatable systems rather than one-off stunts.

## Framework / Step-by-step
The seven techniques, in rough order of popularity:

1. **Content marketing specifically to generate links (link bait)** — Study a competitor's most-linked pages (top pages by links in a backlink analysis tool) to learn which topics and formats earn links in your niche (data studies and statistics dominate in SEO/marketing). Build your own version, then actively promote it — links don't arrive by discovery alone.
2. **Competitor analysis and targeting their links** — Review a competitor's full backlink profile looking for *patterns*, not single links to replicate. Example: if a competitor's team appears on many podcasts, podcast outreach is a proven channel in your industry — curate those same shows plus new ones.
3. **Guest posting** — Target sites that already get meaningful organic traffic on your topic (500+ word articles filter, one result per site, in a content research tool). Prioritize sites with multiple authors — a signal they may accept outside contributors (e.g., one keto site had 20 authors and ~19K monthly topic visits). Write genuinely useful posts with natural, branded anchors; mass-produced guest posts for keyword links are a link-scheme risk.
4. **Reactive PR** — Respond fast to time-sensitive journalist requests (HARO-style services send ~3 digests/day). Set up inbox filters on relevant keywords so you only see queries you're genuinely qualified to answer. A domain expert (e.g., an on-staff veterinarian answering a pet-care query) earns the quote and the link.
5. **Broken link building** — Find dead pages with 10+ referring domains (one example: a deleted keto page with 104 referring domains). Check the archived version, rebuild the content if it fits your site, then pitch everyone still linking to the dead page.
6. **Chasing brand mentions** — Search the web for pages mentioning your brand that don't link to you. Filter out noise: authority 30+, your language, first seen within 6 months, one page per domain. Pitch only where a link genuinely helps readers. Bonus tactic: get included in listicles via `best [business type] in [location] -"[your business name]"` — but the pitch must make sense (invite the listicle author to actually experience your business).
7. **Brand news** — Promote genuinely newsworthy company milestones (launches, funding, IPOs) to tech/business press. One brand's PR lead landed major tech-press coverage of a product launch this way.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Pull the most-linked content for 3-5 competitors; note recurring formats
- [ ] Commit to one linkable-asset format proven in your niche, then plan promotion (not just publication)
- [ ] Scan competitor backlink profiles for tactic patterns (podcasts, roundups, guest posts, tools)
- [ ] Build a guest-post target list of multi-author sites with topic-relevant traffic
- [ ] Subscribe to journalist request digests; add keyword filters; answer within the hour where possible
- [ ] Run a broken-link sweep for dead pages with 10+ referring domains in your topic
- [ ] Set up an unlinked-mention search with authority/recency/dedup filters
- [ ] Search for "best [your category] in [your city]" listicles that omit you

## Data points & benchmarks
- 270 SEOs surveyed in one industry link-building survey (the year of the survey matters; check for a newer edition)
- Broken-link targets worth rebuilding: pages with 10+ referring domains (example found: 104 RDs)
- Guest-post qualification example: 20 authors, ~19K monthly organic visits on one topic
- Unlinked-mention qualification filters: authority 30+, same language, first seen ≤6 months, one page per domain
- Journalist digests arrive ~3x per day — filtering is mandatory to keep it usable

## Mistakes to avoid
- Publishing link bait and assuming links follow without promotion
- Copying individual competitor links instead of identifying the *tactic* behind them
- Pitching listicle inclusion cold with no reason (authors won't add a business they've never experienced)
- Responding to journalist queries outside your expertise — relevance and credibility win the quote

## Key principles
- The techniques that survive year over year are the unglamorous ones: systematized prospecting, fast responses, and assets built from proven patterns.
- Every "ask" tactic (outreach, reactive PR, listicle pitches) works only when the ask is backed by genuine relevance or value exchange.
- Competitor link profiles are tactic maps, not link shopping lists.

## ARTICLE who-links-to-my-site

### How to Find Who Links to Your Website (and What to Do Next)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Finding who links to you is easy — the Search Console's links report is free, and a backlink analysis tool adds context, quality metrics, and competitor data. The value comes from what you do next: nurture serial linkers, make more of your most-linked content types, reclaim broken-page links, replicate competitor links, and pitch "likely linkers."

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Two ways to see your linkers:**
1. **Search console (free, your sites only)** — Links report → Top linking sites (up to 1,000 domains, with linking/target page counts) and Top linked pages (sort by *linking sites*, not incoming links — unique domains matter more than repeat links). Limits: 1,000-row cap, no anchor/nofollow context, no site-quality metrics.
2. **Backlink analysis tool (any site — including competitors)** — a referring-domains view with authority, follow status, traffic, first-seen dates; a backlinks view with anchors, surrounding text, and filters. Works on any domain, subfolder, or URL you don't own.

**Five actions once you know who links:**
1. **Nurture serial linkers** — sites linking repeatedly are your warmest audience. Find yours (top linking sites sorted by target pages; ignore social networks/forums/UGC), then find competitors' serial linkers (their referring domains sorted by links-to-target) and build relationships with those sites too.
2. **Learn from your most-linked content** — different niches reward different formats: studies dominate in SEO, calculators and statistics in personal finance, leaks and exclusives in tech news. Check your top linked pages (by linking sites) and competitors' top pages by links (live pages only), then produce more of the winning format.
3. **Reclaim value from broken pages** — dead pages have no internal links, so their link equity is stranded. Find 404s with backlinks (Search Console's Page indexing report cross-checked against top linked pages, or a backlink tool's top pages by links filtered to 404), then reinstate the page, 301 it to a relevant replacement, or ask linkers to update.
4. **Replicate competitors' links** — (a) their broken pages: pitch their linkers your working equivalent (worked example: 29 sites still linking to a competitor's deleted guide); (b) their guest posts: search their backlinks for "/author/" URL patterns — those sites demonstrably accept guest contributions from businesses like yours.
5. **Pitch likely linkers** — find patterns in your existing backlinks (e.g., many links came from "content marketing" guides), then find all similar pages that don't link yet (title + content mention filters narrowed 52,000 pages to 1,028 prospects in the example) and show them your content.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Export Search Console top linking sites + top linked pages (by linking sites)
- [ ] List serial linkers; open a relationship track for each (and for competitors')
- [ ] Identify your niche's winning link format; schedule more of it
- [ ] Fix every 404 with referring domains (reinstate/redirect/reclaim)
- [ ] Pull competitor 404s with links; pitch replacements to their linkers
- [ ] Find competitor guest-post placements via /author/ URLs; pitch those sites
- [ ] Mine one backlink pattern → build a "likely linkers" list → outreach

## Data points & benchmarks
- Search console caps link reports at 1,000 rows — beyond that you need a backlink tool
- Competitive bar example (illustrative): a head term like "best credit cards" may need links from 100-200+ unique sites to crack the top 5
- Low-competition screen: keywords with very low difficulty scores (e.g., under ~10 in many tools), or pages with 500+ monthly visits and fewer than 5 referring domains
- Niche format examples: studies (SEO), calculators/stats (finance), leaks/exclusives (tech media)

## Mistakes to avoid
- Sorting top linked pages by raw incoming links instead of linking sites (one site's 50 sitewide links ≠ 50 endorsements)
- Counting social networks and forum links as "linkers" — that's UGC noise
- Leaving 404s with backlinks unfixed — you already paid for those links
- Pitching competitor linkers without a contextually better resource
- Chasing head terms needing hundreds of links when low-difficulty topics compound faster

## Key principles
- Knowing who links to you is diagnostics; the money is in comparison with competitors and in patterns.
- Serial linkers — yours and your competitors' — are the highest-ROI outreach targets that exist.
- Links to dead pages are sunk costs until you restore or redirect them.
- Make more of whatever your niche already links to; don't guess formats.
