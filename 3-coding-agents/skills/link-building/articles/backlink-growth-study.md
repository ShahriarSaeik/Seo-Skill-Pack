# How Many New Backlinks Do Top-ranking Pages Get Over Time (New Data)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Top-ranking pages attract new links faster than the pages below them — the "vicious circle of SEO": high rankings → exposure and credibility → more links → even stronger rankings. A study of 200,000 pages found #1 pages typically gain +5% to +14.5% new followed referring domains per month, so challengers must out-build not a static target but a moving one. Pace of acquisition alone, however, showed no measurable ranking effect — quality still decides.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Why the circle spins:**
1. **Exposure** — most searchers click the first few results; when they later need a reference to cite, they link to the page they actually read. Lower-ranking pages never got read, so they never get cited.
2. **Credibility** — a #1 position functions as an endorsement ("this is the best page for the topic"), making people more comfortable citing it.

**Study design:** 10,000 non-branded keywords (2-5K monthly searches) → top-20 pages each → 200,000 pages; measured followed referring domains acquired in the preceding 3 months by ranking position.

**Findings:**
- Top-3 results attract more new followed referring domains than the rest of the SERP; the median pace of link growth declines steadily with ranking position.
- #1 pages typically grow their followed referring domains +5% to +14.5% per month.
- Compounding math: if the leader has 55 referring domains and you need 3 months to match that, their profile will have grown +17-50% in the meantime (to ~64-83) — you must build up to ~83 links in 3 months just to keep pace.
- Counter-test: correlating link acquisition *rate* with 3-month SERP movement showed no clear relationship — pace alone doesn't move rankings (link quality, content, and behavioral factors confound it; SERPs regularly show pages with fewer RDs outranking pages with more).

**How to apply:** before targeting a keyword, open the referring-domains growth graph of the current #1 page (rough gauge — includes nofollow). If it's climbing fast, either budget serious link velocity or choose a different battle.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] For each target keyword, chart the #1 page's referring-domain growth over 12 months
- [ ] Calculate the leader's monthly growth % and project it across your expected campaign timeline
- [ ] Set your monthly link target above the leader's projected pace, not their current count
- [ ] Prefer keywords where the #1 page's growth is flat or slow (weaker circle)
- [ ] Track your own pages' RD growth % — early momentum compounds
- [ ] Don't interpret slow acquisition as automatic decline — pace ≠ ranking factor

## Data points & benchmarks
- 200,000 pages / 10,000 keywords analyzed
- #1 ranking pages: +5% to +14.5% followed-referring-domain growth per month
- Worked example: 55-RD leader → 64-83 RDs after 3 months of compounding
- Median link-growth pace declines monotonically with ranking position

## Mistakes to avoid
- Planning link counts against the leader's *current* profile instead of their projected one
- Assuming raw acquisition speed itself is a ranking factor — the SERP-movement test found no such link
- Ignoring the compounding advantage of early ranking wins (get to page one, then the circle helps you)
- Giving up on keywords where leaders are strong everywhere — check flat-growth leaders first

## Key principles
- Rankings and links form a feedback loop: visibility earns links, links defend visibility.
- SERP leaders are moving targets — model growth rates, not snapshots.
- The practical use of this data is keyword selection and velocity planning, not proving causation (it is one correlational study, and tool link counts are estimates).
- Plan for earned links only: buying links to hit a velocity target is a spam-policy violation (paid links must be rel="sponsored" or nofollow).
- Quality links + genuinely useful pages remain the strategy; velocity math just sizes the budget.
