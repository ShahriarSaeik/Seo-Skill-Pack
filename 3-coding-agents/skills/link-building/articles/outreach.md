# Why Your Outreach Email Got Deleted (And How to Write Ones That Don't)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Templated outreach IS spam, and recipients can spot it instantly. Segment prospects by influence, use their real email, lead with value and specificity, time your pitch to their recent work, ask with an easy out, and follow up at most once. One practitioner doubled results (7-10 links per 50 emails vs. per 100) by applying these rules.

## Framework / Step-by-step
**Segment prospects into four groups and treat each differently:**
- **Sharks** — famous names with huge audiences. Emails from strangers don't get read; only personal introductions or genuinely outstanding, creative work get through. Huge payoff if you land one.
- **Big Fish** — smaller-but-meaningful audiences. Reachable with a personal email, never a template. Don't ask them for links/tweets — ask for critique or idea validation; if your work deserves it, they'll share it anyway.
- **Small fish** — growing sites actively promoting themselves in communities and via guest posts. Your ideal outreach targets.
- **Spawn** — brand-new sites. They'll reply to anything, but their links carry almost no value. Skip.

**The 9 rules:**
1. **Use the correct email address.** Research the real address; guessed addresses landing in catch-all inboxes get ignored almost always (one practitioner's estimate: ~99%). Wrong email = "you didn't care."
2. **Think what's in it for them.** Show something genuinely new (state the actual takeaways, not "check my amazing research"), or feature them (quotes, lessons learned, results with their product) — nobody can resist content about themselves.
3. **Only pitch your best work, with social proof.** One first impression; attach evidence it's not just you who thinks it's good (comments, community upvotes, notable tweets/shares, citations). Get initial traction first, then use it.
4. **Re-think your outreach excuse.** "You wrote X, I wrote something similar" is anti-value. Use: a different opinion, the one important aspect they missed, or a better resource BECAUSE {specific reason}. This requires actually skimming their article.
5. **Templates that don't look like templates.** Short, curiosity-gap subject lines. Break automation-shaped patterns (the "I saw your post / you linked to X / mine is better / please link" skeleton). Flattery must be specific ("your advice on X is going straight into my workflow this month") or absent.
6. **Show you know them.** Reference their actual recent work and preferences ("I knew he'd just published an epic guide, so…"). Demonstrated effort obliges a reply.
7. **Timing is your friend.** Authors are most receptive right after publishing — one blogger added 22 suggested tools to a fresh post within days of launch. Old posts rarely get revisited. Set alerts for new relevant articles and reach out immediately.
8. **Ask wisely.** Never ask for tweets (it converts your email into a favor request and forces awkward refusals — good content gets shared unasked). Do ask for links, softly, with an easy out: "Maybe it's worth mentioning in your article — or in a future one?"
9. **Follow up once.** Short, polite, explicitly final ("won't bug you again"). Multiple follow-ups = you become the cautionary tale.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Bucket every prospect: Shark / Big Fish / Small fish / Spawn; target the middle two
- [ ] Manually verify each email address before sending
- [ ] Lead with 2-3 concrete takeaways of your asset
- [ ] Add one credibility proof point (traction metric or citation)
- [ ] Write a specific, non-generic hook referencing THEIR work
- [ ] Pitch only fresh articles (alerts, weekly)
- [ ] Ask for feedback or a soft link; never a tweet
- [ ] One follow-up, then stop

## Data points & benchmarks
- Applying these rules roughly doubled one practitioner's efficiency: from 7-10 links per 100 emails to 7-10 per ~50 (a single anecdote, not a benchmark)
- Guess-emails to catch-all domains: ignored ~99% of the time (practitioner estimate)
- Freshly published posts are dramatically more link-amendable than old ones (22 additions to a day-old post)

## Mistakes to avoid
- The 4-link template skeleton — instantly recognizable, instantly deleted
- Generic flattery ("great article, learned a lot") — worse than nothing
- Pitching average work to top people — burns your one first impression
- Mass-emailing the most famous people in your niche
- Asking for tweets/shares explicitly
- Endless automated follow-up chains
- Offering money or gifts for links without disclosure — paid links must be tagged rel="sponsored" or nofollow

## Key principles
- There's a human on the other side; demonstrated effort is the personalization that matters
- Value first: their audience's needs, not your link count
- Fame is inversely proportional to template tolerance
- Timing beats persuasion: catch authors while they still care about the piece
- A good ask leaves the recipient a graceful exit
