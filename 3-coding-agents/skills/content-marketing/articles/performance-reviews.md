# The Content Marketer's Guide to Performance Reviews

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
No consensus exists on reviewing content marketers — a survey of managers found everything from pure-metrics shops to no-review shops. What works: match the method to your people and strategy, avoid pay-for-performance incentives in creative work, fix recruitment instead of over-engineering reviews, let employees self-track, and sweat the small logistics. With the right hires, regular casual chats beat formal scorecards.

## Framework / Step-by-step
Review types (pick and mix):
- **Rating scale** — scorecard table of variables (creativity, teamwork, efficiency) rated 1–5, summed
- **Self-assessment** — employee reviews themselves against goals/KPIs/OKRs, then compares notes with the manager
- **360 feedback** — managers, peers, customers (sometimes self) fill questionnaires
- **Management by objectives** — performance = goal attainment (articles shipped, traffic growth, leads)
- **Continuous real-time feedback** — ongoing task/project discussions, no formal intervals (agile-style)

Gathering techniques: checklist (forced yes/no), scorecard (scale), free-form essay (often as a supplement).

Survey findings (20 managers; small sample, directional only):
- 55% use only quantitative metrics; 20% use NO metrics; 15% mixed; 10% only qualitative
- Most popular quantitative: traffic and MQLs (also: shares, MRR influence, rankings, engagement, referrals, dwell time)
- Qualitative: reader feedback, product-placement quality, internal feedback, manager opinion
- 50% review quarterly, usually blended with monthly/weekly/real-time feedback
- Raise decisions mostly reuse the same performance metrics, plus work ethic, annual raise norms, retention intent
- A minority skip reviews entirely (mostly bootstrapped/small-team philosophies)

Five tips:
1. **Try different methods; find what fits** — people and organizations differ; the real question is "how does this review help this person achieve their goals?" Test-drive advice; observe impact; don't copy blindly
2. **Be careful with carrot-and-stick** — pay-for-performance reduces enthusiasm, creativity, cooperation, risk-taking; produces temporary compliance only. Reward great work with raises, yes — but don't install incentive systems to improve output. Instead: training, useful feedback, room for self-determination
3. **Reviews can't fix broken recruitment** — hire intrinsically motivated people and "regular casual chats" suffice; hire wrong and you'll need ever-more-complex assessment systems. Be the manager who removes roadblocks (and steps aside when needed) to earn trust
4. **Let employees self-track** — writers report their own numbers (rankings, visibility, links, traffic growth; tag by author): transparent standards, ownership, less unfair assessment, easier data collection
5. **Don't forget the small things** — clear standards (no "impress me" games), private time/place (late day, enough time, no audience), share the scorecard copy with the employee, prepare notes/agenda in advance, ensure two-way conversation

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Choose a review type per team maturity: MBO for metric-driven teams; continuous feedback for agile; 360 for leadership-heavy roles
- [ ] Blend quarterly reviews with lighter monthly/real-time touchpoints
- [ ] Use both quantitative (traffic, leads) and qualitative (reader feedback, craft quality) evidence
- [ ] Never rank writers against each other for rewards
- [ ] Have each writer self-report their portfolio metrics before the meeting
- [ ] Send the agenda and standards ahead; hold the review privately; end with the employee's voice

## Data points & benchmarks
- 55/15/10/20 split: only-quant / mixed / only-qual / no metrics
- Quarterly cadence is the modal choice (50%)
- Motivation research (older, widely cited): rewards tend to yield temporary compliance, not lasting behavior change

## Mistakes to avoid
- Incentive plans in creative teams — they suppress exactly what content work needs
- Turning reviews into psychological guessing games
- Unprepared, public, or one-way review meetings
- Expecting a review system to compensate for bad hires

## Key principles
- Reviews should help both sides; nobody trusts a manager who only judges
- With the right people, trust + casual regular chats outperform formal scorecards
- Find your own way — no template survives contact with your specific team
