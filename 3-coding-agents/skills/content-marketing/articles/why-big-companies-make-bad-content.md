# Why Big Companies Make "Bad" Content

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
The author argues that big brands publishing off-topic, never-converting content isn't an accident — it's deliberate. Vanity traffic can still build brand awareness and investor confidence, may help site-wide authority, and denies competitors SERP share. For companies at scale, "bad" content is a cheap digital billboard with good attribution. This is an explanation of the pattern, not a recommendation for most sites (see mistakes below).

## Framework / Step-by-step
The four strategic reasons:
1. **More traffic is better than less.** Every URL and targeted keyword is a new doorway into the site. Even non-converting traffic: strengthens domain authority (lifting every other page), builds brand awareness and serendipitous discovery, and denies rivals traffic and share of voice. Multiplied across hundreds/thousands of pages, indirect revenue impact is large. Straying from your niche doesn't necessarily break topical authority — one major publisher's off-topic health subfolder generated ~4M estimated monthly organic pageviews (third-party estimate).
2. **The bigger you get, the cheaper content becomes.** Marginal cost per "unit" of content is low at scale: freelancer networks, established research/briefing/editing/publication processes, and little opportunity cost — after a decade of blogging, most product-relevant topics are exhausted (finite supply of high-traffic, high-relevance topics). Efficient gating, lead capture, scoring, and retargeting squeeze value from tiny conversion rates.
3. **When you IPO, your audience becomes everyone.** Growth requires expanding total addressable market; post-IPO, the audience includes institutional investors, analysts, journalists, and retail investors. They influence company worth via share price, not subscriptions. Serendipitous brand appearances in search results feed this second funnel: they download ebooks, add to subscriber counts reported in filings, and remember the brand when the ticker hits the news.
4. **Your competitors will make you.** SEO is an arms race over a finite keyword set. Leaving thousands of SERPs uncontested lets rivals build a backlink/brand moat that becomes nearly impossible to challenge; the gap compounds at accelerating pace.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Before dismissing big-brand content as incompetent, ask which of the four strategic goals it serves
- [ ] If you've exhausted product-relevant topics, quantify the indirect value (links, authority, awareness) of adjacent high-volume topics
- [ ] For scale plays, drive marginal content cost down (templates, freelancer networks, programmatic processes)
- [ ] Wire up gating, lead scoring, and retargeting to monetize small conversion rates
- [ ] Audit which high-value SERPs competitors own uncontested — claim them before moats form
- [ ] Don't copy this play below scale: relevance-first content still wins for most companies

## Data points & benchmarks
- One legacy publisher's off-niche health subfolder: ~4M estimated monthly organic pageviews (third-party estimate).
- After ~a decade of consistent blogging, companies exhaust the finite supply of high-relevance, high-traffic topics.
- Examples of the pattern: software brands publishing emoji how-tos, crypto stock guides, quote listicles, and slogan roundups — pure top-of-awareness plays.
- The giveaway that it's deliberate: these pieces still get dedicated CTAs, lead capture, and retargeting wiring — the machinery of intent, not neglect.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Judging enterprise content by SMB conversion logic — the value model differs.
- Assuming growth-stage companies "forgot" content best practice.
- Imitating the strategy without the infrastructure (retargeting, scoring, scale economics) that makes it pay.
- Ignoring the competitive denial effect: uncontested SERPs become rival moats.
- Misreading this as a license to publish mass-produced, low-value pages or to host third-party/affiliate content on a strong domain just to borrow its rankings — that risks scaled content abuse and site reputation abuse spam actions.

## Key principles
- "Bad" content = cheap digital billboards with surprisingly good attribution.
- Traffic value shows up indirectly: authority, awareness, denial, and investor sentiment.
- Content strategy must match company stage: relevance-first early, breadth-at-scale late.
- There are two funnels for public companies: customers and capital-market influencers.
- Growth inefficiency is real too — legal review, stakeholder sign-offs, generic brand voice, search/content misalignment — but it explains only part of the pattern.
