# "Taste" in Content Marketing: What It Actually Means and How to Develop It

- **Updated**: 2026-10-04

## TL;DR
When anyone can generate content in seconds, creation stops being valuable — knowing what's worth making becomes the scarce skill. That judgment is taste: not elitism, but applied pattern recognition built from massive consumption and thousands of editorial decisions. It can't be outsourced to AI or stolen by competitors, which makes it the most durable differentiator.

## Framework / Step-by-step
What taste is:
- **Applied judgment** — consistently distinguishing better from worse in your domain via pattern recognition across quality levels
- **A coherent evaluation framework** — known signals of good work (precision, unexpected connections, structural integrity) vs. lazy work (borrowed framing, unearned certainty, generic transitions)
- **Cultural and contextual fluency** — knowing when a metaphor is tired, when a topic is exhausted vs. peaking, what your specific audience is ready for
- **Compressed experience** — the residue of thousands of editorial decisions, good and bad

Where taste shows up daily: topic selection (what you refuse to cover), framing (angles not yet cheapened), structure (design vs. default templates), references (obscure essays and academic papers, not the SERP's usual suspects), tone (recognizable without a byline), editing (deletion decisions), and timing (when to speak, when to wait).

How to develop it:
1. **Consume a lot, especially the good stuff** — across formats; know your industry's history ("if you know history, you can pretty much predict the future"); read outside your vertical (economics, psychology, fiction) for cross-pollination
2. **Examine why things work or don't** — articulate taste in specifics, not "I liked it": why does this work? how would I improve it? what does the "me" version look like?
3. **Put in your reps** — judgment only improves with volume: write 100 essays, make 100 videos; taste is compressed experience, and experience requires doing
4. **Create feedback loops** — publish, then talk to readers; watch what they reference, share, and reply to
5. **Accept that taste alienates as much as it attracts** — if everyone loves your work, your choices aren't strong enough; polarization is a byproduct of genuine perspective, not ragebait

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Keep a swipe file of likes/dislikes with written reasons for each
- [ ] Schedule consumption outside your niche weekly
- [ ] For every piece you admire, write one sentence on why it works
- [ ] Track your rep count (aim for 100 of your core format)
- [ ] Before publishing, check the seven taste decisions: topic, framing, structure, references, tone, cuts, timing
- [ ] Hire for judgment and defensible choices, not just execution

## Data points & benchmarks
- One writer at 154 articles and counting still describes their taste as "developing" — expect years, not months
- AI and competitors can mimic your style, format, and even sentence quirks — but cannot steal the judgment system that produced them
- Distinctive points of view get clicked, shared, and mentioned more; they generate replies and conversations, not just views

## Mistakes to avoid
- Confusing taste with elitism — disliking popular things isn't taste; plenty of popular things are genuinely good
- Passive consumption without articulating why something works
- Intentional polarization or controversial takes you don't believe
- Working in stealth instead of building audience feedback loops

## Key principles
- The internet removed publishing gatekeepers; AI removed creation gatekeepers — judgment about what should exist is the new moat
- Most brands will use AI to chase efficiency and produce rivers of acceptable content nobody remembers; the winners slow down, develop a point of view, and publish less but matter more
- Taste survives platform and algorithm changes because it isn't built on hacks
- Your reference library is a map of your mind
