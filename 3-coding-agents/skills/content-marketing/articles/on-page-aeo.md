# On-Page AEO: 4 Writing Frameworks for Better AI Visibility

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Human-friendly content is AI-friendly content: both people and language models skim, retrieve snippets, and lean on heuristics. Four writing frameworks — drawn from military communication, consulting, and journalism — plausibly improve your odds of being understood, retrieved, and cited by AI assistants (the supporting data is correlational, from individual studies).

## Framework / Step-by-step
1. **BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front)** — state the conclusion, recommendation, or key finding in the first one or two sentences of every section, then support it. Apply at every level: introduction states the key argument; the first sentence of each H2 delivers the section's point; titles directly answer the target question. Test: read only the first sentence of each section — if the entire argument is comprehensible from those alone, BLUF is working.
2. **Declarative statements** — write definitive assertions that can stand alone as answers ("X is defined as", "X refers to", "X means"). Avoid hedged constructions ("might", "could potentially", "it seems like"). Use definitive language for definitions, established facts, core concepts, and recommendations; keep qualified language for emerging research, contested claims, and predictions.
3. **Specificity (entity density)** — maximize the ratio of named entities (brands, people, places, specific concepts, metrics) to total words. Rule: name the tool, cite the source, specify the metric. Every time you write something vague ("use a tool", "according to research"), replace it with a concrete reference.
4. **Strategic repetition** — place your most important idea at multiple points, rephrased for each context: stated in the introduction, echoed mid-article as a contextual reminder, and reinforced in the conclusion (without padding or keyword stuffing). Each rephrasing matches slightly different query formulations, creating multiple extraction opportunities.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Rewrite introductions so the key takeaway is in sentence one or two
- [ ] Convert hedged sentences into declarative ones for all definitions and core claims
- [ ] Audit a draft for vague phrases; replace each with a named entity or metric
- [ ] Ensure each key insight appears in the intro, body, and conclusion, rephrased naturally
- [ ] Confirm the page actually ranks first — writing quality only decides citation among ranking pages
- [ ] Track which of your URLs AI platforms cite (any AI-visibility tracker) and correlate with these frameworks

## Data points & benchmarks
- In one study, 44.2% of AI citations came from the first 30% of the content
- In the same research, citation winners were almost 2x more likely to contain definitive language: 36.2% vs 20.2%
- Heavily cited text had ~20.6% entity density vs 5–8% in standard prose — 3–4x the normal rate (one study)
- In a study of 1.4M assistant prompts, 88% of cited URLs came from the general search index — ranking is the prerequisite
- Only about half of retrieved URLs get cited; the writing-quality gap determines which half

## Mistakes to avoid
- Burying the lede: building up to the finding instead of leading with it
- Academic hedging on definitions and established facts — it signals uncertainty to models
- Vague, jargon-filled filler that could describe any topic
- Stating your key insight only once — a model retrieving a different snippet never sees it

## Key principles
- Neither humans nor AI read pages linearly; both skim and retrieve
- AI models retrieve snippets, not pages — write sections that survive extraction
- These are not magic fixes: content must rank first, then writing decides citation vs ignored
- The frameworks predate LLMs by decades because the underlying job (fast extraction of meaning) is the same
