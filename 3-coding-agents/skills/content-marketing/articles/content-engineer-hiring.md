# Why Not to Hire a "Content Engineer" (And What to Hire Instead)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
The "content engineer" — an AI-native role hired to systematize and scale content production — optimizes for the wrong future. Scaled content is the most accessible it has ever been and the least effective it has ever been. Hire marketing experts who can learn AI, not AI experts who might learn marketing.

## Framework / Step-by-step
Three arguments against the role:
1. **Experimentation, not scale.** The content engineer's remit is automating what works to the Nth degree — but the golden era of scaled content is over. One large SEO blog's scaled-content case: 2,161 articles across six languages, programmatic strategies peaking at ~34,000 auto-generated pages and millions of pageviews — and today a fraction of peak search clicks, with its biggest programmatic strategy moved off the main domain. Indexing is harder, Google's "scaled content abuse" policy (2024) is enforced actively, competitors can clone programmatic plays wholesale, and AI answers have broken the unit economics of volume publishing. Anything fully automatable is destined to become worthless — if you can publish 1,000 programmatic pages a week, so can every competitor, until nobody profits.
2. **Quality, not quality assurance.** AI raised the floor of content (passable output for dollars and minutes) but not the ceiling (nobody is producing remarkable new forms with it). Most AI adoption is cost-motivated: stretching the same budget, not unlocking new customer acquisition. A content engineer is a QA role — consistency, uniformity, brand safety — not a quality role. Content needs weirdness, irregularity, and real individual voices, not more consistency.
3. **Marketing experts, not AI experts.** The "bridge between marketing and technical AI implementation" is dissolving: models get easier to use daily (single-prompt image generation, deep research compressing dozens of exchanges, plain-language API access, automatic model selection). Like prompt engineers, content engineers are a stopgap role for problems the tools themselves are solving. AI fluency is becoming table stakes for everyone, like internet fluency — not a dedicated hire.

Where growth actually is (headwinds vs. tailwinds): headwinds for programmatic and top-of-funnel content; tailwinds for original research and data content, platform-native thought leadership, and short/long-form video.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Audit how much team energy goes to scaling vs. experimenting with new formats
- [ ] Shift budget from volume publishing toward original research, data content, and video
- [ ] Write job descriptions around marketing/writing skill first; treat AI fluency as trainable
- [ ] Ask candidates for evidence of judgment (angles, taste, experiments), not tool stacks
- [ ] Test: could this content strategy be copied wholesale by a competitor in a week? If yes, de-prioritize it

## Data points & benchmarks
- ~34,000 auto-generated pages at peak, millions of pageviews — now a fraction of peak clicks; strategy relocated to a separate domain
- Useful half-life of "scaling" content with AI: months, and shrinking (the author's estimate, not a measured figure)
- Most companies' AI use is cost-motivated; monthly content spend stays flat — AI stretches budget rather than creating new acquisition channels

## Mistakes to avoid
- Basing hiring on AI skills you won't need for long, at the expense of marketing skills you will
- Building a team of automation specialists scaling unremarkable strategies
- Missing emerging opportunities (research, video, platform-native content) while polishing dying ones
- Confusing consistency (QA) with quality (remarkable work)

## Key principles
- The ability to automate something and the diminishing of its returns are fundamentally linked
- Great marketers can learn AI; AI experts rarely become great marketers
- Between a great writer with no AI knowledge and a middling writer fluent in automation, take the great writer every time
