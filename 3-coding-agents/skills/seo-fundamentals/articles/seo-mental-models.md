# 6 Mental Models for SEO Decisions

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
Mental models shape every SEO decision, usually unconsciously. Three models improve judgment under uncertainty — first principles, the Pareto principle, and Red Queen theory. Three others quietly damage strategies — local maxima, sunk cost fallacy, and confirmation bias. Spot them deliberately.

## Framework / Step-by-step

### Models to use
1. **First principles thinking** — break problems down to what is almost certainly true, then reason up. In an industry built on speculation, anchor to basics: blogging exists to reach qualified people and influence sales (so high-intent low-volume beats irrelevant high-volume); search content distributes via search (so don't force social promotion on it); search engines aim to surface the most relevant, highest-quality content (so gaming the system eventually ends badly).
2. **Pareto principle (80/20)** — a small share of causes produces most effects. Prioritize ruthlessly: pitch only proven link-earning content for links; recover traffic by focusing on pages that lost the most. Treat 80/20 as a heuristic, not literal numbers.
3. **Red Queen theory** — constant effort is needed just to maintain position, because competitors and the search engine itself keep evolving. SEO is an always-on strategy with no finish line; even top pages get pushed down and must be re-optimized.

### Models to watch out for
4. **Local maxima** — immense effort for marginal improvement means you may be optimizing inside a small hill. Ask "what can I do differently?" — e.g., one large SEO blog's slow-growth content program jumped to a new level only after launching programmatic pages, something years of ordinary optimization couldn't do.
5. **Sunk cost fallacy** — continuing a project because of past investment, despite evidence it won't pay off. Cutting losses beats blind persistence; you can return later with fresh thinking. (That same blog never ranked for the head term "seo" despite years of attempts — and growth didn't suffer.)
6. **Confirmation bias** — overweighting evidence that supports existing beliefs. Watch for it in: attributing rankings to a favored factor, keyword selection driven by audience assumptions, strategies "validated" by a single agreeing article, audits that prioritize personally affirming findings, and overconfidence in familiar tactics.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] When a strategy rests on speculation, restate it from first principles
- [ ] Rank work by expected impact; ignore the long tail of low-impact tasks
- [ ] Treat SEO as continuous maintenance, not a one-time project
- [ ] If effort-to-gain ratio collapses, test a different approach instead of pushing harder
- [ ] Kill or pause projects that only survive on sunk-cost reasoning
- [ ] In audits and strategy reviews, actively look for disconfirming evidence

## Data points & benchmarks
- On one large SEO site, ~80% of traffic comes from no more than 6% of pages
- The top 20% of pages there contribute 96.8% of total traffic — concentration is even stronger than 80/20
- One programmatic SEO launch delivered growth that would have taken years of incremental optimization

## Mistakes to avoid
- Taking 80/20 literally instead of as an approximation
- "Hustle culture" pressure making sunk-cost decisions feel like persistence
- Craving theories and speculation instead of returning to verifiable basics

## Key principles
- "It takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place."
- Loss aversion is a defense mechanism for past decisions — not a strategy.
- The best problem-solving starts from what is demonstrably true, not what is conventionally believed.
