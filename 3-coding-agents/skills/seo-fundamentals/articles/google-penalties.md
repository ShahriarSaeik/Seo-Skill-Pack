# Google Penalties: The Newbie-Friendly Guide

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
A Google penalty (manual action) is a human-imposed punishment for violating quality guidelines — distinct from algorithmic demotions. Today, almost all penalties come from deliberate manipulation: unnatural links (to or from your site) and thin content. Recovery = fix the root cause everywhere, document your effort, then request reconsideration.

## Framework / Step-by-step

### Identifying which hit you took
1. **Manual action** — always visible in your Search Console under "Manual actions" with a stated reason and affected scope (whole site, section, or URL).
2. **Algorithmic action** — no notification. Diagnose by matching traffic drops to known update dates; check industry forums to see if peers were hit simultaneously.
3. Important: being "hit" by a core update isn't a penalty — someone may simply be doing it better. Core updates promote good sites as well as demote weak ones.

### Common manual action types
- Unnatural links to your site / from your site (most common, nearly always deserved)
- Thin content with little or no added value
- Pure spam, cloaking, sneaky redirects, hidden text/keyword stuffing
- Scaled content abuse, site reputation abuse, expired domain abuse (spam policies updated in 2024)
- Structured data issues, UGC spam, third-party spam abuse
- News/Discover violations (separate products; don't affect web rankings)

### Auditing a link profile (for unnatural-link actions)
- **Anchor text check** — natural profiles are dominated by URL and brand anchors. Heavy keyword-stuffed anchors from many domains = manipulation signature.
- **Referring domain check** — link-selling sites link out to disproportionate numbers of sites relative to their size and have little/no organic traffic. Red flag ratio example: a healthy blog had ~2K indexed pages linking to ~1.5K sites (~1:1); a 500-page site linking to 5K sites is a flag.
- **Visual inspection** — made-for-link pages have nonsensical content and messy images.

### Recovery process
1. Remove links under your control.
2. Send removal requests to webmasters (multiple attempts, multiple contacts; document everything).
3. Disavow links you couldn't remove — but disavowing alone without removal attempts can get your reconsideration rejected.
4. For thin content: remove or improve, and show evidence of what changed.
5. Fix ALL affected pages — fixing only some usually gets the request rejected.
6. Submit reconsideration request with your documentation; a human reviews it. Reviews take weeks to months (observed range: 3 weeks to 5 months). No limit on resubmissions.
7. Algorithmic actions: no reconsideration exists — fix issues and wait for recrawl/reindex.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Check manual actions report before deep-diving any traffic drop
- [ ] If manual: read the stated reason, audit anchors + referring domains
- [ ] Remove → request removal → disavow (in that order); log every step
- [ ] Remediate every affected page before requesting review
- [ ] If algorithmic: identify the update's target, improve, wait for recrawl
- [ ] Monitor for hacking/UGC spam (common innocent-source penalties)

## Data points & benchmarks
- Older figures (circa 2020): Google detected ~40 billion spammy pages daily; automated systems blocked ~99% of spam — manual actions cover the small remainder.
- 2.9 million manual-action-related messages sent to site owners in 2020.
- An older Google estimate: ~25–30% of the web is duplicate content, but there is NO duplicate-content penalty (Google ranks the original and canonicalizes).
- Reconsideration response times: typically weeks; observed extremes of 3 weeks to 5 months.
- Manual actions can expire on their own; algorithmic actions don't.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Disavowing every low-quality link preemptively — if you never bought links, ignore the cruft; only disavow actively placed unnatural links.
- Assuming negative SEO will sink you — Google ignores most attack links; sites hit after attacks almost always had their own link building exposed.
- Expecting traffic to return to penalty-era peaks after recovery — the unnatural links were contributing (that's why they were spam); post-recovery baseline is your "clean" level.
- Believing a lifted penalty taints the domain — no grudge is held; there's no reset button either, so history must be cleaned up or outlived.

## Key principles
- "Almost all Google penalties now are given because the site owner was trying too hard to manipulate Google" (a well-known penalty-recovery consultant).
- Core update losses are relative, not punitive — improve rather than appeal.
- Recovery rewards demonstrated effort, not technical loopholes — humans read your reconsideration request.
