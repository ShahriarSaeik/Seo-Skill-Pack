# UX and SEO: Winning the Searcher, Not Just the SERP

- **Updated**: 2026-10-05

## TL;DR
User interaction signals (clicks, scrolls, quick-backs) are not direct ranking factors, but they train the learning-to-rank models that shape future results — documents and testimony from an antitrust trial described user interactions as one of three core pillars of search. Build sites where information architecture, navigation, layouts, internal links, and performance serve both humans and crawlers.

## Framework / Step-by-step
1. **Map information architecture** (SEO + UX jointly): start from keyword research and topic hubs; keep intuitive main navigation and clear labels; keywords in URLs (rule of thumb: max ~3 levels deep); every important page reachable within ~3 clicks of the homepage; plan pages visually with per-page cards holding target keywords, titles, internal links, URL, and wireframes; tag each page by purpose (organic traffic, brand, lead nurture, support).
2. **Build intuitive navigation**: ≤7 main-menu items (cognitive load limit); use dropdowns/mega-menus for depth; footer for mid-tier SEO pages, legal, and credibility; breadcrumbs for hierarchy and orientation; clarity beats creativity — themed/vague labels confuse users and crawlers.
3. **Design balanced page layouts**: avoid designer minimalism (sub-100-word pages give engines nothing) and SEO walls of text. Apply UX principles: aesthetic-usability effect, cognitive load (50–100 word sections under headings), principle of least effort, "could my mom / a drunk user use this?" test. Wireframe before design; strong pages have a keyword-led hero, clear CTAs, internal-link areas, structured content blocks, and supporting visuals.
4. **Use internal links strategically**: relevance first; descriptive, varied anchor text (never "click here", never forced exact-match); link across site sections when it helps the reader.
5. **Optimize performance**: crawl for slow loads, broken links, and Core Web Vitals (LCP, INP, CLS); layer behavioral analytics — rage clicks, dead clicks, and quick-backs flag where rankings exist but the page fails the searcher.

## Actionable checklist
- [ ] Rebuild the site outline from keyword/topic data, not designer intuition
- [ ] Trim main navigation to ~7 items; move secondary pages to footer/dropdowns
- [ ] Add breadcrumbs on deep pages
- [ ] Rewrite clever labels into plain, keyword-aligned ones
- [ ] Wireframe money pages with hero + CTA + link blocks before visual design
- [ ] Audit anchors: replace vague ones, vary exact-match ones
- [ ] Fix CWV failures and watch rage/dead clicks and quick-backs in session analytics

## Data points & benchmarks
- Search's three ranking pillars (per trial documents): document body, anchors around the web, and user interactions.
- Sections of 50–100 words keep cognitive load manageable.
- Heuristics, not rules: main menu of ~7 horizontal items; URL depth of ~3 levels; important pages within ~3 clicks of the homepage.
- Quick-backs (immediate return to results) = the clearest "ranks but doesn't satisfy" signal.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Designers: minimal no-text pages, trendy experimental navigation, stripping optimized headings/links during redesigns, treating headings as styling, wrong or cannibalized keyword selection, intent misalignment.
- SEOs: walls of text, cluttered layouts with too many CTAs, snippet-page message mismatch, ignoring brand/tone, interrupting flow with pop-ups, "telling and selling" instead of guiding.

## Key principles
- UX signals shift rankings over time through machine-learned models, not real-time tweaks — chronic dissatisfaction compounds into lost visibility.
- UX and SEO share one goal: help people find and use information effectively.
- Either discipline dominating the build produces sites that either look good and can't rank, or rank and repel visitors.
